## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword spotting (KWS) is tasked with detecting user-specified wake-up words within a continuous audio stream. It serves as the primary trigger for interactive systems such as smart speakers, mobile voice assistants, and in-car voice interfaces; subsequent recognition and dialogue chains are activated only upon keyword detection. In traditional KWS, the wake-up words are fixed in the model parameters at the factory, and users cannot change them: switching words requires re-collecting data, re-training, or fine-tuning, which entails long R&D cycles and high costs, making it practically infeasible for products. Open-vocabulary (user-defined) keyword spotting extracts the "keyword" from model parameters, turning it into an entity that can be registered at runtime—users complete registration by speaking a sentence or typing a line of text, after which the system performs a binary "match or not" judgment on all audio. This paradigm transforms keyword spotting from a fixed-class classification problem into a registration-verification problem, structurally resembling speaker verification: the registration side provides keyword templates, and the verification side scores query audio. The paper emphasizes at the outset that this route allows "custom keywords without re-training or fine-tuning," which is the premise for all discussions.

The paper targets two most critical pain points under this paradigm.

The first pain point is the high false alarm rate caused by confusable words. On always-on listening devices, the experience cost of false wake-ups is extremely high—when the device lights up and responds without the user speaking, each occurrence consumes trust. The biggest source of false alarms is not random noise, but words that are highly similar in pronunciation to the registered word, differing only in a few phonemes. The hard negatives presented in the paper's Fig. 2 are highly representative: the registered word is "information," and the query audio is "induration." The two are very similar in overall contour, with differences concentrated in individual phonemes. For such samples, sentence-level feature representations dilute local differences through temporal pooling, causing the decision maker to see almost overlapping embeddings, making false alarms inevitable. The criticism of existing methods in the abstract is that "high false alarm rates caused by confusable words are prevalent in open-vocabulary scenarios."

The second pain point is the fragmentation of registration modalities. In actual products, registration methods have various constraints: audio registration (users repeat a few times) is sensitive to recording environments and user pronunciation quality, leading to poor registration quality in noisy environments; text registration (users type) bypasses audio quality issues but requires the system to reliably convert text to pronunciation. Different users and scenarios suit different registration methods, and an ideal system should allow users to freely choose or even combine both modalities. However, most existing systems support only one of them, modalities cannot complement each other, user convenience is limited, and better performance cannot be achieved through modality fusion (these are two shortcomings explicitly listed in the paper's abstract).

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

Organized by registration modality, the shortcomings identified by the paper focus on four aspects.

First, audio registration methods (query-by-example) use dynamic time warping or metric learning to measure the similarity between registered audio and query audio. The judgment granularity of this route is acoustic frames or sentence-level embeddings. Dynamic programming-based similarity lacks resolution on hard negatives where "the overall contour is similar but local phonemes differ," and performance is constrained by the quality of registered audio.

Second, sentence-level contrastive learning methods in text registration (CMCD [20], CLAD [23]) only apply contrastive constraints at the sentence level. The paper explicitly points out that such methods "ignore finer-grained phoneme-level" information. The consequence is directly reflected in Table I: CLAD achieves an AUC of only 76.15% and an EER as high as 30.30% on the hard negative set LPH, while the EER is only 8.65% on the easy negative set LPE—the huge gap between the two sets indicates that its failure mode is precisely concentrated on the confusable words the paper aims to tackle; sentence-level granularity is almost powerless against confusable words.

Third, existing work has begun to seek robustness through word-level and phoneme-level fine-grained modeling: Nishu et al. trained an audio encoder using connectionist temporal classification loss and built a phoneme-to-vector database (CED [8]); Lee et al. used phoneme-level detection loss to handle variable pronunciations (iPhonMatchNet [11]). However, the paper argues that the phoneme alignment in these methods "lacks sufficient precision to accurately map phoneme-level audio to text"—the alignment boundaries learned implicitly are uncontrollable and uncheckable, and phoneme-level supervision is actually noisy, leading to errors on hard cases.

Fourth, single modality support. The baseline systems in Table I either only support text registration or only support text plus audio registration; no method supports all three registration modes (text, audio, text plus audio) within a unified framework. Although MM-KWS [24] is multi-modal, it trades performance for generating 1.5 million pairs of augmented training data, which has high data manufacturing costs. The paper's abstract states this point as: most existing methods are "limited to audio-only or text-only registration, ignoring user convenience and modality fusion that can bring better performance."

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

In summary, this paper aims to answer three questions simultaneously. First, how to make phoneme-level alignment accurate—shifting the granularity of contrastive learning from sentence to phoneme, and ensuring alignment boundaries are explicitly controllable and reproducible, rather than letting the model guess implicitly. Second, how to systematically generate and utilize hard negatives—confusable words are long-tailed in natural data, and hard negatives collected naturally are insufficient to support the learning of decision boundaries; active construction is needed, along with a supporting supervision organization method. Third, how to unify three registration modalities in the same model—sharing representations and training objectives, flexibly switching at inference time based on the modality actually provided by the user, masking missing modalities, and achieving better results than any single modality during fusion. These three correspond to the phoneme-level contrastive learning, memory bank plus third-class discriminator, and multi-modal flexible registration verifier modules in the following sections, respectively.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall architecture is shown in Fig. 1, consisting of four parts: audio-text phoneme-level contrastive learning path, audio-audio phoneme-level contrastive learning path, a modality-branching verifier, and a context-independent phoneme memory bank. The base consists of two frozen pre-trained encoders: the audio side uses a pre-trained Whisper-Tiny encoder, and the text side uses a pre-trained grapheme-to-phoneme conversion model (G2P, implemented with g2pE); features from both modalities are mapped to the same 128-dimensional embedding space via a projection layer (a concatenation of LayerNorm, fully connected layer, and non-linear activation). The figure explicitly marks frozen modules (Frozen Modules); training only updates the projection layers, verifier, and memory bank.

The motivation for this design can be broken down into three layers. First, leveraging pre-trained representations. Academic datasets are limited in scale, and training an audio encoder from scratch is unrealistic; freezing Whisper-Tiny means training only needs to learn "the mapping between modalities," not "what speech is," saving data and simplifying the problem into a pure alignment problem. Second, a single phoneme memory bank serves dual purposes: it acts as a supplement to the similarity matrix in the text registration branch during forward propagation, and as a raw material library for data augmentation. One bank, two uses, making the design economical. Third, the verifier branches by registration modality but does not separate by modality: during training, losses from three modalities are jointly optimized (Eq. 8); during inference, the corresponding branch is activated based on the user's registration method, and missing modalities are masked. One model, three usages, avoiding the maintenance of multiple independent models. The right side of Fig. 1 draws three verification branches outputting binary decisions for audio registration, text registration, and audio-plus-text registration, which is the direct illustration of this design.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

During the training phase, query audio features and text features are obtained by their respective encoders plus projections, denoted as $E^q \in \mathbb{R}^{T^q \times d}$ and $E^t \in \mathbb{R}^{T^t \times d}$, where $d$ is the frame dimension (128), and $T^q$, $T^t$ are the number of audio frames and text length, respectively. Since the number of audio frames is much larger than the number of phonemes, the dimensions on both sides do not match, preventing direct comparison. The paper uses the Montreal Forced Aligner to provide precise audio frame boundaries for each phoneme, then performs global averaging on the frames belonging to each phoneme, compressing the audio into a phoneme-level sequence $\hat{E}^q \in \mathbb{R}^{T^t \times d}$ of the same length as the text. This step is the foundation of the entire method: alignment is explicitly provided by an external tool, and boundaries are checkable and reproducible.

The contrastive loss adopts the InfoNCE form (Eq. 1):

$$L_{cl}^{at} = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(\hat{E}_i^q, E_i^t)/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(\text{sim}(\hat{E}_i^q, E_j^t)/\tau)}$$

where $\text{sim}(\cdot)$ is cosine similarity, and $\tau$ is the temperature parameter. The key lies in the organization of negatives: all phonemes of all words in a mini-batch are concatenated into $N$ phoneme samples, with paired audio-text phonemes as positives, and all other phonemes in the batch as negatives (including other phonemes of the positive sample itself). That is, the phoneme /B/ in "Because" will use /B/, /P/, /D/, etc., appearing in other words in the same batch as negatives—the supervision signal to distinguish /S/ from /SH/ is repeatedly tempered in this pool. The loss for the audio-audio path (Eq. 2) is of the same form as Eq. 1, except that the text phoneme embeddings are replaced by registered audio phoneme embeddings $\hat{E}^a$ obtained through the same forced alignment and frame averaging; the phoneme sequence lengths on both the query and registration sides are normalized to be equal to the text length $T^t$ (for the same word, the number of phonemes is the same), so phoneme positions correspond one-to-one, and the organization of positives and negatives is exactly the same as the audio-text path.

The update of the phoneme memory bank is momentum sliding average (Eq. 3): $p_k \leftarrow \alpha p_k + (1-\alpha) p_k^{new}$, with momentum coefficient $\alpha$ set to 0.8, and selective averaging is performed on high-quality phoneme data to improve the overall representation quality of the bank. This update idea, derived from momentum contrast and self-distillation, aims to allow the phoneme prototypes in the bank to evolve smoothly without following the jitter of single batches of data.

Training configuration (Section III.B of the paper): Trained for 50 epochs on four V100 GPUs, SGD optimizer, initial learning rate 0.01, batch size 64. The learning rate scheduling strategy is not reported in the paper.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Phoneme-Level Contrastive Learning

The core judgment of phoneme-level contrastive learning is: the discriminative information for confusable words is not uniformly distributed acoustically, but concentrated in a few specific phoneme positions. The visualization in Fig. 3 corroborates this—embeddings of the same phoneme form tight clusters, while different phonemes are separated. Pairs like "the prince's" and "the princes" (almost homophonic), and pairs differing by only one phoneme where the phoneme itself is similar (e.g., /S/ and /SH/), are precisely the hardest samples to separate in the embedding space. Sentence-level loss is naturally insensitive to such local differences: after one temporal pooling, a difference in one phoneme changes at most a small segment of the sequence, the movement of the sentence-level embedding is small, and the decision boundary has no foothold. Shifting the contrastive granularity down to the phoneme is equivalent to placing the classification boundary directly at the source of confusion, letting each phoneme independently bear the discriminative responsibility—this is the weight of the word "phoneme-level" in the paper's title.

There are two notable choices in implementation. First, using forced alignment to explicitly obtain boundaries, rather than implicit alignment via temporal classification loss as in CED: implicit alignment is a byproduct of model learning, and boundary precision is uncontrollable, whereas forced alignment is a mature external tool, and frame boundaries are directly reproducible—the paper's criticism of existing phoneme-level methods for "insufficient alignment precision" is aimed precisely here. Second, forced alignment is only used during the training phase: during inference, the similarity matrix for the text registration branch is automatically aligned by self-attention, and users registering a new word do not need to run the aligner, constituting no deployment dependency.

The audio-audio path answers the symmetry problem of the audio registration modality: there is also a need for phoneme-level consistency constraints between query audio and registered audio. The two paths share the same phoneme coordinate system (both undergo forced alignment and frame averaging), ensuring that audio registration also benefits from fine-grained alignment, rather than only the text registration path enjoying the dividend.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Context-Independent Phoneme Memory Bank and Third-Class Discriminator

The paper's judgment is "the primary factor affecting model performance is the existence of hard negatives." Since hard negatives are long-tailed in natural data, the paper uses a memory bank plus editing operations to manufacture them in batches: randomly sampling phonemes from an independently maintained phoneme memory bank, and performing insertion, substitution, and deletion operations on the positive phoneme sequences to synthesize hard negatives that are highly similar in pronunciation but differ in specific phonemes. This is equivalent to directional perturbation in the phoneme space, with the perturbation direction pointing directly to the positions where the model is most likely to confuse—much more efficient than random noise augmentation, because every perturbation falls where the decision boundary needs reinforcement the most.

The third-class discriminator is the accompanying supervision reorganization: inspired by visual wake word work [35], the original hard negatives mined from the training set and the synthetic hard negatives generated by the memory bank are treated as a third class, and auxiliary training is performed using a three-class cross-entropy loss (i.e., $L_{u3}^{at}$ in Eq. 5 below). Why three classes instead of a general two classes? Because the decision difficulty of ordinary negatives and hard negatives is different: mixed in the same "negative" class, the gradient signal of hard negatives would be drowned out by the massive number of ordinary negatives, and the model tends to treat hard negatives as annotation noise; when separated into its own class, the decision maker is explicitly required to learn an independent boundary for hard negatives. Additionally, the paper performs hard example mining within the training set: finding words that are not recognized accurately based on training results, and re-pairing them with existing words in the training set to further supplement real (rather than synthetic) hard negatives.

The "context-independent" form of the memory bank is a double-edged sword: phoneme prototypes stored out of context allow unrestricted sampling and concatenation, enabling free combination for augmentation; but phonemes in real speech are strongly affected by coarticulation, and the acoustic realization of the same phoneme varies greatly in different contexts—this contradiction is left to the limitations of the method (see the Limitations section).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Unified Verifier for Multi-Modal Flexible Registration

The verifier has three branches divided by registration modality, all participating during training, activated on demand during inference, and masking missing modalities.

Text registration branch: The cosine similarity matrix $M^{at} \in \mathbb{R}^{T^t \times T^q}$ is calculated between the phoneme-level embeddings of the query audio and the registered text. Context-independent phonemes sampled from the memory bank are added to this matrix, followed by self-attention aggregation (Eq. 4: using $M^{at}$ as query, key, and value simultaneously), and finally, a gating recurrent unit and fully connected layer output the sentence-level posterior score. The motivation for adding sampled phonemes to the similarity matrix is to let the verifier have seen "phonemes appearing out of nowhere" during training, making it more robust to insertion-type hard negatives and ambiguous pronunciations. The total loss for this branch (Eq. 5) is $L^{at} = L_u^{at} + L_{u3}^{at} + L_{cl}^{at}$, i.e., the sum of sentence-level binary cross-entropy, three-class augmented cross-entropy, and phoneme-level contrastive loss—the sentence-level loss manages the decision, the phoneme-level loss manages the representation, and the three-class loss manages hard negatives, with clear division of labor.

Audio registration branch: The similarity matrix $M^{aa} \in \mathbb{R}^{T^a \times T^q}$ is calculated between the embeddings of the query and registered audio. The maximum value is taken along the query audio dimension to obtain $M^{qa} \in \mathbb{R}^{T^a}$ as the query vector, and $M^{aa}$ is used as key and value for cross-attention (Eq. 6). The intuitive meaning of taking the maximum value along the query dimension is: each registered phoneme only cares about the segment of frames in the query audio that is most similar to it, simplifying the matching problem from "sequence-to-sequence" to "retrieval anchored by registered phonemes." The loss for this branch (Eq. 7) is $L^{aa} = L_u^{aa} + L_{cl}^{aa}$; the sentence-level loss uses focal loss instead of binary cross-entropy—the standard use of focal loss is hard sample weighting and class imbalance suppression; the paper does not detail the motivation for this choice here, but it is speculated to be related to controlling the weight of hard negatives.

Text-plus-audio registration branch: The features from the two paths are concatenated and passed through a fully connected layer to output the fused decision, with the sentence-level loss being binary cross-entropy ($L_u^{ata}$). The total loss for the entire system (Eq. 8) is $L = L^{at} + L^{aa} + L_u^{ata}$, jointly optimizing the three modalities. This design ensures that "flexible registration" is not a patchwork of three independent models, but three read-out heads on the same representation space.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to sentence-level contrastive learning (CLAD [23]), the difference lies in granularity: under the same contrastive learning framework, this method shifts the constraint from the sentence level to the phoneme level. On Table I, the AUC on LPH improves from 76.15% to 95.56% (both pure text registration with augmentation, an increase of 19.41 percentage points), and the ablation in Table II shows that removing the phoneme-level constraint causes the AUC to drop by 5.74 percentage points—the shift in granularity is the largest single source of gain. Compared to existing phoneme-level methods like CED [8] and iPhonMatchNet [11], the difference lies in the acquisition method of alignment: explicit forced alignment replaces implicit alignment, pushing CED's 92.70% (Table I) to 95.56%. Compared to AdaKWS [37], AdaKWS relies on adaptive instance normalization for conditional modeling; this paper does not use this mechanism, achieving 95.56% AUC for pure text registration versus 95.09% for AdaKWS. The comparison with MM-KWS [24] best reveals the methodological orientation: MM-KWS generated 1.5 million pairs of augmented training data, while this paper trains on the original dataset and achieves higher LPH AUC (96.59% vs. 96.25%) and lower EER (8.47% vs. 9.30%) relying on phoneme-level alignment constraints—constraint design is more efficient than stacking data. Finally, this paper is the only system in Table I that reports results for all three registration modalities (pure text, pure audio, text plus audio); flexibility itself is a differentiator.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The experiment uses the LibriPhrase dataset [20], with phrases extracted from the train-clean-100 and train-clean-360 subsets of the LibriSpeech corpus, ranging from one to four words in length. The evaluation set is constructed from the train-others-500 subset, with negatives divided into easy and hard groups based on edit distance (Levenshtein distance [39]), i.e., the LPE and LPH evaluation sets. Anchor texts are used for text registration evaluation, and anchor audio is used for audio registration evaluation. Specific numbers of phrases, positive-to-negative ratios, and speaker scale: not reported in the paper (the scale definition of LibriPhrase needs to be traced back to the original paper [20]).

There are two notable points in this construction. First, the training uses clean subsets, while the evaluation uses other subsets; the recording quality and speaker composition on the evaluation side differ from the training side, testing generalization rather than memorization. Second, dividing the evaluation sets into easy and hard based on edit distance isolates the "confusable word problem" into a separate track, LPH—all core conclusions of the paper must be verified on LPH, while LPE is closer to the conventional performance baseline.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Two metrics: AUC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) and EER (Equal Error Rate, i.e., the error rate when the false alarm rate equals the miss rate). The reason for choosing these two metrics is that they are threshold-independent: the working threshold for registration-based keyword spotting deployment varies with devices and scenarios; threshold-independent metrics allow for fair cross-system comparison; the equal error rate provides a single-number summary of the working point. Reporting both LPE and LPH sets is also a deliberate design: on LPE, most methods are already close to saturation (AUC above 99%), and the real differentiator, and the one corresponding to product pain points, is LPH—looking at both sets together allows one to immediately distinguish which improvements are universal and which are specifically targeted at confusable words.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table I provides a panorama (asterisks indicate that the system uses data augmentation, and the registration mode column indicates the modalities supported by each baseline).

In the pure text registration track, methods have improved over time: CMCD 73.58%, CLAD 76.15%, iPhonMatchNet 88.23%, CED 92.70%, AdaKWS-Tiny 93.75%, AdaKWS-Small 95.09% (all AUC on LPH), and this method reaches 95.56%, 0.47 percentage points higher than the previous best pure text system AdaKWS-Small, with EER dropping from 11.48% to 9.96% (a drop of 1.52 percentage points); on LPE, AUC is 99.91% and EER is 1.21%, matching AdaKWS-Small's 1.21%. Notably, the advantage of this method over CLAD isa step change: AUC is 19.41 percentage points higher, and EER is 20.34 percentage points lower—under the same contrastive learning framework, the benefit of shifting granularity to the phoneme level far exceeds other engineering improvements, which empirically validates the paper's criticism of sentence-level contrastive learning for "ignoring phoneme-level" information.

In the multi-modal track, this method (text plus audio registration, with augmentation) achieves LPH AUC 96.59%, EER 8.47%, and LPE AUC 99.97%, EER 0.57%, comprehensively surpassing MM-KWS's 96.25%, 9.30%, 99.95%, 0.68%. The paper specifically notes that this is the result after model fusion; before fusion, the AUC on LPH was already 96.41% and EER 8.85%, which itself surpassed the best system at the time; fusion contributed an additional 0.18 percentage points AUC and 0.38 percentage points EER reduction.

For the audio registration modality, this method achieves 93.09%, 13.94% (LPH). It must be pointed out that there are no other audio registration baselines in Table I for comparison; the horizontal position of audio registration cannot be confirmed from the main table, and its value can only be evaluated in the ablation studies within the paper (Table III).

Table I and Table II together reveal a comparison of modality and augmentation: pure text without augmentation 92.45% (Table II), with augmentation to 95.56% (increase of 3.11); without augmentation but with audio modality to 95.06% (increase of 2.61); with both to 96.59%. That is, the hard negative augmentation on the text registration branch (3.11) yields greater benefits than simply stacking the audio modality (2.61), and the two can largely be stacked—this provides a valuable ranking for the product side: do hard negative augmentation first, then add multi-modality.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

Text registration ablation (Table II, AUC on LPH): Complete system with augmentation 95.56%; removing augmentation 92.45%; removing memory bank 92.36%; removing phoneme-level contrastive learning 86.71%. Corresponding EERs are 9.96%, 14.08%, 14.29%, 21.05%. Three findings:

First, phoneme-level contrastive learning is the largest single factor. Removing it causes AUC to drop from 92.45% to 86.71% (drop of 5.74), and EER to worsen from 14.08% to 21.05% (worsening of 6.97). A closer observation is that its effect is highly concentrated on hard negatives: on LPE, AUC only drops from 99.81% to 98.49% (drop of 1.32), and EER worsens from 1.71% to 5.81%. A drop of 5.74 on LPH versus 1.32 on LPE indicates that the phoneme-level mechanism almost does not consume capacity on easy negatives; it is born for confusable words—this strictly aligns with the design intent and again verifies the core judgment that "discriminative information is concentrated in a few phonemes."

Second, the independent contribution of the memory bank is small; its value is realized through augmentation. Looking only at "removing memory bank," AUC drops only 0.09 percentage points (92.45% to 92.36%), and EER worsens by 0.21 percentage points, almost within the noise level; but the overall augmentation (including memory bank sampling, three-class discrimination, and training set mining) brings a 3.11 percentage point improvement (92.45% to 95.56%, 3.11% given in the original paper). A reasonable interpretation is: the memory bank, as a supplement to the similarity matrix in the text registration branch, has limited effect on its own; its main value is serving as an augmentation raw material library—the generation of hard negatives via insertion, substitution, and deletion of phonemes requires sampling from the bank. This also means the two designs are deeply bound, and the ablation table does not fully decouple them (the individual contributions of the three-class discriminator and training set mining are not reported by the paper).

Third, the benefits of augmentation are extremely asymmetric across the two modalities. Augmentation in the text registration branch brings 3.11 percentage points; augmentation in the audio registration branch (Table III) brings only 0.14 percentage points (92.95% to 93.09%). The reason can be read from the loss structure: the channel through which augmentation acts is the three-class loss of the text registration branch; the loss for the audio registration branch (Eq. 7) has no corresponding term, and it can only benefit indirectly through shared encoders and shared training.

Audio registration ablation (Table III, AUC on LPH): Complete system (text plus audio fusion, with augmentation) 96.59%; audio registration with augmentation 93.09%; audio registration without augmentation 92.95%; removing phoneme-level 90.82%. Phoneme-level contrastive learning contributes 2.13 percentage points in the audio modality (the paper states this as an absolute improvement of 2.13%), and EER improves by 2.44 percentage points (14.15% vs. 16.59%), and is also concentrated on LPH (AUC on LPE differs by only 0.02). The value of fusion: from the best single-modality audio registration 93.09% to fusion 96.59% (higher by 3.50), the paper concludes from this that "audio provides complementary benefits to text"—dual-modality registration is not redundant, but information complementary.

Visualization analysis (Fig. 2, Fig. 3) supplements evidence from a mechanistic level. The attention map comparison in Fig. 2 shows three types of samples: positive (registered word "the house"), hard negative (registered word "information", query audio "induration"), and easy negative (registered word "the captain", query audio "swear allegiance"), showing that hard negatives are most easily misjudged due to significant phoneme similarity with positives, while this model can distinguish them through phoneme alignment. The visualization in Fig. 3 shows that after contrastive learning, phoneme embeddings exhibit clear intra-cluster aggregation and inter-cluster separation; it also honestly exposes remaining difficulties—"the prince's" and "the princes" are homophonic and acoustically inseparable; the cluster for the near-phoneme pair /S/ and /SH/ is still very close, being the main source of misclassification. These two figures essentially draw the line for the method's capability boundaries.

## Main Contributions

The paper summarizes three contributions itself, which are supported by experimental evidence:

1. Proposes a phoneme-level contrastive learning method, shifting the contrastive learning of open-vocabulary keyword spotting from the sentence level to the phoneme level, obtaining precise phoneme-level representations using forced alignment plus frame global averaging, and providing fine-grained supervision via intra-batch phoneme negatives—ablation proves this is the largest single source of performance gain (Table II: AUC on LPH drops by 5.74 percentage points after removal).
2. Proposes a hard negative augmentation scheme using a context-independent phoneme memory bank plus a third-class discriminator, using insertion, substitution, and deletion of three phoneme edits to directionally manufacture confusable negatives, combined with training set hard example mining—augmentation brings an overall 3.11 percentage point improvement (Table II), and acts specifically on the text registration branch.
3. Constructs a keyword spotting system that uniformly supports three registration modes: text, audio, and text plus audio. One model branches by modality, masks missing modalities, and after fusion achieves LPH AUC 96.59%, EER 8.47%, and LPE AUC 99.97%, EER 0.57% on LibriPhrase (Table I), surpassing all previously published systems.

Beyond the contributions, the methodological value of this paper lies in demonstrating a route of "constraints prior to data": not relying on 1.5 million pairs of augmented data (the approach of MM-KWS), but achieving better results on the original data through phoneme-level alignment constraints.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, training relies on forced alignment. The boundary quality of all phoneme-level supervision is determined by the aligner: training data must have transcript text, and the alignment quality of the aligner under accents, spontaneous speech, and noisy conditions will degrade, directly polluting the phoneme-level supervision. Although inference does not require alignment (the text registration branch relies on self-attention for automatic alignment), this dependency on the training side limits the method to "languages and data with clean transcripts"; extending to low-resource languages would require changing the aligner or reverting to implicit alignment, which is precisely the precision shortcoming criticized by the paper itself.

Second, the "context-independent" setting of the memory bank contradicts the coarticulation of real speech. The bank stores phoneme prototypes out of context, but the acoustic realization of the same phoneme varies greatly in different contexts (this is exactly the reason why context-dependent modeling exists in speech recognition). The /S/ and /SH/ clusters in Fig. 3 are still close, indicating that the current granularity has not yet reached the limit of distinguishing near-phonemes; for homophonic pairs like "the prince's" and "the princes," any pure acoustic matching is theoretically unsolvable, and can only be resolved by language model priors or additional context.

Third, absolute performance is still far from productization. An EER of 8.47% on LPH is the best result in academic comparison, but the false alarm budget for always-on listening products is usually measured in false wake-ups per hour; there is a gap between AUC/EER and actual working point metrics, involving threshold selection and conversion of the base negative ratio, which the paper does not report any deployment-oriented metrics for. On the model side, the paper also does not report parameter count, computational cost, or inference latency—the base is a Whisper-Tiny encoder plus a grapheme-to-phoneme conversion model; the feasibility of this combination on microcontroller-level edge devices is questionable, and its deployment posture is completely different from that of small models in the edge keyword detection series (such as binarization and low-bit quantization routes).

Fourth, the best results require the heaviest registration burden. The 96.59% dual-modality result requires users to provide both text and audio registration simultaneously, making the registration process the most cumbersome; the difference between light registration (pure text 95.56%) and heavy registration is about 1 percentage point, and products need to make a trade-off between registration friction and performance.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

Single evaluation dimension: Evaluated only on LibriPhrase (English, read style, relatively clean), without noise, far-field, multi-speaker, or multi-lingual conditions, and without investigating the robustness of the audio registration modality when the quality of registered audio changes—this is exactly the key argument in the paper's introduction for distinguishing the applicable scenarios of audio and text registration; lacking this set of experiments means the key argument lacks data support. There are no external baselines for audio registration (all baselines in Table I are pure text or text plus audio modalities), so the horizontal position of the audio path cannot be confirmed. Incomplete ablation decoupling: the three-class discriminator, training set mining, and memory bank augmentation are bundled in the augmentation setting, and their individual contributions are not reported by the paper; the rationale for the values of the temperature parameter and momentum coefficient 0.8 is not reported; training details such as learning rate scheduling are not reported. Additionally, no significance or variance information is attached to all results; differences at the 0.09 percentage point level (such as the memory bank alone) are difficult to interpret without variance information.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the paper's own evidence chain, there are at least four directions. First, upgrade the memory bank from context-independent to context-dependent: store phoneme bigram or trigram prototypes, or cluster phoneme embeddings by context, directly targeting the near-phoneme confusion and coarticulation mismatch exposed in Fig. 3. Second,摆脱 (break free from) the dependency on forced alignment: use a learnable alignment module (e.g., distilling alignment boundaries or contrastive alignment objectives) to obtain accuracy close to explicit alignment on data without transcripts, so that the method can be migrated to low-resource languages and spontaneous speech. Third, edge-side deployment: distill or quantize the phoneme-level alignment verification paradigm into streaming small models, combine with edge-side keyword spotting architectures, and simultaneously supplement reports on parameter count and latency. Fourth, adjudication of homophones and near-homophones: introduce language model priors or context features for homophonic registered word pairs, explicitly separating samples that are "acoustically inseparable" from the acoustic matching task, rather than letting the decision maker bear the burden directly.
