## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the technology for detecting specific trigger words within continuous audio streams, serving as a hands-free voice entry point for smart speakers, wearable devices, voice assistants, and IoT devices. Unlike large-model voice interactions that can be "woken on demand," KWS models must **run persistently and remain powered on** at all times on the device to ensure immediate response to wake words. This imposes a hard constraint: the model must simultaneously satisfy small memory footprint and low computational cost; otherwise, it cannot be deployed on low-resource edge chips. This is the fundamental reason for the existence of the "small footprint KWS" sub-field—parameter count and Multiply-Accumulate operations (MACs) are not merely academic embellishments, but the entry threshold for deployment.

The second pain point stems from real-world acoustic environments. The paper points out at the beginning that the success of recent deep neural network-based small footprint KWS models has largely been built on datasets with **near-field, low-noise, clean recordings**, whereas real usage scenarios are often noisy homes, public places, and industrial areas. Background noise interferes with keyword detection, leading to two types of actual failures: **false activations** (noise is mistaken for a wake word, causing the device to wake up inexplicably) and **missed detections** (the user says the wake word, but the device does not respond). For always-on voice entry products, these two types of errors directly determine the lower bound of user experience.

The third pain point is not explicitly stated in the paper but runs throughout the text: **small networks inherently lack the ability to learn noise**. Large models can absorb noise perturbations through redundant capacity, whereas small models in the order of 100K parameters "cannot learn" when directly facing high-noise training data—as mentioned in the related work, "small neural networks struggle to learn in high-noise conditions." This is precisely the motivation for introducing curriculum learning (curriculum learning) later in the text. The contradiction between noise robustness and model capacity is the core tension this paper aims to address.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper梳理s the gaps in existing solutions from three technical clues:

- **Mainstream small footprint architectures ignore noise robustness.** From Chen et al. first treating KWS as a classification problem solved by DNNs, to Tang and Lin’s dilated convolution ResNet, Choi et al.’s TC-ResNet (1D time-domain convolution for real-time KWS), BC-ResNet’s broadcast residual learning (combining 1D and 2D convolutions), and MatchboxNet’s grouped depthwise separable convolutions, the optimization goal of this main architectural evolution line has almost always been accuracy and computational efficiency on clean test sets. The paper explicitly criticizes: most studies do not consider noise robustness, which is exactly the key factor determining whether a voice interface can be successfully deployed. In other words, the community has competed for years on "accuracy" and "speed," while the dimension of "noise resistance" has been systematically ignored.
- **Data augmentation-based noise resistance methods are insufficient for small networks.** Noise mixture training (adding environmental noise to training speech for multi-condition training) is a widespread method to enhance the noise robustness of acoustic models. However, as mentioned above, small networks find it difficult to learn high-noise samples directly. Therefore, curriculum learning has been introduced (e.g., Braun et al.’s progressive noise addition curriculum, Higuchi et al.’s data-parameter-based dynamic curriculum), feeding samples from easy to hard. The paper’s evaluation is: these methods have improved, but **still fall short when dealing with high-noise environments**.
- **The combination of attention mechanisms and noise-robust KWS remains a blank.** Attention mechanisms themselves are mature: SE-Net first proved the effectiveness of channel attention, CBAM combines channel attention with spatial attention, and ECA-Net implements dimensionality-reduction-free efficient channel attention using 1D convolutions. However, the paper points out that these mechanisms often come with considerable computational and memory overhead, hindering edge deployment; the integration of lightweight attention (SE, ECA) with noise-robust KWS systems "is still under exploration." As a direct predecessor, ConvMixer (Ng et al., ICASSP 2022) uses a mixer module for global channel-weighted feature interaction and performs well under noisy far-field conditions, but **the extraction of local features still has room for improvement**—and local features are exactly the key for the model to cope with different noise levels and complex audio inputs. This judgment is the foundation of the entire paper: ConvMixer provides a global information flow, lacking a cheap local selection mechanism.

In addition to the deficiencies listed by the paper itself, another criticism implicit but not elaborated upon can be supplemented from the perspective of methodological mechanism: SE and ECA are both **pure channel attention**—regardless of which frequency band in the spectrum is contaminated by noise, all frequency units in the same channel share a scalar weight. Additive noise is physically frequency-selective (thunder is concentrated in low frequencies, DTMF tones are narrowband, car horns have their own fundamental frequency clusters). The granularity of "one weight per channel" does not match the physical distribution of noise. This observation explains why the attention module finally chosen in this paper performs fine-grained weighting on the frequency dimension as well; it is the key to understanding the motivation behind the C2D module design.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the key challenge this paper aims to solve can be stated as: significantly improve the accuracy of small footprint KWS models under strong noise conditions of 0 dB, -5 dB, and -10 dB, **without almost increasing the number of parameters and computational cost** (this is a rigid requirement of the small footprint constraint). This requires the attention module to simultaneously satisfy three mutually constraining conditions: First, the granularity must be fine enough to express frequency-specific information such as "which frequency band of which channel is credible"; Second, the overhead must be small enough, not exploding a model with 119K parameters like CBAM or self-attention; Third, the insertion position must be reasonable, compatible with the residual-style block structure of ConvMixer without disrupting the original information flow. Additionally, on the training side, the problem of small networks failing to learn high-noise data must be solved. This is a design search problem under the triple constraints of "granularity—overhead—position," and the paper’s contribution is essentially finding a Pareto-optimal point in this search space.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall skeleton of FCA-Net directly follows ConvMixer (Ng et al., ICASSP 2022), divided into three sections (Fig. 1):

- **Pre-Convolution Section (Pre-Block 1-3)**: Three "1D depthwise separable convolution + BN + Swish" units, with kernel sizes of 5, 7, and 1 respectively. Their role is to perform layer-by-layer temporal local smoothing and channel reorganization on the input Mel-spectrogram features. The arrangement of kernels increasing first and then shrinking to 1 allows the front section to aggregate adjacent frame information in a slightly larger temporal receptive field first, and then perform pure channel mixing using 1×1-style convolutions.
- **ConvMixer Section (ConvMixer Block 1-4)**: Each block has a dual-path structure internally. The **frequency path** sequentially consists of 2D convolution + Swish, 2D depthwise separable convolution + Swish, and 2D convolution + BN + Swish (output channels: 1), responsible for local feature extraction on the frequency dimension; The **time path** is a 1D depthwise separable convolution + BN + Swish, followed by an MLP mixer (time channel mixing + frequency channel mixing) and a skip connection. The configurations of the four blocks change step by step (Fig. 1): ConvMixer 1 time kernel 9, frequency kernel 5, 2D convolution filters 64; ConvMixer 2 time kernel 11, frequency kernel 5 (filter count marked as 32 in Fig. 1, alternating with the 2D Pre-Block annotation, causing ambiguity in text extraction); ConvMixer 3 time kernel 13, frequency kernel 7, filters 16; ConvMixer 4 time kernel 15, frequency kernel 7, filters 8. The time kernel increases block by block (9→11→13→15) while the number of filters decreases sharply block by block (64→32→16→8). This is a "wide receptive field with few channels in deep layers, narrow receptive field with many channels in shallow layers" funnel design—shallow layers use a large number of channels to encode rich local spectral patterns, while deep layers use wider temporal context to assemble these patterns into semantic-level evidence.
- **Post-Convolution Section (Post-Block 1-3)**: Three 1D depthwise separable convolutions + BN + Swish, with kernel sizes of 17, 19 (dilation rate 2), and 1 respectively. The output channels are all 128, followed by Linear + Sigmoid to output 12 classes.

The motivation for choosing ConvMixer instead of ResNet/TC-ResNet/BC-ResNet as the backbone is stated directly by the paper: the mixer unit computes **cross-global channel weighted feature interaction**, improving information flow, and this model has already been verified to be effective under noisy far-field conditions. A deeper layer of complementarity lies in the fact that the mixer’s "global channel mixing" is good at reorganizing discriminative information scattered across channels, but is powerless against the **fine selection of local time-frequency structures**; the attention module exactly fills this local selection link. Global organization plus local selection is the essence of the name FCA-Net (Frequency & Channel Attention)—placing attention on the cross-granularity of frequency and channel dimensions.

On top of the backbone, FCA-Net inserts the C2D attention module after each ConvMixer block, a configuration called FCA-Net-all. The paper also defines three ablation variants: FCA-Net-pre (adding attention only in the pre-convolution section), FCA-Net-post (adding attention only in the post-convolution section), and FCA-Net-final (adding attention before the final linear classification layer), to systematically study the impact of insertion positions.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The operations of the ConvMixer block are described by Equations (1)-(4) (the paper’s original symbols are slightly loose, and the references to f/f1/f2 are not completely consistent between the text and formulas; the following is a literal transcription):

$$z = \sigma \circ f_1(\sigma \circ f(x)) \tag{1}$$

where f is 2D depthwise separable convolution, and σ is Swish activation. The input x is first processed by 2D-DWS convolution, passed through activation, convolved again, and passed through activation again to obtain frequency domain features z.

$$y_1 = \sigma \circ \text{BatchNorm}(f(z)) \tag{2}$$

The frequency domain features z are then passed through a 1D depthwise separable convolution (referred to as f2 in the text), BN, and activation to obtain time domain features y1.

$$y_2 = \sigma \circ \text{BatchNorm}(f_2(y_1)) \tag{3}$$

The time domain features pass through another layer of 1D depthwise separable convolution plus BN plus activation.

$$\tilde{y} = x + y_1 + f_3(y_2) \tag{4}$$

The final output is the sum of the input x, intermediate features y1, and processed features, where f3 is the mixer layer. This three-way summation is a progressive residual: the original input, frequency domain processing results, and time domain processing results are all retained in the output. If any path fails to learn well, it will not block gradient propagation—this is especially important for small networks, as small networks have less representation redundancy, and hard residuals ensure that the "gain" nature of attention and convolution rather than "replacement" nature. The mixer layer f3 consists of two types of MLPs: time channel mixing and frequency channel mixing, each containing two linear layers and a GELU activation, independently processing each time channel and frequency channel.

The mathematical core of the C2D attention module is Equations (5)-(7). The input feature map X has C channels, F frequency points, and T time frames. First, global average pooling is performed along the time dimension:

$$z(c, f) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} X(c, f, t) \tag{5}$$

Obtaining a C×F channel-frequency plane z, where each element represents the average activation of the corresponding (channel, frequency point) pair over the entire sentence. Then this plane passes through two consecutive 2D convolution layers (containing BN and ReLU) to calculate attention weights:

$$\omega = \sigma(\text{Conv2}(\text{ReLU}(\text{BN}(\text{Conv1}(z))))) \tag{6}$$

(The specific type of σ in the equation is not explicitly stated by the paper; assuming the normalization convention for attention weights, it is Sigmoid, making it fall between 0 and 1 to act as a gate.) Finally, the weights are multiplied element-wise back to the original feature map:

$$X' = \omega \odot X \tag{7}$$

The shape of ω is C×F, broadcast and reused on the time dimension—all frames of the same speech sentence share a set of attention weights.

There are two ingenious design choices in this mathematical structure worth dissecting. **First, why pool only along the time dimension instead of global pooling?** SE/ECA compresses the entire feature map into a C-dimensional vector, averaging out frequency and time information; C2D retains the frequency dimension, effectively using "the average energy of this channel in this frequency band in a sentence" as the basis for attention calculation. Pooling brings translation invariance (the position of the keyword in a 1-second window is uncertain, and attention should not depend on time position), while retaining the frequency dimension preserves noise localization capability. **Second, why use 2D convolution instead of fully connected layers to generate weights?** There is strong local correlation between adjacent channels (corresponding to features learned by adjacent filters) and adjacent frequency points (Mel filter banks themselves overlap) on the C×F plane. 2D convolution exactly models this local correlation, and the number of parameters is only related to the kernel size, completely different from FC layers whose parameters are proportional to the square of C×F. The paper also cites Figure 4 (not included in the text extraction) to compare the differences between convolutional attention and traditional fully connected attention.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: C2D — Fine-Grained Channel-Frequency Attention Based on 2D Convolution

C2D (Convolution-based two-dimensional attention) is the core new module of this paper. Its design starting point is to provide CNNs with **fine-grained attention weights covering combinations of different channels and frequency positions**. Compared to SE (one scalar per channel) and ECA (also one scalar per channel, just using 1D convolution instead of FC for cross-channel interaction), the weight granularity of C2D is a 2D grid of C×F—it can express frequency-specific judgments such as "the 37th channel is unreliable in the 500-800 Hz segment, but reliable in the 2-4 kHz segment."

Why is this granularity particularly important for noisy KWS? Because the physical characteristic of additive noise is frequency selectivity: the MUSAN noise used in the paper’s experiments includes DTMF dual-tone multi-frequency signals (concentrated in several narrow bands), thunder (mainly low-frequency energy), and car horns (with specific fundamental frequency clusters). Suppose a layer’s features simultaneously encode vowel formant information and consonant frication information. When a broadband strong noise enters, it contaminates the feature channels of specific frequency bands. Pure channel attention can only globally down-regulate this channel (including the uncontaminated frequency bands), whereas C2D can only suppress the contaminated frequency bands. Ablation data supports this analysis: inserted at the all position, C2D reaches 92.65% at 0 dB, ECA is 91.86%, and SE is only 89.82% (Table II). The finer the granularity, the better under noise.

Regarding cost, the overhead of C2D is almost negligible: FCA-Net’s MACs relative to the ConvMixer backbone only increase from 22.2M to 22.3M (+0.45%), and the parameter count remains 119K (Table I). The reason for the low overhead is precisely the time-dimension pooling in Equation (5)—the attention branch first compresses on the T dimension, and subsequent convolutions are performed on the small C×F plane. Compared to the convolutions on the large C×F×T cube of the main network, this is a drop in the bucket. The two usually contradictory goals of fine granularity and low overhead are achieved simultaneously here, which is the most valuable lesson in the entire design: **by "pooling first then convolving," the input dimension of the attention branch is reduced from three-dimensional to two-dimensional. The granularity loss only occurs on the time dimension (which happens to be insensitive to noise), exchanging for the complete retention of the frequency dimension.**

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Systematic Ablation of Attention Insertion Positions (Four Variants of FCA-Net)

The second innovation is not a single module, but **treating "where to insert attention" as a first-class research object**. The paper defines four variants: FCA-Net-all / pre / post / final, and performs full cross-experiments with three attention modules (SE, ECA, C2D) (Table II). This 3×4 position-module matrix is rare in KWS literature—most attention papers only report "attention makes it better," without answering "why insert it here."

The experiments reveal a **non-monotonic position effect**: taking C2D as an example, inserting only in the pre-convolution section (pre) drops to 89.08% at 0 dB, and inserting only in the post-convolution section (post) drops to 89.02%, both lower than the backbone without attention (90.62%); inserting at the all position surges to 92.65%, and inserting at final (before the classification layer) also reaches 91.18%. In other words, attention is not a panacea; **inserting it in the wrong position is actually poison**. Mechanistically, this can be understood as follows: the pre-convolution section and post-convolution section are pure convolution pipelines, where feature semantics change step by step from raw spectrum to classification semantics. Inserting attention at a single point creates a drastic feature distribution reorganization at that point, disrupting the adaptation of subsequent convolutional layers to the pre-trained distribution; whereas the ConvMixer section itself is a three-way summation residual structure (Equation 4), adding attention after each block is equivalent to gentle recalibration at each residual node, and the attention is "buffered" by the residual structure. Additionally, the multiple attention modules at the all position are stacked level by level, equivalent to performing noise filtering at each level of feature abstraction. The attention in deep layers can make more precise judgments based on the features already filtered by the previous layer—this cascading effect cannot be obtained by single-point insertion. The paper’s original text does not give this explanation (only reports the data); the above is the author’s inference based on the structure, but the data itself (Table II) is sufficient to support the conclusion that "position is not free."

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Curriculum-Based Multi-Condition Training Strategy

On the training strategy side, FCA-Net follows the curriculum-based multi-condition training of Ng et al.’s ConvMixer paper, but explicitly lists it as one of the three major components of the system. The specific approach: training is divided into four stages of gradually increasing difficulty—the first stage uses completely clean samples; the subsequent three stages gradually introduce noise into fixed N samples, with SNR decreasing in steps of -5 dB. The conditions are evenly distributed among the N samples, i.e., the second stage [clean, 0], the third stage [clean, 0, -5], and the fourth stage [clean, 0, -5, -10].

Why is curriculum necessary instead of one-step mixed training? The analogy is that humans learn according to a schedule: first learn easy clean samples, then learn difficult samples containing strong noise. For small networks, this order has real optimization-theoretic meaning: the inter-class structure of clean speech (which word corresponds to which formant trajectory) is low-complexity and highly regular, and small-capacity models can stably converge to this solution. If one starts with -10 dB mixed training directly, the loss surface is ruggedly disturbed by noise, and small models easily converge to shortcut solutions that only learn to "distinguish signal-to-noise ratio" rather than "distinguish words." Curriculum learning establishes clean speech representation anchors first, allowing the gradients in subsequent noise-adding stages to fine-tune around this anchor, avoiding representation collapse. Note that the clean condition list always retains clean samples (clean samples account for one-quarter to one-half in each stage), which prevents the model from "forgetting" performance on clean speech in the later stages—according to Table I, FCA-Net achieves 98.25% on clean V2-12 (highest among small models), and noise enhancement does not sacrifice clean performance; this design is an important reason.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

The differences with three types of existing methods can be viewed in a coordinate system. **Difference from SE/ECA series channel attention**: SE generates channel weights from a pooled vector using two fully connected layers (intermediate dimensionality reduction C→C/r→C, reduction ratio r not reported in the paper); ECA cancels dimensionality reduction and uses 1D convolution with kernel size k=Ψ(C) adaptively determined for local cross-channel interaction (the specific form of Ψ is not given in the paper, citing the original literature). Both work on the C dimension; C2D extends the working plane to C×F, using 2D convolution instead of FC/ECA’s 1D convolution. The granularity rises from "channel" to "channel × frequency point," which is the essential difference. **Difference from CBAM series spatial attention**: CBAM adds a spatial attention path in addition to channel attention, with the two paths in series; C2D covers channel and frequency simultaneously with a single module (in KWS, frequency is the main semantic of "space"), avoiding the doubling of overhead caused by dual-path structures. **Difference from Transformer series (KWT, AST)**: KWT-3 has 5,361K parameters and 526.3M MACs, AST-Tiny has 5,805K parameters and 782.2M MACs (Table I). The global modeling capability of self-attention comes at the cost of two orders of magnitude in resource overhead; FCA-Net achieves noise performance close to or even better with 119K parameters and 22.3M MACs (92.65% at 0 dB vs. KWT-3’s 83.97%, AST-Tiny’s 83.31%, Table I), taking the low-cost route of "local convolution attention + mixer global mixing." **Difference from direct predecessor ConvMixer**: The backbone is exactly the same (119K/22.2M), the difference lies only in inserting a C2D after each ConvMixer block, exchanging a 0.45% MACs increment for a 2.03 percentage point improvement at 0 dB and a 2.93 percentage point improvement at -10 dB (Table I, Table II), which is a typical "attention leverage" improvement.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

**Speech Data**: Google Speech Commands V2 (GSC V2), 105,000 utterances, 35 words, 1 second per utterance, 16 kHz sampling rate. The task uses the official train/validation/test split for 12-class classification: up, down, left, right, yes, no, on, off, go, stop (10 command words) plus silence and unknown, with the unknown class swallowing the remaining 25 words. **Noise Data**: 930 noise files from the MUSAN dataset, totaling about 6 hours, 16 kHz, covering technical and non-technical sounds (the paper gives examples of DTMF tones, thunder, car horns), added to command word samples to simulate noisy far-field environments. Whether the noise files used for training and testing were split disjointly is not reported by the paper—this relates to the strictness of generalization evaluation.

**Feature Configuration**: MFCC, sampling rate 16 kHz, 40 MFCC coefficients, Mel-spectrogram parameters n_fft=400, hop length=160, n_mels=64, f_min=20 Hz, f_max=8000 Hz; each command is 1 second, with zero-padding on the right if insufficient. (The coexistence of 40 MFCCs and n_mels=64 is a noteworthy configuration; the paper does not explain the relationship between the 64 Mel filter banks and the 40 output coefficients.) **Training Details**: Data augmentation includes time shifts of -100 to 100 ms, spectral masking with maximum length 25 in both time and frequency directions (SpecAugment), and noise addition with SNR of 0/-5/-10 dB; regularization uses mixup with a ratio of 0.2; batch size 128; initial learning rate 0.005, decaying by 0.85 every 4 epochs after the 5th epoch; optimizer Adam; a learning rate scheduler with Cosine Annealing Warm Restarts is used simultaneously—step decay and cosine annealing are used in parallel, and the paper does not explain how the two are superimposed; trained for 200 epochs in total, with early stopping if no improvement for 20 epochs.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper uses three layers of metrics. The first layer is **clean performance**: accuracy on the official GSC V2-12 test set, which is a community-passed comparable benchmark, used to confirm that the changes do not sacrifice clean scenarios. The second layer is **noise performance**: accuracy after adding noise to test commands to four SNR levels: 20 dB, 0 dB, -5 dB, and -10 dB—0 dB means speech and noise have the same energy, -10 dB means noise is 10 times stronger than speech. The latter two levels exceed the operating points of most products and belong to the stress test zone. The third layer is **resource overhead**: parameter count (K) and Multiply-Accumulate operations MACs (M). The rationale for selection is implicit but clear: for always-on edge KWS, accuracy and resources are two legs that must be reported simultaneously; noise papers that only report accuracy cannot answer "whether it can be deployed."

Notably, the metrics **not** reported by the paper are: false alarm rate, ROC/DET curves, latency and memory on actual devices, and variance over multiple runs. The first metric for KWS product deployment is actually the number of false wake-ups per hour. This paper uses closed-set 12-class classification accuracy to proxy open-set detection performance, which is a substantial gap in the evaluation design (see Limitations section for details).

Four findings are worth expanding. **Finding 1: SE is inferior to no attention at all positions**. SE’s best result is 89.82% at the all position (0 dB), which is 0.8 points lower than the backbone without attention (90.62%)—on a small model with 119K parameters, SE’s dimensionality reduction bottleneck (FC structure C→C/r→C) not only introduces extra parameters but also pools away all frequency information, effectively buying negative returns with overhead. This explains why "universal accuracy-boosting modules" like SE cannot be copied directly when transferred to small footprint KWS. **Finding 2: ECA is conditionally effective**. ECA also deteriorates at the pre position (89.37% at 0 dB), but has positive gains at post/all/final, reaching 80.00% at -10 dB at the all position (+2.53 points over the backbone), indicating that dimensionality-reduction-free 1D convolution channel interaction is usable on small models, just with a lower ceiling than C2D. **Finding 3: C2D has the strongest and non-monotonic position effect**. Single-point insertion (pre/post) actually causes a full-line decline (89.08%/89.02% at 0 dB), only all and final positions are effective, with the all position being comprehensively optimal. This confirms the analysis in the Methodology section: attention needs to couple level by level with ConvMixer residual blocks to play a role; single-point insertion in a pure convolution pipeline causes sudden reorganization of feature distribution. **Finding 4: C2D-final has a slight advantage in light noise**. At 20 dB, C2D-final’s 96.09% surpasses the all position’s 95.99% (on Clean, all is slightly higher 96.96 vs. 96.92), indicating that when noise is light, performing feature recalibration only once before the classifier is sufficient, and avoids disturbing intermediate features; but as noise increases (starting from 0 dB), the advantage of the all position widens (92.65 vs. 91.18), requiring level-by-level filtering under strong noise. This provides a tunable knob for engineering deployment: for scenarios with clear noise environments, the insertion strategy can be selected according to the noise level.

Synthesizing Table II, the gain of C2D-all relative to the backbone is Clean +0.64, 20 dB +0.47, 0 dB +2.03, -5 dB +1.60, -10 dB +2.93 percentage points—a monotonic trend where the stronger the noise, the greater the gain (except for a slight shrinkage at -5 dB relative to 0 dB) directly supports the mechanistic explanation that "attention learned noise-related frequency-selective suppression," because if attention were just ordinary feature recalibration, the gain would be independent of the noise level.

## Main Contributions

The paper summarizes its contributions in three points, which can be refined into four after deep reading:

1. **Proposed the C2D attention module**—generating fine-grained attention weights on the channel-frequency plane using two 2D convolution layers, achieving frequency-specific feature recalibration with a 0.45% MACs increment (22.2M→22.3M, Table I), filling the application gap of lightweight attention in noise-robust small footprint KWS. This is the core contribution at the methodological level.
2. **Established a systematic ablation paradigm of "attention type × insertion position"** (Table II)—for the first time answering both "what attention to use" and "where to insert it" in the KWS context, revealing the non-monotonic position effect where single-point insertion deteriorates and block-by-block insertion yields the greatest benefits, providing direct methodological reference value for subsequent attention-based KWS work.
3. **Achieved noise SOTA within the small model group**—119K parameters, V2-12 clean 98.25%, 0/-5/-10 dB noise 92.65%/87.97%/80.40%, comprehensively surpassing BC-ResNet-6 with 1.7 times the parameters and same-scale ConvMixer (Table I), and surpassing two Transformer large models KWT-3 and AST-Tiny by 8-9.5 points with a 45-49 times parameter advantage.
4. **Verified the complementarity of the "global mixer + local fine-grained attention + curriculum training" trio**—the three mechanisms are responsible for global information flow organization, frequency-selective noise suppression, and training curriculum from easy to hard, respectively. Their indispensable combination provides a replicable configuration template for noise KWS.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**Attention is static**. The global average pooling along the time dimension in Equation (5) means that the entire speech sentence shares a set of C×F weights, and attention cannot change over time. However, many actual noises are non-stationary (sudden knocks, human voice interference, intermittent mechanical noise). An ideal solution should also perform attention on the time dimension. C2D’s design of exchanging time dimension for overhead is sufficient for stationary noise but has a clear ceiling for time-varying noise. **The closed-set classification task setting is distant from real KWS deployment**. In the 12-class setting, the unknown class swallows 25 words, and the model only needs to distinguish unknown from 10 command words; real wake word systems face arbitrary open-set audio (TV sound, music, irrelevant human speech). Evaluation of false activation behavior requires false alarm rate metrics. This paper proxies everything with accuracy (the paper does not report any false alarm/FPR/DET data). **The output layer uses Linear + Sigmoid (Fig. 1)** to handle 12-class single-label classification, which is also a very unconventional choice. Sigmoid output is not normalized, and the paper does not discuss its cooperation with cross-entropy training. Furthermore, the assumption that the 12 classes are not mutually exclusive does not match the task setting. **The noise resistance method is only additive noise**. The paper adds MUSAN noise under the name of "simulating noisy far-field environments," but the dominant degradation in real far-field is convolutional reverberation (room impulse response). The mechanisms by which reverberation and additive noise destroy the spectrum are different. The paper does not report any reverberation experiments, and the support for the far-field claim is incomplete.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

**Single dataset**. All experiments were completed on GSC V2 (105K sentences, 35 words, English, 1 second), without cross-dataset validation (e.g., CREMA, self-collected far-field data). The generalization ability for multiple languages, multiple speakers, and long phrases is unknown. **The train/test split of noise is not explained**. The 930 files of MUSAN are used for both training noise addition and (presumably) testing noise addition. Whether disjoint file sets were used for the two purposes is not reported by the paper—if the test noise appeared in training, the noise generalization conclusions would be overestimated. **No statistical significance control**. All results are from single training, without multiple seed repetitions and variance reporting. Differences at the 0.3-0.6 point level in the ablation table (e.g., 95.85 vs. 95.83 for ECA-final and ECA-post at 20 dB) likely fall within the random fluctuation interval. **Two summary numbers in the text do not match the table**: "improvement of 2% to 3%" and "maximum improvement of 7.4%" cannot be strictly reproduced from Table I (see Experimental Results section), weakening the rigor of the conclusions. **Curriculum learning is not ablated separately**. The ablation in this paper only covers attention modules and positions (Table II). The independent contribution of curriculum-based multi-condition training to the final performance (relative to ordinary one-time multi-condition training) is not isolated and verified in this paper, and one can only trace back to the original paper by Ng et al. **Several implementation details are missing**: The kernel size and number of channels for the two 2D convolutions inside C2D, the dimensionality reduction ratio r for SE, and the form of the kernel function Ψ for ECA are not reported by the paper. Reproduction requires referring to the code or original literature. **No real-device verification**. 22.3M MACs stays on paper; latency, peak memory, and power consumption were not measured on actual DSP/MCU. The last mile of always-on deployment is not connected.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

Following the methodological limitations, there are several natural extensions. **Time-dimension dynamic attention**: Replace the global average pooling in Equation (5) with lightweight time modeling (e.g., 1D convolution or grouped temporal pooling), making ω become frame-level weights of C×F×T′, specifically targeting non-stationary noise; overhead control can borrow ECA’s adaptive kernel idea, also performing kernel size adaptation on the time dimension. **Joint simulation of reverberation and noise**: Superimpose room impulse response convolution (RIR convolution) on top of MUSAN noise addition, making the "far-field" claim solid; on the training side, the curriculum can also expand from a single SNR axis to a dual-axis curriculum of SNR × reverberation time. **Open-set evaluation and false alarm optimization**: Introduce per-hour false trigger rate and DET curves, change the unknown class to open-set rejection based on embedding distance, and align evaluation with product metrics. **Compression and deployment pipeline**: Superimpose quantization (e.g., int8 or low-bit) and pruning on the 22.3M MACs model, combined with streaming inference to achieve real-device always-on verification, and report measured power consumption. **Transferability verification**: Insert C2D into other small footprint backbones like BC-ResNet and MatchboxNet to verify whether "inserting fine-grained attention after each residual block" is a universal law; on multi-channel (microphone array) inputs, extend attention to channel selection, connecting with Ng et al.’s 2023 work on multi-channel centroid perception. **Training strategy ablation**: Isolate the independent contribution of curriculum learning, and explore the combination upper limit of dynamic curriculum (e.g., data-parameter method by Higuchi et al.) automatically scheduled by noise difficulty and C2D.

Overall evaluation: This is a "small and accurate" engineering paper—the magnitude of innovation is not large (backbone and training strategy are all followed), but it landed the insight that "attention granularity must match the frequency-selective physical characteristics of noise" at the lowest cost (+0.1M MACs), and gave extra information beyond the paper’s narrative with a 3×4 position ablation table (single-point insertion deteriorates). For engineers doing edge-side noise-resistant KWS, the three most transferable judgments to take away are: First, channel-level scalar attention (SE) may have negative returns on small models; if granularity is not done, it should be done to the frequency dimension; Second, attention must couple level by level with residual structures; inserting it at a single point in a pure convolution pipeline will disrupt feature distribution; Third, gains under noise should be reported by SNR tier; only when the gain amplifies with increasing noise is it evidence that the mechanism is working at a mechanistic level.
