## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Since the proposal of "Attention is All You Need" in 2017, Transformers have comprehensively surpassed CNNs and RNNs in natural language processing. Their core advantages are threefold: stronger modeling of long-range dependencies (attention shortens the forward and backward signal path lengths between any two positions in the sequence; shorter paths make long-range dependencies easier to learn), superior semantic feature extraction capabilities, and high parallelizability during training. These advantages directly catalyzed the emergence of large language models with hundreds of billions of parameters, such as ChatGPT, and prompted researchers to push Transformers into tasks traditionally dominated by CNNs: computer vision, keyword spotting, and gesture recognition.

However, this capability comes at a cost—both model size and computational overhead are substantial. For the Internet of Things (IoT) movement's goal of enabling "cheap, resource-constrained devices to call frontier models with high energy efficiency," Transformers pose a dual obstacle: first, the memory requirements during inference cannot fit into the device; second, the time required for execution is too long. This paper concretizes this contradiction to a real hardware anchor point: ARM's Keyword Transformer (KWT) model, KWT-1, has 607k parameters, each being a 32-bit floating-point number, occupying 2.42 MB of memory (Table I, Table IV), while the target platform, the lowRISC Ibex microcontroller, has only 64 kB of RAM, a 50 MHz clock speed, and no floating-point unit (FPU) (Table II). The model is 37 times larger than the memory, which is the first wall. The second wall is the form of computing power: on an RV32IMC core without an FPU, all floating-point operations rely on software emulation, with floating-point division (the normalization denominator in SoftMax and related operations in GELU) being particularly expensive. The third wall is energy consumption: KWS requires devices to listen continuously; the number of clock cycles multiplied by the energy consumption per cycle equals battery life. If the number of cycles cannot be reduced, power consumption cannot be reduced.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper's critique of related work focuses on two lines. The first line is hardware acceleration works: AI-RISC and AIfES target low-power applications but do not specifically target Transformer architectures; neural network processors like FlexACC and DaDianNao support various machine learning architectures and outperform general-purpose processors in speed and energy efficiency, but their design goal is high-throughput accelerators rather than memory constraints at the microcontroller level; RISC-VTF proposes accelerating Transformers using RISC-V custom instructions; A³ uses approximate methods to accelerate the attention mechanism. These works share two blind spots: first, they typically start from the perspective of high-power, high-performance systems, rarely focusing on memory-related challenges in the process of "fitting Transformers onto the edge"—and memory is precisely the primary bottleneck in microcontroller scenarios; second, they often measure acceleration ratios by simulating or emulating Transformer operations, rather than performing end-to-end acceleration on a real model. This makes it impossible to answer the question of "how much accuracy degradation does hardware acceleration actually bring," because the accelerated entity is not a real, verifiable-accuracy model.

The second line is the software deployment ecosystem: the current popular practice for moving models to resource-constrained platforms involves libraries like TensorFlow Lite, ONNX, or TVM, and KWT-1 itself has a TFLite mobile version. The problem is that these libraries themselves can be several MB in size—no pressure for modern smartphones with several GB of memory, but many embedded platforms cannot even install the library. A deeper problem is that the abstraction boundary is wrong: these frameworks isolate embedded engineers and computer architects from the low-level details of model computation, making it very difficult to perform operator acceleration and memory optimization for specific platforms. In other words, generality is exchanged for the "right to not touch the bottom layer," but in a world of 64 kB RAM, the bottom layer is precisely the only space that can be mined.

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

The paper aims to answer a full-link question: to what extent can a state-of-the-art Transformer keyword detection model, after undergoing a combination of pruning, quantization, bare-metal rewriting, and hardware instruction extension, run on a 64 kB RAM, FPU-less RISC-V microcontroller—how much accuracy is lost, how much volume is reduced, how many cycles are saved, and what is the area cost, with every number coming from real execution rather than simulation. Broken down, there are four sub-challenges: (1) To what extent can the model be shrunk without causing the Transformer's accuracy to collapse, and which layers can be safely pruned; (2) How to select the scale factor for static quantification on an FPU-less platform to find a balance between accuracy and overflow; (3) How to implement the entire Transformer inference pipeline in bare-metal C without introducing MB-level frameworks, and manually manage the extremely limited memory; (4) Design custom instructions on an FPGA with minimal area overhead (the paper ultimately achieves about 29%), replacing the most time-consuming non-linear operators (GELU, SoftMax) from software-emulated floating-point to hardware lookup tables, gaining approximately 5x acceleration.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The paper adopts a deliberately "anti-abstraction" overall route, divided into four steps: Step 1, retrain and structurally prune KWT on a PC to obtain KWT-Tiny; Step 2, perform static post-training quantization to obtain KWT-Tiny-Q; Step 3, implement inference operations in bare-metal C and compile them to the RISC-V platform; Step 4, supplement the processor with custom hardware blocks and extend the instruction set to accelerate inference. The motivation for this design is stated plainly in the introduction—existing works either only care about quantization and ignore hardware, or only perform hardware acceleration but use simulated operations instead of real models, leading to unassessable accuracy degradation. Only when the full link is real can the cost of each step be measured, and the "acceleration for accuracy" trade-off can be clearly calculated.

The base model KWT is built on the Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture, a post-norm, encoder-only Transformer. A single inference pipeline is: raw audio is converted to Mel spectrograms (MFCC), the spectrogram is cut into flattened patches along the time dimension, mapped to high dimensions via a linear projection matrix, added with positional embeddings, and fed into串联 Transformer layers (self-attention blocks plus MLP blocks), finally outputting categories via a linear mapping. The original text cites KWT achieving 98.6% accuracy on Google Speech Commands; the baseline actually used in this paper is the KWT-1 configuration from the literature, with 607k parameters, 35 output classes, and 96.9% accuracy (Table I).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The core operation chain of the Transformer layer is as follows. The input signal $X_l$ is first split into Query, Key, and Value vectors (Equation 3), and the self-attention output is:

$$SA(X_l) = softmax\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right)V \tag{1}$$

where $d_h$ is the dimension of each attention head, and dividing by $\sqrt{d_h}$ is to control the variance inflation of dot products as dimensions increase; SoftMax is defined as $softmax(\vec{x})_i = e^{x_i} / \sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{x_j}$ (Equation 2). The attention output undergoes layer normalization: first standardized by mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ (Equation 4), then applying pre-calculated scaling $\gamma$ and shifting $\beta$ (Equation 5). It then enters the MLP block:

$$FFN(x) = GELU(xW_1 + b_1)W_2 + b_2 \tag{6}$$

where GELU uses the Gaussian error function (Equation 7):

$$GELU(x) = x \times \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + erf\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right) \tag{7}$$

Finally, categories are output via linear mapping $Linear(x) = xW_1 + b_1$ (Equation 8). Three types of expensive operations appear densely in this pipeline: matrix multiplication, SoftMax, and GELU calculations, and intermediate results between layers must be released from memory in time—this lays the groundwork for the operator-level acceleration and manual memory management described later.

Quantization adopts static post-training quantization, where weights are uniformly multiplied by a power-of-2 scale factor and rounded to INT8 (Equation 9):

$$W_{int} = floor(W_{float} \times 2^y) \tag{9}$$

Choosing powers of 2 is an intentional engineering decision: quantization and dequantization on embedded platforms degrade to shift operations, costing almost nothing. To prevent data loss caused by the accumulation of multiple INT8 weight multiplications during inference, intermediate residuals are stored in INT16.

The mathematical basis for hardware acceleration is the numerically stable form of SoftMax (Equation 10):

$$softmax(\vec{x})_i = \frac{e^{max(x)-x_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{max(x)-x_j}} \tag{10}$$

This transformation does not change the result but changes the exponent parameter from the unbounded $x_i$ to the bounded $max(x)-x_i$ (the paper's tests show all values fall between 0 and 10), which is the key to making finite-length lookup tables possible—without boundedness, the LUT scheme simply does not hold.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Iterative Pruning and Retraining for Dual-Class Output (KWT-Tiny)

Facing the first wall of "2.42 MB cannot fit into 64 kB," the paper adopts an iterative method to evaluate the impact of each layer on inference accuracy layer by layer, deleting the layers with the least impact. Experiments found that the most sacrificable aspect is depth—i.e., the number of串联 Transformer layers: one Transformer layer is sufficient to support two output classes, while the original KWT-1 requires 12 layers to support 35 classes. This conclusion itself is informative: the capacity brought by depth is mainly paying for multi-class discrimination; when cut to a dual-class task, depth is the first redundancy. Furthermore, the input MFCC is reduced from [40, 98] to [16, 26], which the paper calls a reasonable balance point between memory constraints and accuracy constraints. The complete structural comparison is in Table III: input dimensions [40,98]→[16,26], patch dimensions [40,1]→[16,1], layer normalization vector DIM 64→12, depth 12→1, number of attention heads 1→1, MLP dimension 256→24, dimension per head 64→8, attention score matrix SEQLEN 99→27, output classes 35→2. Training was completed based on the Torch-KWT library.

During the pruning process, a transferable empirical rule was derived: for KWT-Tiny, the combination of shrinking the MLP network and Transformer block depth presents the best accuracy-volume trade-off, while excessive shrinking of the normalization vector leads to a sharp drop in accuracy. The reason is not hard to understand: LN's $\gamma$ and $\beta$ act on features dimension by dimension; DIM directly determines the width of the feature space. Cutting it is equivalent to directly compressing the representation itself, whereas cutting depth or MLP width only cuts the "amount of processing done on the representation."

Results (Table IV): KWT-Tiny has only 1646 parameters (compared to 607k, a reduction of 99.73%), floating-point memory usage is 6.58 kB (compared to 2.42 MB, also a reduction of 99.73%), volume is shrunk 369 times, and accuracy is 87.2%, i.e., paying a cost of about 9.7 percentage points. The paper attributes this to the iterative method correctly identifying the least important layers and reducing them accordingly. It must be clearly stated that KWT-Tiny has only two output classes, and testing uses "dog" and "notdog" classes—this makes it naturally suitable for single wake-word detection scenarios like "Hey Google" or "Alexa," rather than full 35-word class discrimination.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Static Post-Training Quantization and Asymmetric Dual Scale Factors

There are two motivations for quantizing KWT-Tiny: (1) storing weights as INT8 instead of FLOAT32 directly shrinks the model by 4 times; (2) performing floating-point emulation operations on embedded devices without an FPU is very expensive, especially floating-point division. In addition to the INT8 weights and INT16 intermediate residuals of Equation 9, the paper follows the conventions of other Transformer quantization techniques (citing literature) to keep SoftMax and layer normalization calculated in floating-point—because quantizing these operations has been proven to cause significant damage to accuracy. In terms of engineering implementation, this means intermediate results are dequantized before entering SoftMax and LayerNorm, and the outputs of these two layers are re-quantized for the next layer, forming a layer-by-layer exchange rhythm.

What truly has ablation value is the selection of scale factors. If the scale is too large, intermediate results will overflow; if too small, the accuracy of quantized weights will decrease—this is a pair of opposing constraints. The paper's approach is to feed each audio input from the GSC dataset to KWT-Tiny and different versions of KWT-Tiny-Q with various $2^y$ values, comparing accuracy one by one (Table V): weight scale 8 paired with input scale 8 yields 60.3%; 16 paired with 16 yields 71%; 32 paired with 32 yields 77.3%; 64 paired with 32 yields 82.5%; 64 paired with 64 yields 65.2%. The optimal scheme is to use different scale factors for weights and inputs. The paper gives a clear explanation for the reason: weight values range between 0 and 1, while the amplitude of elements in the MFCC input vector can reach hundreds. Therefore, weights can use a much larger scale factor than inputs to improve quantization accuracy without encouraging overflow; conversely, raising the input to 64 hits the overflow wall, and accuracy drops directly from 82.5% to 65.2%. Ultimately, KWT-Tiny-Q consumes only one-quarter (25%) of KWT-Tiny's memory, i.e., 1.646 kB, with an accuracy of 82.5%, losing about 5 percentage points relative to the floating-point version.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Bare-Metal C Transformer Tensor Library and Manual Memory Management

To bypass the dual problems of TFLite/ONNX/TVM-like libraries "being several MB themselves and blocking the bottom layer," the paper built a simple C library for Transformer computation, aiming to maximize customizability and memory optimization space. The library supports both floating-point computation for non-quantized models and INT16 computation for quantized models, and is designed to be compatible with most Transformer-based models (the paper names encoder-decoder, Vision Transformers, and BERT). To cover all operations required for the inference pipeline (Fig 1 and Fig 2), the library implements eight functions (Table VI): computeMeanAndVariance() calculates mean and variance for layer normalization (corresponding to Equation 4); layerNorm() normalizes input vectors element-wise and applies $\beta$ and $\gamma$ (corresponding to Equation 5); matrixMultiply() performs matrix multiplication using the basic $O(n^3)$ algorithm; Softmax() implements Equation 2 using built-in expf() and floating-point division; gelu() implements Equation 7 using built-in erf(), sqrt(), and floating-point division; linear() implements Equation 8 based on matrix multiplication; splitIntoQKV() splits the flattened input into Q, K, and V (corresponding to Equation 3); scaledDotProductAttention() combines the former two to calculate the result of Equation 1. The cost is candidly stated: embedded engineers must be familiar with how to build the inference pipeline themselves using these library functions—but this is still far lighter than developing an entire tensor library from scratch.

Memory management is the most "manual" part of this scheme. In a bare-metal, OS-less environment, the commonly used malloc() is usually unavailable, so the paper manually implemented an alternative: two global arrays of predefined sizes serve as a global memory bank, the size of which is determined by "dry-running" the inference pipeline to ensure the maximum intermediate result can fit into one of the banks. Why two banks are needed: according to the pipeline in Fig 1, there are two residual results that must be present simultaneously and added together, which a single bank cannot satisfy. In the C implementation of KWT-Tiny, the sizes of the two banks are $SEQLEN \times MLP\_DIM$ and $SEQLEN \times DIM\_HEAD \times 3$ (attributes defined in Table III, the latter corresponding exactly to Q, K, and V). The linker is configured according to maximum runtime requirements: 60 kB program memory plus 4 kB stack, and the compiler "-Os" volume optimization flag must be enabled to fit into the Ibex platform—this detail indicates that the floating-point version of KWT-Tiny, along with program logic, has pushed the 64 kB limit to the brink.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: RISC-V Custom Instructions and Lookup Table-based GELU/SoftMax Acceleration

To select which operators to hardware-ize, the paper used data: operator-level profiling of a single inference of the unaccelerated, unquantized KWT-Tiny (Fig 3 gives the entire inference, Fig 4 gives the internal self-attention, Fig 5 gives the internal MLP) revealed that GELU and SoftMax operations are the most time-consuming. In addition to the profiling conclusion, there is a layer of universality consideration: these two operators are shared by many deep neural networks, not just belonging to Transformers, so the investment in hardware acceleration for them can be amortized across a broader family of models.

The implementation carrier is the modularity and scalability of the RISC-V open-source instruction set—the standard reserves coding space for custom instructions, and the toolchain (compiler, linker, assembler) is mature. The processor chosen is lowRISC Ibex: open-source, single-core, written in SystemVerilog, parameterizable, and low-power oriented, conforming to the RV32IMC standard (32-bit RISC-V, supporting basic integer operations, fixed-point multiplication/division, and 16-bit compressed instruction extension), synthesized on an Xilinx Arty-A735T FPGA. In terms of acceleration mechanism, the paper adds an R-type instruction to Ibex's decoding logic (format see Fig 6), occupying the RISC-V reserved custom-1 opcode (value 7'b0101011), with funct7 always 0, and behavior distinguished by funct3 (Table VII): 3'b000 is ALU_EXP using a lookup table to approximate exp(X); 3'b001 is ALU_INVERT using a lookup table to approximate 1/X; 3'b011 is ALU_GELU using a lookup table to approximate GELU(X); 3'b100 is ALU_TO_FIXED converting floating-point to Q8.24 fixed-point representation; 3'b101 is ALU_TO_FLOAT for reverse conversion. Operands uniformly use Q8.24 fixed-point format. The instruction is called directly in assembly in C code via the asm keyword—this choice has clear engineering reasons: there is no need to modify the compiler toolchain to recognize new operations, and the code maintains general compilability.

The acceleration idea for SoftMax is to replace floating-point division with the lookup table of ALU_INVERT. As mentioned earlier, the numerically stable form of Equation 10 constrains the value of $e^{max(x)-x_i}$ between 0 and 10, so exp() can also be approximated by the lookup table of ALU_EXP. The C code loops through vector X, calling ALU_EXP for each element to accumulate the sum, then uses ALU_INVERT(sum) to get the reciprocal, and multiplies to get the final SoftMax result. The granularity trade-off of the lookup table is: 32 split points per unit interval, achieving a fair balance between accuracy and ROM usage. Accordingly, the ALU_EXP and ALU_INV tables are each 320 32-bit numbers, totaling 2.56 kB ROM; the indexing methods for the two tables are organized according to Equation 11 and Equation 12 respectively, where the reciprocal table is $LUT2[(z \times 32) - 1] \approx 1/z$.

The acceleration of GELU (Fig 7) is more elegant and is the paper's self-admitted novelty: a simple piecewise approximation. If $x > 1.595$, then $GELU(x) = x$; if $x < -1.857$, then $GELU(x) \approx 0$; in the transition interval $-1.857 \leq x \leq 1.595$, a 32-element lookup table $LUT3[x] \approx GELU(x)$ is used (Equation 13). The values of the two thresholds are not guessed but are near-optimal choices for a 32-element LUT calculated via gradient descent, with a cited accuracy degradation of only 0.0042%—two orders of magnitude lower than quantization error, practically invisible. With the GELU table added, the total ROM overhead for all LUTs is 2.69 kB.

Hardware synthesis results (Table VIII, Arty-A7-35T): LUT resources increased from the baseline Ibex's 5092 to 7368, DSP from 10 to 16, FF from 5276 to 6074, BRAM remained unchanged at 16. The Overhead column in the table (10.94%, 6.67%, 1.92%) is the incremental proportion relative to the total capacity of the FPGA device, while the "approximately 29% area growth between baseline and modified Ibex" expressed in the paper's text is the overall area expansion of the modified version relative to the baseline—these are two different denominators, and care should be taken when reading the table.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Belonging to the same "RISC-V custom instruction acceleration Transformer" route as RISC-VTF, the difference in this paper is fundamental: RISC-VTF-like works measure acceleration ratios using simulated operators, while this paper keeps a model that is trained, quantized, compiled, and deployed on hardware throughout the entire process, so the accuracy loss corresponding to every bit of acceleration has a number (in this paper, from 82.5% to about 80%). The difference with A³ lies in the object of approximation: A³ uses approximate methods to touch the attention mechanism itself, while this paper does not touch the model structure and attention mathematical form at all, only performing hardware approximation on the two non-linear operators GELU and SoftMax, minimizing accuracy risks at the model level. The difference with neural network processors like DaDianNao and FlexACC lies in the objective function: they optimize peak throughput and energy efficiency, targeting server-level workloads, while this paper optimizes "minimum area cost under microcontroller memory constraints"—the 29% area overhead and 2.69 kB ROM are its scoreboard. The difference with the TFLite deployment ecosystem was described in Section V: abandoning general frameworks, writing a bare-metal C library that can be cropped, exchanging engineering thresholds for memory and optimization space. Compared to the mainstream CNN small model route in the KWS field (such as DS-CNN, etc.), the value of this paper lies in holding onto the Transformer architecture itself, exploring where the boundary is where it "can still be used" after being shrunk—the paper's original words are that as long as the pruned layers are chosen correctly, Transformers can still maintain high accuracy after scaling for embedded applications.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

All experiments use the Google Speech Commands (GSC) dataset. The KWT-1 baseline corresponds to its full classification task of 35 word classes; KWT-Tiny is tested on the full GSC dataset, with two output classes set to "dog" and "notdog." As for the division method of the training set/validation set/test set, training epochs, learning rate, and other training details, the paper does not report; the total number of dataset samples is also not reported by the paper (the common version of GSC is about 100,000, but no number is given in this paper, so it is not cited). The method for searching scale factors is to feed each audio input from GSC to the floating-point KWT-Tiny and various quantized versions with different $2^y$, comparing accuracy one by one (Table V).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric system covers three levels. Model level: parameter count and model volume (floating-point kB / quantized kB), directly corresponding to the first constraint of "whether it can fit into 64 kB RAM." Deployment level: program volume (kB, including -Os optimization) and clock cycles for a single inference—cycles are chosen instead of wall-clock time because cycles are decoupled from clock speed and, under the assumption of fixed energy consumption per cycle, are proportional to energy consumption. The paper infers about 5x power reduction based on the 5x reduction in cycle count. Hardware level: LUT, DSP, FF, BRAM resource usage after FPGA synthesis (Table VIII), measuring the silicon area cost of acceleration. Accuracy level uniformly uses classification accuracy (top-1 accuracy, the GSC convention), with the four models being 96.9%, 87.2%, 82.5%, and about 80% respectively. It needs to be pointed out that the paper does not report measured power consumption or energy efficiency numbers; the power consumption benefit is purely inferred; nor does it report false acceptance rate/false rejection rate or DET-like metrics, which are more critical for wake-word scenarios.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Core comparisons are all conducted within its own four-stage pipeline (Table IX). KWT-1: 607k parameters, 2.42 MB, accuracy 96.9% (program volume and cycle count are not applicable because it cannot fit into the target platform). KWT-Tiny: 1646 parameters, 6.584 kB, program 58.8 kB, inference 26×10^6 clock cycles, accuracy 87.2%. KWT-Tiny-Q: parameters unchanged, model 1.646 kB, program 44.4 kB, cycle count reduced to 13×10^6, accuracy 82.5%. KWT-Tiny-Q with hardware: model 1.646 kB plus 2.69 kB ROM, program 44.6 kB, cycle count reduced to 5.5×10^6, accuracy about 80%.

These four steps are worth breaking down cycle by cycle: from 26M to 13M is the 2x contribution of quantization (INT8/INT16 integer operations replacing soft floating-point), from 13M to 5.5M is the approximately 2.4x contribution of custom instructions (GELU/SoftMax replaced by lookup tables), totaling 26M/5.5M ≈ 4.7x, i.e., the paper's claimed approximately 5x acceleration and the inferred approximately 5x power reduction. The accuracy ladder is also clear: pruning and retraining -9.7 percentage points (96.9→87.2), quantization -4.7 percentage points (87.2→82.5), hardware approximation about -2.5 percentage points (82.5→about 80%), cumulatively about -17 percentage points throughout; the abstract summarizes the pruning phase as "volume shrunk 369 times, accuracy loss about 10%." Cost side: area growth about 29% (Table VIII and text), ROM increased by 2.69 kB, program volume dropped from floating-point version 58.8 kB to quantized version 44.4 kB (increased slightly to 44.6 kB after adding hardware due to added lookup table call logic). Based on the 50 MHz clock speed in Table II, 5.5M cycles is approximately 110 ms for a single inference (this is a calculated value based on table numbers; the paper does not directly report wall-clock time or real-time rate).

Horizontal comparison with external SOTA (such as comparison with other contemporary small models like DS-CNN, BC-ResNet on GSC, or actual measurement comparison with TFLite Micro on the same platform): the paper does not report. The comparison chain is always anchored to this self-evolving lineage of KWT-1.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper has two systematic ablations. The first is the quantization scale factor grid (Table V). The results of the five combinations listed earlier can read three pieces of information: first, symmetric configurations improve monotonically (8/8→16/16→32/32 from 60.3% to 77.3%), indicating that within the non-overflow interval, the finer the quantization granularity, the higher the accuracy; second, upgrading from 32/32 to asymmetric 64/32 increases by another 5.2 percentage points to 82.5%, indicating that at this point, weight quantization accuracy is the main bottleneck, while the input side, because amplitude can reach hundreds, still has overflow margin to exchange; third, 64/64 collapses to 65.2%, verifying that using a large scale on the input side triggers intermediate result overflow—thus establishing the necessity of asymmetric dual scale factors, rather than empirical tuning. The second ablation is implicit in the structural pruning process (Table III): depth 12→1 and MLP 256→24 are the best knife-edge for accuracy-volume trade-off, while excessive shrinking of the normalization vector DIM leads to a sharp drop in accuracy, drawing the boundary between "can be cut" and "cannot be cut." The third micro-ablation is the GELU approximation itself: a 32-element LUT plus two thresholds optimized by gradient descent (1.595 and -1.857), with a cited accuracy degradation of only 0.0042%, indicating that this approximation is completely negligible relative to quantization error; similarly, 32 split points per unit of the LUT is the balance point between accuracy and ROM, with the exponential table and reciprocal table each having 320 items, totaling 2.56 kB, plus the GELU table totaling 2.69 kB. The latter two numbers corroborate the "+2.69 kB ROM" in Table IX.

## Main Contributions

1. Proposed and verified KWT-Tiny: a Transformer keyword detector for embedded dual-class (single wake-word) detection, with 1646 parameters, 6.584 kB floating-point volume, and 87.2% accuracy, 369 times smaller than KWT-1 with an accuracy loss of about 10% (Table IV), and providing the pruning rule that "cutting depth and MLP is safe, cutting normalization vector is dangerous."
2. A static post-training quantization scheme, core to which is the asymmetric power-of-2 scale factor for weights and inputs (64/32 optimal), further compressing the model to 1.646 kB (25% of the floating-point version) with 82.5% accuracy (Table V, Table IX), and explicitly stating the accuracy reasons for keeping SoftMax/LayerNorm in floating-point.
3. A bare-metal C tensor library compatible with various Transformer structures, supporting both floating-point and INT16 (eight functions in Table VI), paired with a manual malloc alternative using dual memory banks, bypassing the non-deployability of MB-level inference frameworks on microcontrollers.
4. A novel GELU hardware acceleration technique: piecewise approximation (thresholds 1.595 / -1.857) plus a 32-element lookup table, with a cited accuracy degradation of only 0.0042% (Fig 7, Equation 13).
5. Five RISC-V custom instructions based on the custom-1 opcode and Q8.24 fixed-point (Table VII), gaining approximately 5x acceleration (26M→5.5M cycles) and inferring approximately 5x power reduction with an area overhead of about 29% and 2.69 kB ROM.
6. Methodological level: providing a full-realistic migration path of "training-quantization-bare-metal deployment-hardware acceleration," making the accuracy and resource cost of each step measurable, directly responding to the gap in existing works of "measuring acceleration with simulation and unable to assess accuracy degradation."

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

The most fundamental limitation lies in the task setting itself: KWT-Tiny has only two output classes, "notdog" mixes all 34 word classes into the negative class, and its accuracy numbers of 87.2%/82.5% are not on the same task difficulty plane as the 96.9% of 35 classes, and cannot be read as a general conclusion that "Transformers only lose 10% accuracy after shrinking." A more practical problem is that single wake-word products are far more sensitive to false wake-up rates and miss rates than top-1 accuracy, and an absolute accuracy of about 80% (after adding hardware) is not enough for a commercial wake-word detector, and the paper does not provide FAR/FRR or DET curves to evaluate this dimension. The limitation on the hardware side is that the effective domain of the lookup table depends on calibration: the boundedness of Equation 10 ($e^{max(x)-x_i}$ falls between 0 and 10) is an empirical fact that holds for this model, this feature, and this data. After changing the model, feature dimensions, or training data, the distribution of exponent parameters may go out of bounds, requiring the LUT to be recalibrated, and the accelerator is not "plug-and-play." On the software side, matrixMultiply explicitly uses the basic $O(n^3)$ algorithm (Table VI), without any loop unrolling, blocking, or multiply-accumulate fusion optimization, leaving considerable room for software compression in cycle count unmined; SoftMax and layer normalization still follow the floating-point path, meaning the soft floating-point library has not been completely cleared. Finally, the power consumption conclusion is inferred from cycle count (5x cycle reduction infers about 5x power reduction), and the paper does not report any measured power consumption or energy efficiency numbers, and the dynamic power consumption characteristics on the FPGA will also differ from the final ASIC tape-out.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

The experimental coverage is narrow: single dataset (GSC), single platform (Ibex on Arty-A7-35T FPGA), single synthesis, without multiple seed repetitions or variance reports. Lack of external baselines: no actual measurement comparison with any contemporary small models (DS-CNN, BC-ResNet, MatchboxNet, etc.) or TFLite Micro on the same platform, making it impossible for readers to judge whether a "1646 parameter Transformer" is superior to mature CNN solutions under the same resource budget. Missing training details: epochs, optimizer, learning rate, and data division are not reported, making it difficult to precisely reproduce the results of KWT-Tiny. The scale factor search only covers five combinations of powers of 2, with coarse granularity, and no comparison with Quantization-Aware Training (QAT), making it impossible to judge how much space remains between PTQ and 82.5%. Robustness under noise and far-field conditions is completely untested, which is exactly the norm for real deployment environments of wake words. Inference latency only gives cycle count, not wall-clock time and real-time rate (calculated as about 110 ms based on 50 MHz, which is the author's calculation based on Table II and Table IX; the original text does not give it).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the paper's own framework, the natural next steps include: expanding the output classes from 2 to multiple wake words and even small-scale vocabularies, and simultaneously introducing false wake-up rate/miss rate evaluation; replacing pure post-training quantization with quantization-aware training, and exploring fixed-point schemes for SoftMax and layer normalization to completely remove soft floating-point dependencies; automating the calibration process for the effective domain of lookup tables to make the accelerator portable to new models. At the library level, matrix multiplication can be replaced with optimized implementations (blocking, unrolling, multiply-accumulate fusion), and the library can be extended to operators such as convolution to cover a broader family of models. At the evaluation level, the most valuable make-up work is actual chip or at least more systematic FPGA power consumption measurement, and direct comparison with CNN-based small models under the same RAM budget—this is the only way to answer the question "on microcontrollers, is a mini Transformer really a good architectural choice?" which this paper started but did not close. Pushing further out, combining it with structural cost-reduction means such as linear attention and low-rank approximation, and making continuous listening a cascaded pipeline of VAD plus KWS to amortize average power consumption, are all directions this route can extend.
