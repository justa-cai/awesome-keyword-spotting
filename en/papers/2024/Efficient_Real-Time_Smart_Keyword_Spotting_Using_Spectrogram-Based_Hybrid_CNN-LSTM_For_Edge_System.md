## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the core voice entry point for devices such as smart speakers, in-car voice systems, and mobile voice assistants. The task is defined as identifying which command word from a predefined vocabulary is spoken, rather than transcribing continuous speech into text. This "classification-only, no transcription" positioning determines that KWS can run on small devices with extremely limited computational resources, marking the most fundamental engineering divergence from Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR).

The paper places the pain point on the deployment paradigm. There are two implementation routes for KWS: cloud computing and edge computing. Cloud computing offloads inference to cloud servers, where edge devices only need to collect and upload data. However, this comes at the cost of relying on stable network connections and server resources, introducing three types of problems: latency, security, and scalability (the paper cites comparative research in reference [30]). For human-computer interactions such as "speaking a command and receiving a device response," network round-trip latency directly degrades the user experience; uploading voice data to the cloud also exposes privacy risks. Therefore, the paper's stance is that edge computing is a more realistic solution—all processing is completed locally on the device offline, without relying on servers or the internet.

However, the edge route shifts all computational pressure back to the device side. The three hard constraints of embedded systems—power consumption, memory, and computing power (citing a survey in reference [5])—determine that KWS models cannot simply copy the complexity of large-scale ASR models. Low-complexity design must be prioritized to support real-time performance. Additionally, the paper points out an often-overlooked engineering fact: mainstream deep learning classifiers first need to offline compute Mel spectrograms from audio before feeding them into the network. This independent feature extraction preprocessing step is "inefficient, time-consuming, and resource-intensive" (the paper's original words), implying additional storage overhead and pipeline fragmentation in real machine real-time systems. Another layer of pain points lies in the evaluation ecosystem: many existing works only measure accuracy offline on public datasets, without running a complete real-time closed loop with real human voice on actual devices. The gap between model accuracy and deployment performance has not been truly bridged.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper reviews existing work from five perspectives (Chapter 2) and points out deficiencies one by one:

- **Capability defects of single architectures**. Pure CNNs are only good at learning spatial structures; classifying speech spectrograms requires stacking more layers to achieve sufficient accuracy (reference [23]); pure RNNs fail to learn local information in spectrograms (reference [22]). The discriminative clues of sound events (such as the transients of consonants and the local shape of formant trajectories) and temporal context (the same word stretched or shortened) require both capabilities. A single architecture either sacrifices depth or sacrifices context.
- **Imbalance between parameter count and accuracy in existing hybrid architectures**. Table 1 summarizes existing CNN-RNN hybrid architectures: CTC methods use 12.21M parameters to achieve 98% but use a custom dataset; hybrid CNN-RNN uses 229K parameters for 97.15%; DenseNet-Bi-LSTM uses 223K parameters for 97.3% (Google Speech Commands v2); Gated Convolutional RNN uses 154.53K parameters for only 90.6%. It can be seen that there is no fully optimized Pareto frontier between parameter count and accuracy; the problems of small models losing accuracy and high-accuracy models not being small enough are prevalent.
- **Separation of feature extraction and model**. The conventional process is to offline compute Mel spectrograms, save them as feature files, and then train and infer the classification network. The paper argues that this is inefficient for real-time systems: every input data requires an independent spectral conversion process, resulting in repeated time and resource overhead, and deployment requires maintaining two sets of software components on the device.
- **Lack of real-device and real-human voice validation**. Most works remain at the dataset testing stage, without running and quantifying the complete chain of "model trained on PC, deployed on embedded GPU, driven by real human voices in real-time, results driving peripheral devices" (inference time, consistency of accuracy before and after deployment).
- **Underutilization of both model compression and model optimization paths**. Data cited by the paper itself (reference [31]) shows: TensorRT FP32 optimization brings a 107.69% throughput increase, FP16 brings a 746.15% increase, and INT8 brings a 1318.15% increase—indicating that precision-level optimization has huge potential on embedded GPUs; meanwhile, TensorRT is faster than TFLite but consumes about 40% more energy, while TFLite saves energy but is about 62% slower (reference [30])—indicating that the choice of optimization framework itself is a triangle trade-off of accuracy-energy-speed, requiring empirical testing on specific platforms rather than theoretical discussion.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the challenge this paper aims to solve is a complete engineering closed loop rather than a single-point algorithmic innovation: Design a low-complexity hybrid CNN-LSTM model (SpectroNet) that (1) embeds Mel spectrogram computation as a custom layer within the network, directly ingesting raw audio waveforms to eliminate the independent spectral conversion process; (2) maintains over 90% classification accuracy with a parameter count far smaller than conventional architectures; (3) is truly deployed on the Jetson Xavier NX edge device, using TensorRT for FP32/FP16 precision-level optimization and quantifying the optimization gains; (4) finally drives the entire system with real human voice (not dataset), outputting classification results via LED indicators on GPIO to verify real-time performance. These four points correspond to the four contributions listed by the paper in the introduction.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The complete layer structure of SpectroNet is shown in the original Table 3: Input layer of 16,000 sampling points (combined with the upper frequency limit of 8000 Hz in Table 4, the sampling rate can be inferred as 16 kHz via the Nyquist relationship, i.e., 1 second of audio; the paper does not explicitly state the sampling rate) → Mel spectrogram layer outputting 118×80×1 (80 Mel filter channels, 118 frames) → Normalization layer → 10 5×1 convolutional kernels (stride 1×1, output 118×80×10) → 2×2 average pooling (stride 2×2, output 59×40×10) → Dense block (60 1×1 convolutions + 15 3×3 convolutions, repeated 2 times, output 59×40×106) → Transition layer (1×1 convolution + 1×2 pooling, output 59×20×10) → Second dense block (output 59×20×106) → Second transition layer (output 59×10×10) → BN-ReLu-Conv layer (1 3×3 convolution, output 59×10×1) → Reshape layer (output 59×10) → LSTM layer (128 units) → Fully connected layer (64 neurons) → Dropout layer (p=0.5) → Softmax layer (10 class outputs).

The design motivation can be decomposed into four levels:

**Why use a hybrid CNN-LSTM**. The reasons given by the paper directly target the shortcomings of the two types of architectures: CNNs only learn spatial data, and classifying speech spectrograms requires deeper networks; RNNs fail to learn local information. SpectroNet uses DenseNet for spatial feature extraction and LSTM for temporal modeling, allowing both to perform their respective duties. The reason for choosing DenseNet for the CNN part is that its forward inter-layer connections (feature reuse) can reduce the required network depth (citing the original DenseNet paper [12]); the reason for choosing LSTM over the original RNN for the RNN part is that LSTM's gating mechanism can combat gradient vanishing (citing [51]).

**Why use only a unidirectional LSTM**. The paper explicitly states that unlike the DenseNet-Bi-LSTM in reference [22], SpectroNet uses only a unidirectional LSTM, but does not provide a reason. Inferring from engineering logic (supplemented by the author, not reported by the paper): Bidirectional LSTM requires future frames to calculate the hidden state of the current frame, which conflicts with the causality requirement of streaming real-time systems ("calculate one frame as it comes"); although the unidirectional structure has half the context, it preserves the feasibility of real-time deployment and saves a set of reverse weight parameters. The comparative data in Table 11 supports this trade-off: C1G2BiLSTM using gated convolutional bidirectional LSTM has 149,898 parameters and 106ms inference time, while SpectroNet has only 89,241 parameters and 61ms.

**Why convolution and pooling are almost only done on the frequency axis**. This is the most notable structural detail in the entire paper. Observing Table 3: the time axis is only reduced from 118 frames to 59 frames by 2×2 pooling early in the network, and the pooling in the two subsequent transition layers is 1×2 (halving only the frequency dimension, keeping the time dimension at 59 unchanged), with no convolutional kernel expansion on the time axis. The paper's own explanation is that "one-dimensional convolution avoids convolution along the time axis, thereby reducing the total number of parameters and avoiding losing any temporal data that can be extracted by LSTM." In other words, the authors delegate the entire responsibility of temporal modeling to LSTM, letting CNN focus on the spectral spatial structure within each frame. This is a design philosophy of responsibility division rather than capability stacking, and it is the structural reason why it can achieve 93.33% test accuracy with 89K parameters (the paper attributes this to "good feature extraction methods").

**Why make the Mel spectrogram an embedded network layer**. The motivation is system-level: conventional classifiers need to run a spectral conversion process independently for each input data, which the paper considers inefficient, time-consuming, and resource-intensive in real-time scenarios; after implementing the Mel spectrogram as a custom layer within the model, audio data can be fed directly into SpectroNet for end-to-end processing, eliminating the need to maintain an independent feature extraction component during deployment.

Internal structure of dense blocks and transition layers (Figure 10, Figure 11): Each dense block consists of a BN-ReLu-Conv sequence, ending with a convolutional layer; each transition layer consists of a BN-ReLu-Conv sequence, ending with an average pooling layer. The paper's explanation of the layer order is: Batch Normalization normalizes each batch of inputs before nonlinear activation, improving training stability and providing regularization; ReLu introduces nonlinearity and reduces the probability of overfitting; the ending convolution and average pooling are responsible for further feature extraction and dimensionality reduction. It should be noted that Table 3 only gives "60(1×1), 15(3×3), repeated 2 times" and output channels (10→106); the exact arithmetic of channel concatenation (106 = 10 + 96) cannot be deduced from the table, and the paper does not report the complete details of channel evolution at each step.

Hardware and system side (Table 5-8, Figure 12): Training uses a PC (Intel i7-9750H 2.60GHz, 8GB memory, GTX 1650 4GB VRAM, Python 3.9.13, TensorFlow 2.12.0); deployment uses Jetson Xavier NX (6-core NVIDIA Carmel ARMv8.2 64-bit CPU, 8GB 128-bit LPDDR4x memory with 59.7GB/s bandwidth, 384-core Volta GPU with 48 Tensor Cores, Jetpack 5.1.2, Python 3.9.0, TensorFlow 2.12.0). The reason for choosing Jetson Xavier NX is cited from [41]: among the Jetson family, it has the best FPS/Watt (Nano has the best FPS/dollar, Xavier AGX has the best absolute FPS), meaning the authors take "energy efficiency" as the primary standard for edge selection. The entire system consists of Jetson Xavier NX, USB sound card connected to a microphone, VGA external monitor, and 10 LEDs connected to GPIO, with LEDs directly indicating the classification results of the 10 command words.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

Chapter 3 of the paper walks through the complete mathematical preprocessing of signal processing and the network using a definition-lemma format. Here, the main trunk is extracted.

**Generation chain of Mel spectrogram** (Definition 3.1-3.3, Lemma 3.1-3.4): The continuous form of STFT is $X(t,\omega)=\int x(\tau)\cdot w(\tau-t)\cdot e^{-j\omega\tau}d\tau$, which means intercepting the signal with a window function centered at $t$ and then performing a Fourier transform, with the purpose of obtaining the frequency information of each frame. The discrete implementation first performs frame segmentation and windowing: $y(m,n)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n+mH)\times w(n)$, where $H$ is the hop length and $m$ is the frame index; then performs discrete STFT for each frame: $S(m,k)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n+mH)\times w(n)\times e^{(-i2\pi nk/N)}$; the power spectrum is $S_p(m,k)=S(m,k)\times \overline{S(m,k)}$ (multiplied by the conjugate). The window function used in the paper's main text is the Hamming window $w(n)=0.56+0.48\cos(2\pi n/M)$ (but Table 4 in the experimental configuration writes Hanning window—this is an inconsistency within the paper, see the Limitations section). The conversion from Hertz to Mel scale is $Mel(f)=1127\times\ln(1+f/700)$, and the reason for choosing the Mel scale is that it is a logarithmic scale, close to human auditory frequency perception. The triangular Mel filter bank is defined by piecewise linear functions (Lemma 3.4), and after filtering taking the dot product, a logarithmic scaling is performed again, completing the Mel spectrogram. The parameters actually used by SpectroNet (Table 4): window length 512, hop 128, 80 Mel filters, frequency range 0-8000 Hz. Window length 512 and hop 128 mean that adjacent windows overlap by 75%, which is a common high-overlap configuration in speech features; however, calculating the number of frames based on 16000 sampling points, 512 window length, and 128 hop should be approximately 122-125 frames, which does not match the 118 frames in Table 3, and the paper does not report the derivation of this number.

**Mathematics of the CNN part** (Lemma 3.5, Definition 3.4, Lemma 3.6): Convolution $S(i,j)=\sum\sum I(i+m,j+n)\times K(m,n)$, adjustable parameters are kernel size and stride; max pooling $S(i,j)=\max A$, average pooling $S(i,j)=\frac{\sum\sum I(i+m,j+n)}{m\times n}$, which replaces pixel values with neighborhood statistics.

**Mathematics of the LSTM part** (Lemma 3.8-3.12): Forget gate $f_t=\sigma(W_{fi}X_t+W_{fh}H_{t-1}+B_f)$, input gate $i_t=\sigma(W_{ii}X_t+W_{ih}H_{t-1}+B_i)$, output gate $o_t=\sigma(W_{oi}X_t+W_{oh}H_{t-1}+B_o)$, cell state update $C_t=f_t\times C_{t-1}+i_t\times\tanh(W_{ci}X_t+W_{ch}H_{t-1}+B_c)$, hidden state update $H_t=o_t\times\tanh(C_t)$. The paper emphasizes that the advantage of LSTM over the original RNN comes from the introduction of the forget gate and output gate and the modified input gate: the forget gate determines which states are discarded, the cell state serves as long-term memory and avoids gradient vanishing through additive updates (rather than multiplicative), and the output gate controls how much updated information is exposed at each time step.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Embedded Mel Spectrogram Layer (Eliminating Independent Feature Extraction Process)

This is the most core contribution of the paper at the system level. The approach is to implement the entire set of "preprocessing → frame segmentation and windowing → STFT → power spectrum → Mel filtering → logarithmic scaling" from Chapter 3 as a custom layer in TensorFlow, directly attached to the network input (Layer 2 in Table 3), making the first layer input of SpectroNet the 16,000-point raw waveform. The engineering benefits are threefold: First, the deployment pipeline is simplified from "recording → offline feature script → network" to "recording → network", saving one component and one data disk write and transfer; Second, the feature calculation paths for training and inference are completely homogeneous, avoiding implicit accuracy loss caused by inconsistencies between offline feature scripts and online implementations; Third, the end-to-end single model is convenient for direct handover to deployment optimizers like TensorRT for overall conversion.

This design also has a cost that the paper does not expand on (author's observation): Table 12 shows that TensorRT only converted 52.57% (FP32)/53.97% (FP16) of the operations in SpectroNet, exactly about half of the operations could not enter the optimization engine. The most reasonable explanation is that the custom Mel spectrogram layer and some reshape/LSTM operations do not belong to the operator set natively supported by TensorRT, and can only fall back to unoptimized execution paths. This directly explains why the actual acceleration ratio measured by the paper is only 10%-14.75%, far lower than the throughput increase of 746.15% for FP16 reported in the cited reference [31]—while the embedded layer brings pipeline simplicity, it also blocks nearly half of the calculations from the optimizer. This is a trade-off clearly visible in the data but not reported by the paper.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Frequency-Axis-First Compact DenseNet-LSTM Responsibility Division

The second innovation is at the structural level: "protecting" the time axis, letting CNN go deep only in the frequency-channel space, and leaving the time dimension to LSTM. This is specifically reflected in three design choices in Table 3: the 5×1 convolutional kernel at the entrance only spans 5 frames in the time direction and looks at only 1 Mel channel in the frequency direction, performing a lightweight time smoothing; the pooling in the two transition layers is 1×2 (only cutting the frequency dimension), and the time dimension remains at 59 frames all the way until entering the LSTM; finally, a 3×3 convolution compresses the 10 channels to 1 and reshapes it into a 59×10 sequence, which is scanned frame by frame by the 128-unit LSTM. The dense connections of DenseNet are responsible for allowing shallow networks to achieve deep feature expression capabilities (feature reuse), which is one of the sources of parameter efficiency. Superimposed with Dropout p=0.5 regularization, the total number of trainable parameters of the entire network is stated in the main text as 142,877 (Table 11 records it as 89,241, an inconsistency, see Limitations).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: TensorRT Precision-Level Deployment Optimization and Real-Machine Measurement Closed Loop

The third innovation belongs to deployment engineering: rebuilding the engine for the trained SpectroNet on Jetson Xavier NX using TensorRT at FP32 and FP16 precisions, quantifying changes in three dimensions—operation conversion rate (Table 12: FP32 converts 193/369 i.e., 52.57%, FP16 converts 197/365 i.e., 53.97%, and the total number of operations drops from 369 to 365 under FP16 mode), engine build time (FP32 258.763 seconds, FP16 430.232 seconds, FP16 takes about 66.29% more build time than FP32), inference time and accuracy (Table 13: Unoptimized 61ms/93.33%, FP32 optimized 54.9ms/93%, FP16 optimized 52.0ms/93%). Finally, a closed-loop verification is performed using real human voice: 10 words, 10 tests per word, totaling 100 real human pronunciations, with classification results demonstrated by lighting up LEDs on GPIO, and a demonstration video is provided (https://youtu.be/1jDInCVdyvU). This way of reporting "precision level-build time-inference speed" side by side puts the real cost of FP16 (builds 66% slower) and benefits (inference 14.75% faster) on the table, which is a rare honest writing style in deployment papers.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Differences with existing work can be viewed from four lines. The first is the most direct reference system, DenseNet-Bi-LSTM (reference [22], 223K parameters, 97.3%): SpectroNet inherits the hybrid idea of "DenseNet for CNN, LSTM for RNN" and the concept of 1D convolution within DenseNet, but replaces bidirectional LSTM with unidirectional (preserving streaming causality), compresses parameters to 89,241 (Table 11), and embeds the Mel spectrogram layer. The second is with the four models compared in the same field (Table 11): AlexNet is the author team's previous spectrogram CNN (58,304,589 parameters, 93.71%, 120ms), SBNet is an environmental sound classification network (874,266 parameters, 92.32%, 60ms), DSCNN is a depthwise separable convolution network (36,320 parameters, 85.40%, 30ms), C1G2BiLSTM is a gated convolutional bidirectional LSTM (149,898 parameters, 89.69%, 106ms)—SpectroNet occupies the balance position of "second highest accuracy (93.33%), second fewest parameters, second fastest speed" in this table. The third is the choice of technical route: the paper comprehensively reviews compression methods such as pruning (theoretically compressible by more than 10 times) and knowledge distillation (two recent works on noise-robust KWS) in Chapter 2, but ultimately does not adopt any architecture compression, instead taking the combination route of "small architecture design + TensorRT deployment optimization". The difference is that the former changes the model structure, while the latter does not change the model but only the inference engine. The fourth is the evaluation method: unlike most works that only report accuracy on datasets, the final evaluation of this paper is real-time inference of real human voice on the device, and the abstract specifically emphasizes zero accuracy loss between PC and Jetson implementations—treating "deployment consistency" itself as a verified metric.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Training, validation, and testing all use the Google Speech Commands dataset (Chapter 5 explicitly writes v2 subset; dataset background is in Table 2, GSC vocabulary is recorded as 24 command words in the table). 10 words are selected from the vocabulary: go, on, off, yes, no, up, down, left, right, stop, totaling 38,546 speech data, divided into training/validation/test sets at 80%/10%/10% (i.e., approximately 30,837 training, 3,855 validation, 3,855 test, evenly distributed to 10 classes with approximately 3,800 per class). The reason for choosing GSC is reasonable: it is the standard public benchmark for limited-vocabulary command word recognition, completely aligned with the KWS task form; the 10-word subset covers a batch of the most commonly used short commands in the original vocabulary. It should be noted that the paper does not use the unknown/silence background classes in the GSC protocol; the impact of this on result comparability is seen in the Limitations section.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper defines a set of metrics for the two stages of training and deployment. Model design stage (Table 9, Table 10): Maximum 100 epochs, initial learning rate 0.001, batch size 64, Adam optimizer, sparse categorical cross-entropy loss (the reason for choosing it is that class labels are encoded as integers rather than one-hot, matching the loss function form), validation patience 10 epochs (early stopping to prevent overfitting); observed metrics are training/validation loss, training/validation accuracy, overfitting percentage (defined as training accuracy minus validation accuracy, e.g., SpectroNet 98.05% − 95.21% = 2.84%), and number of epochs required for convergence. Deployment stage (Table 11-13): test accuracy, single-step inference time (milliseconds), total parameter count, TensorRT operation conversion rate and number, engine build time. This metric system corresponds to the "accuracy-speed-efficiency" triangle of edge KWS: accuracy manages "accuracy", inference time manages "speed", and parameter count and conversion rate manage "efficiency". Missing key metrics: FLOPs/MACs, model disk size, running memory occupancy, power consumption, false alarm rate/rejection rate (the most critical false positive/false negative metrics in KWS engineering) are not reported by the paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Training and Validation Stage (Table 10, Figures 13-16)**. Five models were trained under identical hyperparameters (Table 9): AlexNet converged in 22 epochs, training accuracy 96.21%, validation accuracy 93.92%, overfitting 2.29%; SBNet 27 epochs, 99.56%/92.81%, overfitting 6.75%; DSCNN 30 epochs, 94.09%/86.61%, overfitting 7.68%; C1G2BiLSTM 36 epochs, 96.00%/90.65%, overfitting 5.35%; SpectroNet according to the table is 30 epochs, training loss 0.1360, training accuracy 98.05%, validation loss 0.2325, validation accuracy 95.21%, overfitting 2.84%. The paper's main text states that SpectroNet's validation accuracy of 95.21% is the highest among the five, and overfitting of 2.84% is the second lowest (only higher than AlexNet's 2.29%); on the convergence curves (Figures 13-16), SpectroNet's training loss/accuracy reaches good values fastest, while C1G2BiLSTM is the slowest. The paper also conducted a small experiment: reducing the initial learning rate to 0.0001 would significantly prolong the training time of SpectroNet and AlexNet without significant performance improvement, thereby demonstrating the rationality of choosing 0.001.

**Testing and Real-Machine Inference Stage (Table 11, testing executed on Jetson Xavier NX)**. SpectroNet: 93.33%, 89,241 parameters, 61ms; AlexNet: 93.71%, 58,304,589 parameters, 120ms; SBNet: 92.32%, 874,266 parameters, 60ms; DSCNN: 85.40%, 36,320 parameters, 30ms; C1G2BiLSTM: 89.69%, 149,898 parameters, 106ms. The paper's conclusion chain is: AlexNet is the best overall (highest accuracy, fastest convergence, least overfitting) but nearly 60 million parameters plus the highest latency make it unsuitable for real-time KWS; DSCNN is the lightest and fastest but 85.40% test accuracy is unreliable; SpectroNet is the balance point—the main text states "reaches comparable accuracy with 99.847% fewer parameters than AlexNet, while latency is 28.57% less". It should be noted that there are two data caliber issues (not explained by the paper): First, 61ms cannot calculate the 28.57% number relative to any model's latency in the table (relative to AlexNet should be 49.2% less, relative to C1G2BiLSTM should be 42.5% less), the reference baseline for this percentage is unclear; Second, the conclusion chapter's claim of "about 70% fewer trainable parameters than other common deep learning architectures" cannot be uniformly established from Table 11—relative to C1G2BiLSTM it is only about 40% less, relative to SBNet it is about 90% less, and relative to DSCNN it is actually about 1.46 times more. For close reading, the original numbers in Table 11 are more credible than these percentage conclusions.

**TensorRT Optimization Stage (Table 12, Table 13)**. Operation conversion: FP32 mode converted 193/369 operations (52.57%), FP16 mode converted 197/365 (53.97%), and the total number of operations dropped from 369 to 365 under FP16. Build time: FP32 258.763 seconds, FP16 430.232 seconds (66.29% more). Optimized effects (Table 13): Unoptimized 93.33%/61ms → FP32 93%/54.9ms (10% speedup) → FP16 93%/52.0ms (14.75% speedup), accuracy loss 0.33%, which the paper judges to be negligible relative to performance gains. Real human voice testing was conducted at this stage: 10 times per word, totaling 100 real human pronunciations. The conclusion chapter also gives a horizontal statement: compared to networks with other software implementations, the FP16-optimized SpectroNet inference time is 40%-50% less.

**Summary-Text Number Conflicts That Must Be Recorded**: The optimization numbers given in the abstract are "inference time up to 180 ms/step; FP32 reduces total operations by 59.35%, FP16 reduces by 59.63%; FP32 speeds up by 45%, FP16 speeds up by 62%; accuracy drops FP32 2.68%, FP16 4.84%"—which is obviously inconsistent with the 61ms, 52.57%/53.97%, 10%/14.75%, 0.33% in the main text Table 12/13. The two sets of numbers may point to different experimental rounds or the author's early version (the team's 2023 MWSCAS conference version of SpectroNet), and the paper provides no explanation. When citing for close reading, the main text tables should be taken as the standard.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Strict component-level ablation experiments (e.g., removing LSTM to leave only DenseNet, removing the embedded Mel layer to use offline features, reducing two dense blocks to one, replacing 1×2 pooling with 2×2) are not reported by the paper. The two types of approximate ablation evidence actually provided by the paper are: First, precision mode ablation (Table 12/13), the difference between FP32 and FP16 levels is only inference speed (54.9ms vs 52.0ms) and build time (258.763s vs 430.232s), with accuracy both at 93%, indicating that FP16 relative to FP32 on this network has no additional accuracy cost and is a "free" speed level; Second, learning rate ablation (discussion in Figures 15/16), 0.0001 relative to 0.001 significantly prolongs training time without significant gains. In addition, the five-model horizontal comparison in Table 11 can be seen as a weak substitute for "architecture route ablation"—it compares different designs rather than component deletion of the same design, and cannot answer attribution questions such as "how many points does LSTM actually contribute" or "how much difference does the embedded Mel layer make relative to offline features". This is the biggest gap in the paper's experimental design: the paper repeatedly advocates the necessity of hybrid architecture and embedded layers (CNN only learns spatial, RNN lacks local information, embedded layer saves process), but does not verify these claims with any set of controlled experiments.

## Main Contributions

According to the four points listed by the paper (end of introduction) plus the author's comprehensive judgment:

1. **Hybrid CNN-LSTM architecture with embedded Mel spectrogram layer**: The model directly accepts raw speech input, without the need for an independent spectral conversion process. This turns "feature extraction" from a system component into part of the model, simplifying the real-time deployment chain, which is the clearest system contribution of this work.
2. **Balance point of high accuracy under low parameter count**: 89,241 parameters (Table 11) achieve 93.33% test accuracy and 61ms inference, occupying the second highest accuracy, second fewest parameters, and second fastest speed in the comparison field of Table 11, verifying the parameter efficiency of the responsibility division route of "frequency-axis convolution + time-axis left to LSTM + DenseNet feature reuse".
3. **Complete quantification of edge deployment optimization**: Using TensorRT on Jetson Xavier NX to complete the quantification of conversion rate, build time, inference speed, and accuracy loss for dual precision levels of FP32/FP16 (Table 12/13), providing a complete cost list for the FP16 level "14.75% speedup, only 0.33% accuracy drop, 66.29% more build time".
4. **Real-time closed-loop verification with real human voice on real machines**: Distinguishing from pure dataset evaluation, the system collects real human voice via USB microphone, indicates classification results via LEDs, tests 100 real human pronunciations, and confirms zero accuracy loss between PC and Jetson deployment (abstract), with demonstration video publicly available.

From a broader perspective, the value of this paper does not lie in the height of a single technology (embedded spectrum layer, hybrid architecture, TensorRT are all combinations of existing elements), but in running the entire chain of "model design-data-hardware selection-inference optimization-real machine verification" in one paper and providing full-chain numbers, belonging to typical engineering integration contributions, suitable as a reference template for edge KWS deployment processes.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **The "edge" authenticity issue**: Although Jetson Xavier NX is an embedded module, it is a 10-20W GPU platform, which differs by two to three orders of magnitude in power budget from true wake-word scenarios (wireless earphones, MCU-level milliwatt constant listening). 61ms (52ms after optimization) for 1-second window classification is real-time, but the streaming detection and VAD/endpoint detection mechanisms required for "constant listening" are completely absent in the paper's design—the system appears to be a fixed-window trigger classification, not a continuous streaming KWS.
- **Closed-set classification, no rejection capability**: 10-class closed-set, without unknown/silence background classes, any non-command voice will be forcibly assigned to one of the 10 classes. The most critical false alarm rate/rejection rate metrics for real KWS are not reported by the paper, and 93.33% closed-set accuracy cannot be extrapolated to open-set availability.
- **TensorRT gains locked by custom layer**: Only about 53% of operations are converted (Table 12), inference speedup stops at 10%-14.75%, far lower than the multi-fold throughput increase reported in reference [31] for FP16. This contradiction between the pipeline simplicity brought by the embedded Mel layer and the optimization coverage is not analyzed by the paper, nor is a solution given (such as splitting the Mel layer out of the engine for separate optimization).
- **Sequential nature of LSTM**: Step-by-step scanning of 59 time steps is difficult to parallelize on GPU, although it is not a bottleneck on this Volta GPU, the route itself lacks scalability compared to subsequent mainstream fully convolutional/pure attention KWS architectures.
- **Lack of structural details**: 118 frames cannot be aligned with 512/128 parameter derivation; the concatenation arithmetic of dense block channels from 10 to 106 is irreproducible; the notation of transition layer "15(1×1) convolution outputs 10 channels" is self-contradictory—these missing details directly affect reproducibility.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Large-scale conflicts between abstract and text numbers** (must be wary of in close reading): The abstract's 180ms, 59.35%/59.63% operation reduction, 45%/62% speedup, 2.68%/4.84% accuracy loss, completely do not match the 61ms, 52.57%/53.97%, 10%/14.75%, 0.33% in the main text Table 12/13, and the paper provides no explanation.
- **Multiple internal inconsistencies**: Parameter count main text 142,877 vs Table 11's 89,241; convergence epochs main text says SpectroNet 27 (second fewest) vs Table 10's 30 (SBNet is actually 27); Chapter 3 main text says Hamming window is used, Table 4 writes Hanning; main text says PC Windows 10 supports TensorFlow up to 2.10.0, Table 7 records 2.12.0; layer structure description says 3 dense blocks (paragraph at the beginning of Chapter IV), Table 3 and Figure 10 are both 2; OS field in Table 7 is filled with CPU model. These indicate that this author version has not been sufficiently edited, and any number cited should be cross-checked.
- **Comparison baselines are outdated and not SOTA**: The newest in the control group is DSCNN and C1G2BiLSTM from 2019, without comparing with KWS-specific SOTA such as MatchboxNet, BC-ResNet after 2020; it also does not align with the GSC standard 35-class protocol, and 93.33% 10-class closed-set accuracy cannot be directly compared with accuracy in the 35-class protocol in the literature.
- **Real human test scale is too small and conditions are not described**: 100 pronunciations, number of speakers, and quiet/noise environment are not reported by the paper, with no statistical significance; noise robustness is completely untested (which is exactly the problem the paper admits in its future work needs to be solved).
- **Missing key engineering metrics**: FLOPs, model size, memory occupancy, power consumption/energy efficiency are not reported by the paper, while energy efficiency is exactly the motive claimed when selecting Jetson Xavier NX (best FPS/Watt); conclusion percentages such as "28.57% less latency" and "about 70% fewer parameters" do not match table numbers or have unclear calibers.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Directions given by the paper itself (conclusion chapter): Landing for specific topics (smart home control, music players, etc.), at which time it is necessary to solve the problems of self-built datasets and noise robustness; and porting to other platforms such as FPGA and IC—combined with the team's background (IMEC Microelectronics Center, IC Design Laboratory), dedicated hardware acceleration is the clear next step.

The author's supplementary judgment: First, supplement component-level ablation (removing LSTM, replacing embedded layer with offline features, scanning dense block quantity) to turn the necessity of hybrid architecture from a claim into evidence; Second, try INT8 quantization—the paper itself cites data that INT8 has a 1318.15% throughput increase (reference [31]) but only achieves FP16; the cost of quantization calibration and accuracy loss are worth supplementary testing; Third, split the Mel layer out of the TensorRT engine or rewrite it as a convertible operator, releasing the optimization space for the remaining about 47% of operations locked; Fourth, add unknown/silence classes and false alarm/rejection metrics, moving closer to real KWS protocols; Fifth, increase real human tests under far-field/noise/multi-speaker conditions, and measure actual power consumption, completing the complete energy efficiency data for the claim of "edge real-time"; Sixth, explore streaming windows and endpoint detection mechanisms, upgrading from fixed-window classification to constant listening systems.
