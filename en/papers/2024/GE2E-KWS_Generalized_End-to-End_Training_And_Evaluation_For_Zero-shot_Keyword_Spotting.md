- **Date**: October 2024 (arXiv:2410.16647v1, submitted October 22, 2024)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16647
- **Keywords**: zero-shot keyword spotting, custom keyword enrollment, generalized end-to-end loss, conformer, speech embedding, triplet loss, on-device quantization

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The goal of traditional Keyword Spotting (KWS) is to detect a small, **pre-defined** set of keywords in streaming audio, such as device wake-up phrases like "Hey Google" or control commands like "stop" and "turn on lights." Research focus has consistently been on small-footprint models capable of running continuously on edge devices (citing works such as Chen 2014, Alvarez 2019, Zhu 2023). This technical path carries an implicit premise: the keyword set must be determined at the time of model training; adding new keywords requires retraining or at least incremental training.

However, as devices become more intelligent and personalized, new demand tensions have emerged: users want to **specify their own trigger words**, either by registering audio (saying them a few times) or by specifying them directly via text. This is the problem that Custom Keyword Spotting (custom KWS) aims to solve—allowing users to activate devices or software in the most natural way, without having to accommodate vendor-prescribed wake words. This demand has a significant impact on product experience: wake words are the highest-frequency voice interaction entry point between users and devices, and users have strong personal preferences for "what name to call."

To support arbitrary new keywords without retraining the model (i.e., open vocabulary, zero-shot capability), the mainstream approach is to reformulate the problem from "classification" to "metric comparison": (1) represent a segment of speech using a speech embedding; (2) compare the cosine similarity between the embedding generated at enrollment and the embedding generated from streaming audio windows during inference; if the similarity exceeds a threshold, a hit is declared. The enrollment phrase can be either audio or text—when text enrollment is used, audio is first synthesized using a TTS model to extract embeddings; if there are multiple enrollment embeddings, their centroid is taken to calculate the cosine distance (Section 1). The elegance of this framework lies in the fact that the model only needs to learn "to map speech of the same phrase to nearby positions in the embedding space"; new keywords require no gradient updates.

However, this path hits a hard wall: **the contradiction between accuracy and size**. The models with the best embedding quality are large-scale, big-data trained ASR encoders, such as the 7.5GB ASR encoder used as a baseline in this paper—but a 7.5GB model simply cannot run continuously on edge devices. KWS is an all-day background task; the model must perform continuous streaming inference within a milliwatt-level power budget, requiring the quantized model size to be compressed to the hundreds of KB range. How to make a 419KB edge model defeat a 7.5GB server-grade model on the "enrollment-comparison" task is the objective function for all experimental designs in this paper.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper systematically reviews three categories of existing solutions in Section 2, each with shortcomings pointing to a specific dimension:

- **ASR Encoder Route**: Directly using an ASR encoder to output vector representations for audio windows (Prabhavalkar et al. [8]). ASR encoders have a context length of up to 2.56 seconds, sufficient to cover the entire audio sequence of a keyword without temporal pooling; because they are trained with massive data and large models, they perform well on keyword matching tasks. The fatal problem is **size**: a 7.5GB model can only run on servers, completely conflicting with the deployment constraint of "continuous edge-side operation."
- **Speech Classification Encoder Route**: Extracting speech embeddings from hidden layers of classification tasks using transfer learning. A representative work is Lin et al. [9]: a five-layer convolutional encoder followed by 125 independent classifiers, where classifier parameters share the encoder during training; training data consists of 200 million 2-second short clips scraped from YouTube, with each classifier performing 40-class classification; the output layer of the shared encoder is released as a speech embedding (1.4MB, Kaggle [24]). This route is edge-friendly in terms of model size, but has two deep flaws: first, **misalignment between training objective and serving objective**—classification loss optimizes for "separating classes within the vocabulary," not for "making any two utterances of the same phrase close"; second, **misalignment in evaluation protocol**—Rybakov et al. [12] evaluated mainstream architectures like DNN, SVDF, LSTM, CRNN, MHAtt on speech command datasets using **classification accuracy**, but classification accuracy does not directly measure "the matching quality between two independent utterances (test utterance vs. enrollment utterance)." The paper's experiments confirm this: this 1.4MB classification encoder performs poorly in direct matching evaluation, with an aggregated AUC of only 6.44% (Table 1), the worst among all compared models.
- **Triplet Loss Route**: Triplet loss gained fame in face recognition (FaceNet [14]) and was subsequently migrated to the speech domain for speaker diarization (Bredin [15], Song et al. [16]) and speaker verification (Zhang & Koshida [17]). Recently, Sacchi et al. [18] and Chidhambararajan et al. [19] applied it to open-vocabulary KWS, building embeddings for text and audio separately. However, the paper points out that their sampling method is inefficient: **each batch samples only one positive pair and one negative pair around a single anchor utterance**. This brings two problems: first, the training signal is sparse, slowing down training; second, the selection of a single anchor utterance introduces high variance, leading to unstable convergence. Furthermore, the experiments in these prior works were only based on a two-layer GRU architecture and were not validated on stronger modern architectures.

Beyond methodological aspects, the paper points out a more fundamental gap: **a lack of systematic and reliable evaluation methods for custom KWS within the field**. The triplet paper [18] uses the relatively outdated WSJ dataset [23] collected in 1992; Chidhambararajan's one-shot work [19] reports **single-threshold** metrics on a TTS synthetic dataset and a small-scale collected set with only 4 keywords. These evaluations are neither close to the real user enrollment-detection journey nor capable of supporting reliable horizontal comparisons between models.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

In summary, this paper aims to solve three coupled problems simultaneously:

1. **Alignment of Training Objective and Serving Process**: Design a loss function that simulates the real-time "enrollment + verification" two-stage process during training, while organizing computation using matrix operations to ensure training efficiency and using multi-utterance centroids to reduce sampling variance to ensure stable convergence—rather than looking at only one anchor's two comparison pairs per batch like triplet loss.
2. **Extreme Trade-off Between Accuracy and Size**: The model must be compressed to sub-MB level (final 419KB) after 8-bit quantization, natively support streaming inference, run continuously on edge devices for long periods, and require zero retraining for new keywords; under these constraints, the phrase average matching accuracy must still surpass that of the 7.5GB ASR encoder.
3. **Establishing a Credible Evaluation Benchmark**: Design an end-to-end evaluation process on public datasets that directly measures the matching quality of "enrollment embedding vs. test speech" (rather than classification accuracy), covering both clean and noisy conditions, and covering words never seen during training to verify zero-shot properties, allowing subsequent KWS research to reuse it directly.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall pipeline of GE2E-KWS is: 40-dimensional spectral features with a 25ms frame length are input into the encoder (Section 4.1), and the encoder outputs the sentence-level speech embedding; during serving, cosine similarity is calculated between the enrollment embedding (centroid of multiple utterances) and the test embedding, with threshold-based decision. The encoder is a replaceable backbone; the paper experimented with two types:

- **LSTM**: A standard three-layer LSTM, trading off performance and size by adjusting hidden and output layer dimensions (Section 3.2.1). The motivation for choosing it is straightforward—RNNs are naturally streaming, state recursion suits audio sequences, and it is a classic choice for edge KWS, serving as a representative of "traditional streaming architectures."
- **Conformer**: The ASR encoder architecture proposed by Gulati et al. [21], combining the ability of Transformers to capture global interactions in audio sequences with the ability of CNNs to efficiently utilize local features, it is the SOTA for ASR encoders. The paper follows the standard structure of the original paper, adjusting the number of Conformer blocks, Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) heads, and output embedding dimensions for size scanning (Section 3.2.2). The deep motivation for choosing it is: custom KWS is essentially a sequence representation learning problem; the architectural dividends validated in the ASR domain should be transferable; and the convolutional component's sensitivity to local acoustic patterns was later proven to be the key explanation for better performance under noisy conditions (Section 5.2).

The training framework is based on TensorFlow/Lingvo [27], because it supports **automatic streaming inference conversion** (Section 4.1)—the model is trained in parallel on full sentences during training and automatically converted to streaming inference form upon export, which is the engineering basis for the "natively streaming" claim. On the deployment side, TensorFlow Lite dynamic range quantization is used, dynamically quantizing weights and activations to 8-bit precision based on their value ranges (Section 3.3), compressing the original 2.8MB Conformer to 419KB.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The GE2E (generalized end-to-end) loss is borrowed from the speaker verification domain (Wan et al. [22]). Its core idea is to divide all utterances in a batch into two groups: enrollment utterances and test utterances, construct an enrollment centroid for each phrase from all its enrollment utterances, and then compare the similarity between the centroid and all test utterance embeddings in the batch.

Formal definition (Section 3.1): A batch contains X different phrases Prᵢ (i = 1..X), each phrase has Y utterances (Y is even), coming from different speakers and different speaking styles—this constraint is crucial, forcing the embedding to be discriminative for "phrase content" rather than "speaker timbre." Let eᵢⱼ be the embedding of the j-th utterance of the i-th phrase. Each phrase uses half (Y/2 utterances) for enrollment and the other half for testing. Specifically, utterances with odd indices (j mod 2 ≠ 0) enter the enrollment set, and their centroid is:

$$c_i = \frac{1}{Y/2} \sum_{\substack{j=1 \\ j \bmod 2 \neq 0}}^{Y} e_{ij} \quad (Eq. 1)$$

Utterances with even indices (j mod 2 ≠ 1) form the **positive sample set** for that phrase:

$$p_i = \{ e_{ij} \mid j \bmod 2 \neq 1 \} \quad (Eq. 2)$$

And the **negative sample set** is taken from the test utterances of all other phrases:

$$n_i = \{ e_{kj} \mid j \bmod 2 \neq 1,\ k = 1..X,\ k \neq i \} \quad (Eq. 3)$$

For each centroid cᵢ, the loss is calculated using cosine similarity: maximizing the similarity between the centroid and its positive samples, and minimizing the similarity between the centroid and its negative samples:

$$L(c_i) = \log \sum_{n \in n_i} \exp \cos(c_i, n) - \log \sum_{p \in p_i} \exp \cos(c_i, p) \quad (Eq. 4)$$

Reading this loss form: the first term is the log-sum-exp (smooth maximum) of similarities for all negative samples; minimizing it lowers the similarity between the centroid and each negative sample overall; the second term is the log-sum-exp of similarities for positive samples; since it is a subtracted term, minimizing the loss pushes up the similarity between the centroid and each positive sample. Together, the two terms are equivalent to metric learning that "pulls similar classes together and pushes dissimilar classes apart" in the embedding space, but note that there are no temperature or learnable scaling/bias parameters in Eq. (4) (the paper does not report whether similarity scaling was additionally tuned), which is simpler than the parameterized similarity matrix form in the original speaker GE2E paper. Figure 1(a) gives a minimal example with X=3, Y=4 (2 enrollment + 2 test), showing the entire process of calculating L(c₁) for phrase Pr₁: the encoder first extracts enrollment embeddings e₁(enroll) and test embeddings eᵢ(test) for each phrase, calculates centroid c₁, then calculates cosine similarity with test embeddings of 3 phrases one by one, and substitutes into Eq. (4).

The paper explicitly summarizes two major advantages of GE2E (Section 3.1): first, reducing sampling variance and improving convergence stability by constructing enrollment centroids from multiple utterances; second, formatting the similarity calculation of {enrollment, test} utterance pairs into matrix operations to improve computational efficiency. The former is the "why" at the statistical level, and the latter is the "why" at the system level—for each batch, the similarity between all centroids and all test embeddings can be calculated at once as an X×(X·Y/2) matrix, rather than consuming very few comparison pairs at each step like triplet loss.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Moving the Enrollment-Verification Two-Stage Process into the Training Loop

This is the first contribution claimed by the paper (Section 1): applying the GE2E loss to custom KWS training for the first time, simulating the enrollment and verification two-stage processes of runtime during the training phase. Its value is not just the form of the loss function itself, but the **alignment of training and serving distributions**—during serving, the model executes "centroid of multiple enrollment embeddings → cosine calculation with test embeddings → threshold decision," and the training loss optimizes exactly the same set of mathematical objects (centroids, cosine similarities, positive/negative sample structures). In contrast, classification encoders optimize vocabulary classification cross-entropy during training, and triplet loss optimizes single-anchor comparison pairs; both have misalignments with serving computations.

In terms of implementation (Section 4.1), during training, each batch randomly selects 8 phrases, sampling 10 utterances per phrase—i.e., each batch produces 8 centroids and 40 test embeddings, with supervision signal density much higher than "one anchor + one positive + one negative" triplet sampling. Direct evidence of convergence speed is in Table 1: the triplet Conformer trained for 15 epochs, while the GE2E model trained for only 13 epochs, yet the final accuracy was significantly superior.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Evaluation Protocol and Direct Matching Metrics Simulating Real Serving Environments

The paper's second contribution is designing an end-to-end evaluation process (Sections 4.2, 4.3, Figure 1(b)) that replicates the real user journey: for each phrase (e.g., "up"), **randomly select 10 utterances from the test set of the speech command dataset as the enrollment set**, and use all remaining utterances for testing; the enrollment embedding is composed of the centroid aggregated from the embeddings of 10 enrollment utterances, and then cosine similarity is calculated one by one with all test utterance embeddings. To cover real noise scenarios, 3-way MTR enhancement with background noise of 3 to 15 decibels is applied to the speech, so experiments are divided into clean and noisy conditions.

Metric design is divided into three layers:

- **Similarity Histogram** (Figure 2): The enrollment-test similarity scores are divided into 100 bins with a step size of 0.01 from 0 to 1 for statistics; test utterances of the same phrase are counted as true positives (red frequency bars), and those of different phrases are counted as true negatives (blue frequency bars), then normalized into probability distributions (since the number of negative sample pairs is much larger than positive sample pairs, normalization is required for comparability). The more separated the red and blue distributions are on the histogram, the better the model; the overlapping area is the region where the model will make errors regardless of the threshold set. The "why" of this visualization is: it directly exposes threshold separability, rather than going around classification accuracy.
- **DET Curve** (Figure 3): Thresholds are scanned from 0 to 1 with a step size of 0.01; for each threshold, the False Rejection Rate (FRR) and False Acceptance Rate (FAR) are calculated, drawing the FRR-FAR trade-off curve, and marking the FRR/FAR corresponding to example threshold points on the curve.
- **AUC and EER Numerical Metrics**: AUC takes the area under the DET curve, and EER takes the value when FAR equals FRR; both are better when lower. Since each phrase has its own DET curve, AUC/EER are first measured per phrase, then averaged across phrases to obtain the **aggregated AUC/EER**, used to provide an overall model evaluation independent of specific phrases (in the paper, unless otherwise specified, the aggregated version is referred to).

The verification of zero-shot properties is also embedded in this protocol: Figure 4 specifically marks the four words "no", "go", "on", "up" as **not appearing in the training data**; their AUC performance is still consistently better than baselines, indicating that the model has learned general phrase matching capabilities rather than vocabulary memorization.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Model Size-Accuracy Trade-off Scanning and 8-bit Quantization

The third contribution is systematic size tuning for LSTM and Conformer driven by GE2E loss (Section 5.2): LSTM fixes a three-layer stack (retaining sequence dependency modeling), scanning hidden and output layer dimensions, resulting in four original sizes of 2.8/7.5/15/31MB (quantized to 250KB/630KB/1.2MB/2.7MB); Conformer scanned 5 to 12 Conformer blocks, various MHSA head counts, various positional encodings, and attention output layer dimensions, resulting in four original sizes of 2.8/10/18/24MB (quantized to 419KB/1.2MB/1.8MB/2.4MB). This "size-accuracy" menu allows the product side to flexibly select models according to device type and runtime constraints (Section 1). 8-bit dynamic range quantization (Section 3.3) is the core means of size compression; all comparative evaluations are based on quantized models (Tables 1, 2); the footnote of Table 2 also specifically explains that the quantized size includes metadata and state buffers, which vary with architecture—this explains why the same 2.8MB original model quantizes to 250KB for LSTM but 419KB for Conformer, an asymmetry.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

- **Comparison with Triplet Loss**: The difference is not in the broad category of "metric learning," but in sampling structure and computational form. Triplet loss has only one anchor and one positive/negative pair per batch, while GE2E has a dense comparison matrix of X centroids × all test embeddings per batch; triplet loss variance comes from single anchor selection, while GE2E averages out this variance using multi-utterance centroids. Both are trained with **exactly the same data and model architecture** in this paper (Section 2.3), ensuring fair comparison, and the difference can be fully attributed to the loss function.
- **Comparison with ASR Encoder**: The ASR encoder is a "generalist" route—learning representations from massive speech data with large models, incidentally acquiring good keyword matching capabilities, but the 7.5GB size can only stay on servers; GE2E-KWS is a "specialist" route—directly training sub-MB models for phrase matching objectives, trading volume efficiency for task alignment.
- **Comparison with Classification Encoder**: The embeddings of classification encoders are by-products of the classification task, with the training objective being inter-class separation within the vocabulary; GE2E embeddings are the optimization target themselves, and the objects optimized during training (centroid cosine comparison) are completely consistent with serving computations.
- **Comparison with Original GE2E (Speaker Verification)**: This is cross-domain migration—migrating from embedding consistency of "the same speaker" to embedding consistency of "the same phrase content." To ensure the migration holds, the Y utterances of each phrase in the batch are forced to come from different speakers and speaking styles (Section 3.1); otherwise, the model would degenerate into learning speaker timbre rather than phrase content.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

- **Training Data** (Section 4.1): Processed from the MSWC open-source dataset (Multilingual Spoken Words Corpus [26]), containing approximately 38,000 different phrases, 5.3 million training utterances, and approximately 27,000 speakers. Audio is processed into 40-dimensional spectral features with a 25ms frame length. Each training batch randomly selects 8 phrases, sampling 10 utterances per phrase.
- **Evaluation Data** (Section 4.2): The test set of the Speech Commands dataset [13], 35 phrases totaling 11,000 utterances, from different speakers. For each phrase, 10 utterances are randomly selected for enrollment, and the rest for testing. The noisy condition is 3-way MTR with 3 to 15 decibel background noise.
- Notably, training phrases and evaluation phrases are basically non-overlapping ("no", "go", "on", "up" are explicitly not in the training data, Figure 4); the evaluation itself is a test of open-vocabulary capability.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

As described in the Methodology section: the core statistic is the cosine similarity between the enrollment centroid and test embeddings; first, observe the separability of the score distribution using a 100-bin histogram from 0 to 1 with a step size of 0.01 (Figure 2); then scan thresholds to generate DET curves (Figure 3); finally, take the area under the DET curve AUC and Equal Error Rate EER as numerical metrics, averaging phrase-level metrics across phrases to obtain aggregated metrics. Rationale for selection: (1) AUC/EER are **threshold-free** global metrics, not dependent on a specific operating point, suitable for horizontal comparison of model quality itself, while the production side can freely select operating points on the DET curve according to product tolerance for false accepts/false rejects; (2) they directly measure "the matching of two independent utterances"—i.e., what truly happens during custom KWS serving, correcting the misalignment of previous evaluations using classification accuracy; (3) clean/noisy dual conditions are covered, avoiding optimistic bias from reporting only clean conditions.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Core results are in Table 1 (phrase-level and aggregated AUC) and Figures 2, 3, 4, 5:

| Model | Size | Training Method | AUC: All Phrases Aggregated |
|---|---|---|---|
| ASR Encoder | 7.5GB | Pre-trained | 0.66% |
| Speech Classification Encoder | 1.4MB | Pre-trained | 6.44% |
| Triplet Loss Conformer | 419KB (original 2.8MB) | 15 epochs | 1.283% |
| GE2E Loss Conformer | 419KB (original 2.8MB) | 13 epochs | **0.504%** |

Key points broken down item by item:

- **GE2E Conformer achieves an aggregated AUC of 0.504% with a size of 419KB**, surpassing the 7.5GB ASR encoder (0.66%) by 23.6% relatively, and crushing the same-size triplet baseline (1.283%) by 60.7% relatively (Section 5.1). The size difference is 7.5GB vs 419KB, about four orders of magnitude—under the premise of "continuous edge-side operation," this is not a small victory in accuracy, but a qualitative change in feasibility.
- **The failure of the classification encoder confirms the cost of training objective misalignment**: The 1.4MB size is completely edge-friendly, but the 6.44% aggregated AUC is nearly an order of magnitude worse than all other models (Table 1). This provides clear negative evidence for the route of "using embeddings that are by-products of classification tasks for custom KWS."
- **Phrase-level details** (Table 1): For the "backward" phrase, the AUC of the four models is 0.68%/2.70%/0.76%/0.22%, with GE2E having a significant advantage; for the "bed" phrase, the ASR encoder is abnormally strong (0.04%), while GE2E is 0.51%; for the unseen phrase "up", GE2E is 0.83%, better than triplet's 1.71% and classification encoder's 8.11%, but slightly worse than ASR encoder's 0.73%—indicating that for individual unseen phrases, the representation capability of large-model ASR still has marginal advantages, but the gap has been pulled to an acceptable range. The footnote of Table 1 explains that the complete vocabulary is truncated due to space.
- **The aggregated DET curve in Figure 5** plots the FRR/FAR (average FRR/FAR for all evaluation phrases) of the four models at different operating points together, showing that the GE2E model dominates across the entire operating point interval, not just by luck at a certain threshold.
- **The similarity histogram in Figure 2** explains the source of the AUC gap from a distribution level: the overlap area between the red (positive samples) and blue (negative samples) distributions of the GE2E model is smaller, meaning that regardless of how the threshold is chosen, the inherent errors are fewer.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have a traditional ablation table breaking down components one by one; its "ablation" is reflected in three groups of controlled comparisons, each answering a design question:

- **Loss Function Comparison (Same Data, Same Architecture)**: Triplet vs GE2E compared under exactly the same training data, same Conformer architecture, and similar training epochs (15 vs 13) (Section 2.3, Table 1), with aggregated AUC dropping from 1.283% to 0.504%. This is the cleanest causal evidence in the paper: the 60.7% relative improvement can only be attributed to the loss function and sampling structure. Regarding convergence stability, the paper claims in Section 1 that "fast and stable convergence" was observed; the quantitative evidence is achieving better accuracy with fewer training epochs (13 epochs).
- **Model Size Scanning** (Table 2, Figure 6):
  - LSTM (first four rows of Table 2): 2.8MB/250KB tier EER clean 3.17%, noisy 12.71%, AUC 0.568%; 7.5MB/630KB tier 2.62%/10.65%/0.444%; **15MB/1.2MB tier achieves optimal** 2.32%/10.46%/0.344%; continuing to increase to 31MB/2.7MB falls back to 2.45%/10.91%/0.381%. The paper's explanation is that the optimum appears at 15MB "under our training data recipe"—i.e., capacity dividends are exhausted at the scale of 5.3 million training utterances, and larger models begin to overfit or at least provide no gain, which is a useful sweet spot annotation. Meanwhile, the paper reminds that if the production environment values memory usage or latency more, smaller models are still a reasonable choice (Section 5.2).
  - Conformer (last four rows of Table 2): 2.8MB/419KB tier EER clean 2.94%, noisy 9.69%, AUC 0.504%; 10MB/1.2MB tier 2.75%/10.49%/0.416%; 18MB/1.8MB tier 2.5%/9.21%/0.389%; 24MB/2.4MB tier 2.51%/8.98%/0.382%. Performance approaches optimum near 18MB and enters a plateau; the AUC of 24MB is slightly better (0.382% vs 0.389%) but its DET curve is entangled with that of 18MB; the paper explicitly refuses to conclude which is stronger—this restraint is commendable, as the tiny difference in aggregated AUC may fall within evaluation randomness.
  - Tuning range: Conformer experimented with 5 to 12 blocks, various MHSA head counts, various positional encodings, and attention output layer dimensions (Section 5.2).
- **Architecture Trade-offs** (Table 2, Figure 6): Two conclusions. First, **Conformer is significantly better than LSTM at small sizes**—for the same 2.8MB original size, Conformer's noisy EER is 9.69% vs LSTM's 12.71%, and AUC is 0.504% vs 0.568%. Second, **the landscape reverses when size increases, but conditionally**: LSTM is slightly better under clean conditions (15MB LSTM clean EER 2.32% vs 18MB Conformer's 2.5%), but Conformer is always better under noisy conditions (9.21% vs 10.46%). The mechanistic explanation given by the paper is that the convolutional component of Conformer **can detect noise from local contexts** (Section 5.2)—local receptive fields are more robust to perturbations in noisy spectral features, which is consistent with Conformer's reputation for noise robustness in ASR. Engineering implication: edge-side small model selection should lean towards Conformer, especially when the target scenario has environmental noise.
- **Impact of Quantization**: All Conformer comparisons are based on quantized models (Table 1 footnote, Section 5.1); the 2.8MB original model, after quantization to 419KB, can still surpass the 7.5GB ASR encoder, indicating that the accuracy loss of 8-bit dynamic range quantization can be absorbed by the training recipe for this task (the paper does not report separate AUC comparison values before and after quantization).

## Main Contributions

1. **First migration of GE2E loss from speaker verification to custom KWS training**, simulating the runtime "enrollment + verification" two-stage process within the training loop. Compared to the single-anchor one-positive-one-negative of triplet loss, each batch constructs a registration centroid for each phrase and compares it with all positive and negative test utterances, improving training efficiency through matrix operations and reducing sampling variance through multi-utterance centroids, resulting in faster and more stable convergence in practice (13 epochs vs 15 epochs and significantly higher accuracy, Table 1).
2. **Established an end-to-end evaluation process close to the real world**: Based on the public Speech Commands dataset, simulating audio enrollment + custom keyword detection, defining a metric system that directly measures keyword matching quality (similarity histograms, DET curves, phrase-level and aggregated AUC/EER, clean and noisy dual conditions), and verifying zero-shot capability with four unseen words. The paper deliberately writes the evaluation process thoroughly for other KWS research to reuse directly.
3. **Systematically tuned and verified the size-accuracy trade-off of Conformer in custom KWS**: The 419KB quantized Conformer surpassed the 7.5GB ASR encoder with a 23.6% relative AUC advantage, and beat the same-size triplet baseline by 60.7% (Table 1); the model natively supports streaming, can run continuously on edge devices for long periods, and requires zero retraining for new keywords.
4. **Provided a multi-size selection menu for LSTM and Conformer** (Table 2), and identified two judgments with transfer value: Conformer is better than LSTM at small sizes; LSTM is slightly better under clean conditions while Conformer is significantly better under noisy conditions, with the mechanism lying in the local noise detection capability of the convolutional component.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Enrollment Dependency Not Explored**: The evaluation protocol fixes the aggregation of centroids using 10 enrollment utterances (Section 4.2). In real products, users are often only willing to say 1 to 3 enrollment utterances, and the centroid variance will rise significantly; the sensitivity curve of the number of enrollment utterances to AUC is not reported by the paper. This is a piece that must be supplemented before moving from evaluation benchmarks to product parameters.
- **Text/TTS Enrollment Path Has Only Declarations, No Experiments**: The introduction proposes text enrollment via TTS synthesis followed by embedding extraction (citing [6,7]), but all experiments in the paper only performed real audio enrollment; the impact of the embedding domain gap (synthetic-to-real gap) between TTS synthesized speech and real speech on matching accuracy is not reported by the paper. This is precisely the feasibility key for the smoothest interaction method of "users typing to specify wake words."
- **Word-Level Phrase Assumption**: Training and evaluation are both at the single-word level phrase level (MSWC is a spoken word corpus, Speech Commands is 35 words). Scenarios closer to real wake word forms, such as multi-word phrases (e.g., "turn on living room lights"), variable-length phrases, pauses within phrases, etc., are not involved in the paper; the robustness of centroid comparison to duration mismatch is unknown.
- **Naive Loss Form**: The log-sum-exp comparison form of Eq. (4) does not have a temperature coefficient or learnable similarity scaling/bias (the original speaker GE2E paper includes such parameters), nor is it compared with stronger metric learning losses such as InfoNCE, prototypical loss, or ArcFace-like angular margin losses; the 60.7% improvement is relative to triplet loss and does not represent reaching the upper limit of metric learning.
- **Single Language**: Although the training data MSWC itself is a multilingual corpus, evaluation is only done on the English Speech Commands dataset; multilingual zero-shot capability is not reported by the paper.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Lack of Long-Audio Streaming False Alarm Metrics**: Evaluation is enrollment-comparison per utterance, while the most painful metric for production KWS is the number of false accepts per hour on continuous audio for several hours. The FAR of the DET curve is counted by the number of test utterances, which is not the same dimension as the false alarm rate under continuous streaming listening. The paper claims "natively streaming, sustainable operation on edge devices," but there is no corresponding streaming false alarm experiment to support this.
- **No Runtime Overhead Measurement**: The paper only reports model size (original/quantized); key runtime metrics for edge-side deployment such as latency, real-time factor, peak memory, and power consumption are not reported.
- **Lack of Randomness and Significance**: The enrollment set is randomly selected 10 utterances (Section 4.2), but the paper does not report variance or confidence intervals under multiple random splits; the "entanglement" of the 24MB and 18MB DET curves already implies that the difference may be within the noise range, and other similar number pairs (e.g., LSTM 15MB vs 31MB) also lack significance testing.
- **Unequal Baseline Training Conditions**: The ASR encoder and classification encoder are pre-trained models (their native training data, e.g., the classification encoder uses 200 million YouTube utterances), with data sources different from the GE2E/triplet models (MSWC 5.3 million); only the triplet vs GE2E pair is strictly controlled. Cross-baseline numerical comparisons should pay attention to differences in data recipes.
- **Single Noise Condition**: Only 3-way MTR with 3 to 15 decibel background noise (Section 4.2), without real acoustic scenarios such as reverberation, far-field, and multi-speaker overlap.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Enrollment Efficiency and Robustness**: Systematically evaluate performance degradation under 1/3/5/10 enrollment utterances, research centroid regularization or virtual centroid augmentation for few-shot enrollment; perform threshold calibration per phrase rather than global threshold to absorb score distribution differences caused by phrase length and syllable structure.
- **Closed-Loop Verification of Text Enrollment**: Incorporate the TTS text enrollment path into the same evaluation protocol, quantify the gap between synthetic speech enrollment and real audio enrollment, and explore TTS domain adaptation or audio-text joint embedding alignment (converging with the dual-modal embedding route of [18][19]).
- **Stronger Metric Learning Losses and Cross-Domain Migration**: Introduce temperature-scaled InfoNCE or angular margin losses for comparison; utilize the multilingual attribute of MSWC to perform cross-lingual zero-shot evaluation, verifying the language-independence of phrase matching capabilities.
- **Streaming-Oriented Evaluation Expansion**: Report false accepts per hour and detection latency on continuous audio for several hours, completing the evidence chain for the "natively streaming" claim; add reverberation and far-field enhancement.
- **Runtime Profiling**: Provide latency, memory, and power consumption of models of various sizes on real edge-side hardware (DSP/MCU/mobile SoC), upgrading the "size menu" in Table 2 to a "deployment menu."
- **Collaboration with Large Models**: Since the 7.5GB ASR encoder still has marginal advantages on individual words like "bed" and "up," it can be used as a teacher for knowledge distillation, pouring the large model's representation capability into the 419KB Conformer, further squeezing the upper limit of small models.
