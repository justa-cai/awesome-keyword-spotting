# Synth4Kws: Synthesized Speech for User Defined Keyword Spotting in Low Resource Environments

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Pai Zhu, Dhruuv Agarwal, Jacob W. Bartel, Kurt Partridge, Hyun Jin Park, Quan Wang (Google LLC, Mountain View, USA)
- **Date**: July 2024 (arXiv:2407.16840v1, submitted July 23, 2024)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16840
- **Keywords**: user-defined keyword spotting, TTS synthetic data augmentation, triplet loss metric learning, low-resource environments, on-device streaming model, embedding similarity matching, real data volume planning

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is a persistent voice entry point for devices such as smart speakers, health bands, smartwatches, voice-controlled earbuds, and smartphones. KWS models on these devices must run continuously under constraints of low power consumption and low memory footprint. This constraint defines the model selection boundary for all experiments in this paper: the final model adopted in the paper is only 247Kb after quantification (Section 2.1 of the original text) and can run continuously on the edge device.

Traditional KWS targets fixed wake words, such as Siri or Hey Google, where the vocabulary is frozen before the model goes live. However, the trend toward personalization has changed requirements: users want to choose their own word to trigger a device or software, which is an indispensable part of the interaction experience with AI agents. Consequently, custom keyword spotting (user-defined/custom KWS) has shifted from a "closed-set classification" problem to an "open-set matching" problem—the model must determine whether two speech utterances refer to the same phrase, even for phrases the model has never seen during training. This paradigm shift is the source of all the challenges and all the value in this paper: closed-set classification only requires training data covering dozens of words, whereas open-set matching requires corpus covering an extremely wide range of phrases, languages, speaker demographics, speaking styles, and prosody. Collecting such real-world corpus is both time-consuming and expensive.

Using a high-precision ASR system for keyword detection is a ready-made route, but the energy consumption and memory footprint of ASR make it unsuitable for continuous use on devices like smartphones. Thus, the lightweight approach of generating speech embeddings based on classification models and then comparing them using cosine similarity has become the mainstream for edge devices. The Achilles' heel of the embedding approach lies precisely in the training data: the "separability of arbitrary phrases" in the embedding space is entirely determined by the distribution of phrases seen during training. Insufficient coverage of long-tail phrases directly manifests as matching errors on an open vocabulary.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper梳理s the gaps in existing work from three levels, each pointing to the entry point of this paper:

- **Embedding models do not optimize phrase spacing**. Lin et al. (Reference 5) used a five-layer convolutional network as a shared encoder, trained 125 independent decoders, each classifying on 40 keywords, and used the encoder output as utterance embeddings; Rybakov et al. (Reference 6) further explored architectures such as DNN, CNN, SVDF, CRNN, Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA), and MHSA-RNN, significantly improving classification accuracy. However, the embeddings of such classification models are inherently flawed: they are only trained to discriminate "is this utterance a specific keyword" and are never required to maximize the embedding distance between different phrase utterances. This is sufficient for a fixed vocabulary but insufficient for custom keywords (registered phrases not seen during training)—points for two different phrases in the embedding space may be very close, leading to false acceptances.
- **Low batch sampling efficiency of metric learning schemes**. To directly optimize embedding distances, Sacchi et al. (Reference 7) introduced FaceNet's triplet loss to open-set KWS: sampling anchor, positive (same phrase), and negative (different phrase) samples within a batch, minimizing positive pair distance and maximizing negative pair distance, achieving reasonable accuracy on WSJ using a two-layer GRU. However, each batch constructs only one positive pair and one negative pair, resulting in sparse supervision signals, large gradient variance, slow training convergence, and instability.
- **Existing research on TTS for KWS does not answer the "ratio" question**. TTS technology (MAESTRO, Virtuoso) can already generate speech with speaker and prosody distributions for over 100 languages, and its value has been proven in scenarios such as ASR data augmentation, speaker recognition (Synth2aug), automatic dubbing, and voice conversion. Liu et al. (Reference 18) and Lim and Baek (Reference 19) indeed applied TTS to KWS, but they focused on improving the quality of models for specific product types using TTS data, without systematically evaluating the ratio of synthetic to real data and the impact of this ratio on quality across different resource tiers. In other words, the three questions that engineers truly need to decide—"how much to synthesize, how much real data to mix in, and when synthetic data becomes harmful"—were not answered by previous literature.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The paper converges the above gaps into three highly product-oriented scenarios and provides quantitative answers to each:

1. **Zero Real Data**: When there is not a single word of real data, to what level do the phrase diversity of TTS data and the sampling volume per phrase push the model quality?
2. **Low Real Data**: When there are only about 50,000 real utterances on hand, how much TTS data should be mixed in for optimal results? What happens if too much is mixed in?
3. **Need to Plan Real Data Collection Volume**: Given that there is sufficient TTS data as a baseline, how many more real utterances need to be collected to achieve a certain quality target (e.g., EER not exceeding 5%)?

The third scenario particularly reflects the internal perspective of Google: real data collection is a budget decision involving money and time. The paper attempts to turn the "quality target → real data budget" into an interpolable curve, directly serving resource planning.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The Synth4Kws framework consists of three parts (Fig. 1): (a) TTS data sampling process; (b) phrase diversity and utterance sampling scans under zero-resource/low-resource experimental settings; (c) evaluation process and metric calculation.

On the model side, a conservative but explicitly edge-constraint-serving choice was made: a standard three-layer LSTM, with a hidden layer dimension of 384 and an output layer dimension of 128. The full model is 2.8Mb, and after quantification, it is 247Kb (Section 2.1), with a power level that allows continuous operation on the device. The paper explicitly states that they tried other LSTM sizes and different architectures (including Conformer), and finally selected the 247Kb quantized LSTM based on the "trade-off between accuracy and memory footprint, capable of fitting into most device types." Why not the more fashionable Conformer or TCN? Because the research object of this paper is data ratio rather than architecture. Fixing a verified, deployable small model pins down the architecture variable, allowing all metric changes to be attributed to data. This is a typical "control variables first" experimental design motivation, with the cost that the transferability of conclusions to other architectures is questionable (expanded in the limitations section below).

The training framework uses TensorFlow/Lingvo (Reference 24), for engineering reasons: Lingvo supports automatic streaming inference conversion, and the trained LSTM can be directly converted into an on-device streaming model without manual network modification. Features are 40-dimensional spectral energy features extracted per 25ms frame (Section 3.1). No more complex feature frontends were used, again to focus variables on the data side.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The matching paradigm is "registration centroid + cosine similarity." During the registration phase, the user says the same phrase several times, and the model averages the embeddings of these utterances to obtain the registration centroid. During the inference phase, the streaming model outputs an embedding for the utterance to be checked, calculates the cosine similarity with the centroid, and judges it as a hit if it exceeds the threshold. This paradigm naturally supports any new phrase—phrase information exists only in the registration centroid, and the model is only responsible for learning a universal embedding space where "same phrases cluster and different phrases separate."

The loss function is where the paper truly put effort mathematically, centered on the intra-batch fully paired triplet structure. Suppose a batch contains X phrases, with Y utterances per phrase. Half of the utterances per phrase are used for registration, and the other half for testing. The embedding of each test utterance is compared with the registration centroid of its所属 phrase. Thus, a batch produces:

- Positive pairs: X·Y/2 (each test utterance against its own phrase centroid);
- Negative pairs: X(X−1)·Y/2 (each test utterance against the centroids of the remaining X−1 phrases).

Specific training configuration is X=8, Y=10 (Section 3.1: randomly select 8 phrases, sample 10 utterances per phrase, 5 to build registration centroids), substituting to get 40 positive pairs and 280 negative pairs per batch, with a positive-to-negative ratio of 1:7.

Two key designs directly serve the stability of this mathematical structure. First, using centroids instead of single registered utterances as comparison anchors. The reason given in the original text is "reducing the variance of registered utterance samples and improving training convergence stability"—when a single utterance is used as an anchor, the anchor's own noise (speaker, channel, prosody randomness) directly enters the gradient of every pair. The centroid average smears out this variance, equivalent to performing intra-batch denoising on the anchor. Second, because the number of negative pairs is 7 times that of positive pairs, the training distribution is severely skewed, and the posterior will be biased by the skewed prior. Therefore, negative samples are down-weighted by a factor γ. This is a typical "long-tail pair rebalancing": if all 7 times the negative pairs participate with full weight, the model will over-learn "pulling apart" at the expense of "clustering." Registration matching requires controlling both types of errors. The specific value of γ is not reported in the paper. Additionally, the loss for all pairs is calculated at once using matrix operations, avoiding Python loops for pair-by-pair calculation, significantly accelerating training.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: GE2E-style Batch Construction and Negative Sample Down-weighting

Compared to the traditional triplet sampling of one positive and one negative pair per batch by Sacchi et al., this paper borrows the batch construction idea from the speaker verification domain GE2E (Wan et al., Reference 22) and移植s it to KWS: in a batch, X phrases automatically form a complete positive-negative pair matrix among themselves. Under the same forward computation cost, the supervision signal expands from 2 pairs to 320 pairs (40 positive + 280 negative). Why is this important? Because the effectiveness of triplet-like losses highly depends on the quality and quantity of negative samples within the batch—the more negative pairs and the more diverse the phrases, the more uniformly the embedding space is "pushed apart," making it less likely for certain phrase directions to collapse into local solutions. Coupled with centroid anchor variance reduction and γ negative sample down-weighting rebalancing, this combination allows the small model (247Kb) to converge stably on the open-set matching task, which is the prerequisite for all subsequent data experiments to measure monotonic trends.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Treating the Two Orthogonal Diversity Axes of TTS Data as Controlled Variables

TTS generation uses Virtuoso (Reference 12). The reason for choosing it is its high naturalness and good generalization to unseen text and unseen languages—it is built on MAESTRO (Reference 11), using a mixed training scheme of supervised and unsupervised (untranscribed speech, un-read text) data, supporting over 139 locales (Reference 13). The sampling protocol (Fig. 1(a)) is: the phrase table is taken from 38k unique words in the MSWC corpus (Reference 23), and utterances for each word are sampled and generated from 726 speakers and 5 prosody types.

The paper splits the TTS data volume into two orthogonal axes for separate scanning: **phrase diversity** (unique phrase count 500 → 1k → 10k → 38k, fixed 100 utterances per phrase) and **sampling volume per phrase** (fixed 38k phrases, 10 → 25 → 50 → 75 → 100 utterances per phrase). The mechanisms by which these two axes affect the embedding space are different: phrase diversity mainly shapes **inter-phrase separation**, determining whether new phrases in the open set can be correctly distinguished; sampling volume per phrase mainly shapes **intra-phrase compactness and robustness**, determining how small the embedding fluctuations are for the same phrase across different speakers/prosody. Scanning them separately allows one to see whether their contributions are symmetric with and without real data—the results are indeed asymmetric, which is one of the most informative findings of the paper (see the experimental section).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Using Quality-Data Volume Interpolation Curves for Resource Planning

The method for the third scenario is to plot the quality curve of "TTS baseline (38k phrases × 100 utterances per phrase) + increasing real data" (real data volume takes 0, 50k, 150k, 250k, 500k, 1M, 3M, 5M, Fig. 5), and then interpolate the data points to read out the number of real utterances required to achieve a given quality target. This is not an algorithmic innovation but an innovation in engineering decision-making methods: it turns the question of "whether to spend money collecting data" from a guess into checking a curve. The paper provides two anchors: to achieve EER ≤ 5%, approximately 700,000 real utterances need to be collected; to achieve AUC ≤ 2%, approximately 200,000 are needed (Section 4.3, Fig. 5 interpolation).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to Lin et al. (Reference 5, which also explores the value of TTS data in the speech domain): their experiments and evaluations are oriented towards utterance classification (closed-set), whereas this paper is oriented towards embedding open-set matching, and the evaluation protocol (registration centroid + threshold-independent DET AUC) is designed completely according to the real usage method of custom keywords. Compared to Liu et al. (Reference 18), Lim and Baek (Reference 19): they are "using TTS to get a specific product up to standard," whereas this paper treats the synthetic/real ratio as the research object, systematically scanning across three resource tiers: zero resource, low resource, and planning. Compared to Sacchi et al. (Reference 7): batch construction upgrades from single-pair sampling to GE2E full pairing + centroid anchor + negative sample down-weighting + matrix implementation. Compared to the ASR route: the 247Kb streaming model directly targets continuous on-device operation, not comparing accuracy with large models with tens of thousands of parameters.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

- **TTS Phrase Source**: 38k unique words from the MSWC corpus (Reference 23); utterances for each word are sampled and generated by 726 speakers × 5 prosody types (Section 2.3). Calculated at 38k × 100 utterances for full configuration, the synthetic corpus volume reaches 3.8 million utterances.
- **Real Training Data**: Sampled from the MSWC open-source dataset. The low-resource baseline uses about 50,000 real utterances (Section 3.2); the planning experiment scans up to 5 million (Fig. 5). The paper does not report how many unique phrases are contained in the 50,000 real utterances.
- **Evaluation Data**: Speech Commands dataset (Reference 25) test set, over 11k utterances, 35 unique phrases (Section 3.3). Randomly select 10 utterances per phrase for the registration set, and the rest for testing. Note the deep meaning of this arrangement: models trained purely on TTS must be evaluated on **real speech**, so the metrics in the zero-resource scenario directly measure the "synthetic training → real testing" domain generalization capability, rather than self-indulgence within the synthetic domain.
- **Features and Training**: 40-dimensional spectral energy features, 25ms frames; batch construction 8 phrases × 10 utterances per phrase, 5 to build centroids, 5 for testing (Section 3.1); Lingvo framework.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The evaluation process (Fig. 1(c), Fig. 2) starts from the cosine similarity histogram: in the score histogram for the 247Kb model on the phrase "up" (left of Fig. 2), a perfect model should have the red line (true positives) and the blue line (true negatives) cleanly separated by a vertical threshold line. The overlapping part is the error, divided into false acceptance (FA) and false rejection (FR) according to the threshold direction. The threshold slides from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.01, recording FAR and FRR at each point to draw the DET curve (right of Fig. 2).

Two metrics: **EER** (Equal Error Rate, the intersection of FAR=FRR) and **AUC** (Area Under the DET curve, integrating the full FAR/FRR range). The rationale for selection is clearly stated in the paper: custom keyword products do not have a fixed operating point—the threshold is selected by the user/product according to their preference for "false wake-up vs. failure to wake up." Therefore, **threshold-independent** metrics (AUC) are needed to rank models; meanwhile, to avoid being affected by the choice of registered phrases, AUC must be averaged over all phrases. Both are better when lower, and AUC is the default sorting metric throughout the paper. This metric design itself reflects the characteristics of the task: unlike closed-set KWS reporting accuracy, open-set matching requires beating others on the entire DET curve to win.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Zero Real Data Scenario (Table 1)**: Fixed 100 utterances per phrase, phrase count from 500 → 38k, EER monotonically decreases from 34.42% to 12.62%, AUC decreases from 28.90% to 5.93%; fixed 38k phrases, utterance count per phrase from 10 → 100, EER monotonically decreases from 16.88% to 12.62%, AUC decreases from 9.32% to 5.93% (Table 1, corresponding to the family of DET curves in Fig. 3, triangle curves for phrase scan, dotted lines for sampling volume scan). Both axes show pure monotonic improvement, and the paper emphasizes that the improvement is consistent across different operating points on the DET curve. In terms of gradient distribution, the segment from phrase diversity 1k → 10k yields the largest benefit (EER 30.17% → 15.09%, close to halving), while 10k → 38k shows diminishing returns (15.09% → 12.62%).

**Low Real Data Scenario (Table 2, baseline = trained on 50k real utterances)**: Fixed 100 TTS utterances per phrase, phrase count 0 (pure real baseline) → 38k, EER decreases from 11.73% to 8.7% (relative improvement 25.8%), AUC decreases from 5.15% to 2.94% (relative improvement 42.9%). Fixed 38k phrases scanning utterance count per phrase: 0 → 10 → 25 → 50 utterances, EER sequentially 11.73 → 8.68 → 8.48 → 8.19, AUC sequentially 5.15 → 2.92 → 2.85 → 2.64, peaking at 50 utterances; drops back to 8.88/2.91 at 75 utterances, 8.7/2.94 at 100 utterances. The optimal point 38k × 50 yields a relative improvement of EER 30.1%, AUC 48.7% (Section 4.2). Note an internal inconsistency: the abstract states the AUC relative improvement is 46.7%, while Section 4.2 states 48.7%; recalculating from Table 2 numbers (5.15 − 2.64)/5.15 ≈ 48.7%, so the main text is correct, and the abstract is likely a typo.

A set of cross-table comparisons is worth highlighting separately (derived from comparing Table 1 and Table 2 numbers): the pure TTS full configuration (38k × 100, approx. 3.8 million synthetic utterances) has an EER of 12.62% and AUC of 5.93%, whereas the baseline of merely 50,000 real utterances has an EER of 11.73% and AUC of 5.15%—**no amount of synthetic data can catch up to even a tiny fraction of real data**. This shows that the value of TTS lies not in replacing real data, but in the enhancement effect when mixed with real data (and as a fallback in zero-resource scenarios). This is consistent with the magnitude sense in the planning experiment (Fig. 5) where 700,000 real utterances are needed to reach EER 5%: real data is the main contributor to the quality ceiling.

Horizontal comparison with external SOTA (e.g., public numbers from concurrent open-set KWS papers) is not reported by the paper; all comparisons are internal baselines. This is a boundary to keep in mind when interpreting conclusions.

**Planning Scenario (Fig. 5)**: TTS fixed at 38k × 100, real utterances increase from 0 to 50k, 150k, 250k, 500k, 1M, 3M, 5M. EER and AUC are monotonically positively correlated with real data volume; interpolation gives EER ≤ 5% requires approx. 700,000 real utterances, AUC ≤ 2% requires approx. 200,000 real utterances (Section 4.3).

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The ablation in this paper is not about cutting modules but scanning data ratios, with four findings:

1. **Both axes are purely monotonic under zero resources (Table 1)**: Without real data, phrase diversity and sampling volume per phrase contribute to model quality "proportionally"—the marginal benefit of synthetic data shows no sign of decay at this tier (scanned up to 38k × 100).
2. **Phrase diversity gains decrease rapidly under low resources (Table 2 upper half)**: After mixing in 50,000 real utterances, adding TTS phrases from 500 to 1k still yields visible benefits (EER 10.17 → 9.22), but from 1k to 10k to 38k, it is almost flat (9.22 → 9.13 → 8.7). Why? A reasonable explanation is that real data itself already provides a large amount of inter-phrase separability (MSWC real sampling naturally covers multiple phrases and speakers), and the marginal value of TTS in this dimension is squeezed out by real data.
3. **Sampling volume per phrase is non-monotonic under low resources, with an optimal point (Table 2 lower half)**: Metrics deteriorate reversely after exceeding 50 utterances (8.19 → 8.88 → 8.7). The explanation given in the paper is "excessive TTS data may overwhelm the contribution of real data, and the model obtains less information from real utterances"—i.e., after the synthetic/real ratio is unbalanced, the training distribution shifts towards the synthetic domain, eroding the fitting of the real domain. This is a very important negative result: **synthetic data is not better the more there is; there is an optimal mixing ratio, and exceeding it causes performance drops**.
4. **Quality-real data volume curve is monotonic and interpolable (Fig. 5)**: Provides quantitative anchors for resource planning.

The value of the γ down-weighting factor, learning rate, training steps, optimizers, and other training hyperparameters are not reported in the paper, and the ablation does not cover scans of the two TTS sampling dimensions: number of speakers (726) and number of prosody types (5).

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed the Synth4Kws framework**: The first scheme to systematically study the triad of "TTS synthetic data × custom KWS × resource tier" within the same controlled framework, covering three engineering-realistic tiers: zero real data, low real data, and real data planning.
2. **Provided reusable quantitative conclusions**: Under zero resources, both phrase diversity and sampling volume per phrase monotonically improve quality (Table 1); on the baseline of 50k real utterances, the optimal ratio (38k phrases × 50 utterances per phrase) brings a relative EER improvement of 30.1% and a relative AUC improvement of 48.7% (Table 2, Section 4.2); and for the first time, explicitly reports the ratio reversal phenomenon where **excessive synthetic data backfires on the contribution of real data**.
3. **Provided a resource planning method**: The interpolation curve of quality against real data volume (Fig. 5) turns "achieving EER ≤ 5% requires approx. 700,000, AUC ≤ 2% requires approx. 200,000 real utterances" into a directly queryable budget basis.
4. **Engineering improvements on the training side**: GE2E-style batch construction (8 phrases × 10 utterances, half building centroids) + negative sample γ down-weighting + matrix loss calculation, allowing the 247Kb quantized LSTM to train stably on open-set matching, and automatically converted into an on-device streaming model via Lingvo.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Ratio reversal only has a phenomenological explanation**. For the "drop after 50 utterances," the paper only gives a speculative explanation that "excessive TTS may overwhelm the contribution of real data," without mechanism-level verification, nor does it provide mitigation measures—such as domain-balanced sampling, curriculum learning (synthetic first, then real), domain adversarial loss, or weighted domain adaptation, tools commonly used in synthetic/real mixed training, which the paper did not attempt. Practitioners can only find the optimal point by re-scanning grids, which is costly.
- **Acoustic gap between synthetic and real domains is not explicitly handled**. Although Virtuoso has diversity from 726 speakers × 5 prosody types, synthetic speech has systematic gaps with real recordings in terms of channel noise, reverberation, and spontaneous speech variations. The fact that pure TTS full configuration still cannot catch up to 50,000 real utterances (Table 1 vs. Table 2) is a direct quantification of this gap. The paper did not perform any domain compensation (such as synthetic-side enhancement with noise addition, reverberation addition).
- **English only, word-level only**. Experiments were trained and evaluated based on English and word-level utterances (admitted in the conclusion). Multi-word keywords involve word order and coarticulation, and the complexity of the embedding space is completely different. The authors could only "believe the conclusions are generalizable" without experimental support.
- **Single model**. All conclusions are built on the 247Kb quantized LSTM; numbers for architectures like Conformer, which were tried, are not reported. Whether the optimal point for data ratio shifts with model capacity (whether larger models can absorb more massive synthetic data) remains an unresolved issue.
- **Missing quantification details**. The compression from 2.8Mb to 247Kb (approx. 11 times, Section 2.1 of the original text) uses what quantization scheme, and how much precision is lost after quantization, is not reported.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **No statistical significance control**: All tables are single-run results, with no repetition across multiple random seeds or confidence intervals. Differences in magnitude like 8.19 vs. 8.48 vs. 8.7 in Table 2 (optimal point vs. adjacent configurations) are hard to assert as not being noise without variance information, yet the "optimal point is at 50 utterances" is built precisely on the ranking of these few points.
- **Evaluation set is small and registration is random**: 35 phrases, 10 registration utterances per phrase (Section 3.3). The randomness of the registration set affects metrics; although the paper averaged AUC over phrases, it did not report repeated experiments with multiple random registrations.
- **Inconsistency between abstract and main text numbers**: AUC relative improvement is 46.7% in the abstract, 48.7% in the main text (recalculation supports 48.7%), indicating that results were not proofread rigorously enough.
- **No external baselines**: No comparison with any published open-set KWS systems or other data augmentation methods (noise perturbation, SpecAugment, speed perturbation), making it impossible to judge the incremental value of TTS augmentation relative to these cheaper methods.
- **Interpolation method not verified**: Fig. 5 has only 8 data points. The conclusion that EER ≤ 5% requires 700,000 utterances relies on assumptions about the shape of the interpolation curve. The paper does not report the interpolation method or extrapolation risks.
- **Phrase distribution within real data not reported**: How many unique phrases are covered by the 50,000 real utterances directly determines the interpretation of the marginal value of "TTS supplementing phrase diversity." This key variable is missing.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Automating ratio and sampling strategies**: Make the synthetic/real mixing ratio a dynamic variable during training (e.g., adaptively adjusted based on gradient contribution or domain loss), or use curriculum learning to lay a synthetic foundation first and then fine-tune with real data, avoiding the reversal of "excessive synthetic overwhelming real" from a mechanism perspective.
- **Synthetic-side domain enhancement**: Apply noise, reverberation, channel simulation, and speaker perturbation to TTS outputs to explicitly narrow the synthetic-real acoustic gap, potentially raising the upper limit of pure synthetic models and the optimal ratio for mixed training.
- **Multilingual and multi-word extension**: The authors themselves point out that the method is "even more valuable for low-resource languages" (such languages have scarcer real data, and Virtuoso covers 139+ locales). Pushing experiments to multi-word keywords and multiple languages is the natural next step and the only way to test generalization claims.
- **Joint calibration of model and data**: Scan the optimal synthetic ratio across models of different capacities (from smaller LSTMs to Conformer) to answer "how large a model is needed to consume how much synthetic data," allowing joint optimization of data budget and model budget.
- **Active real data collection**: Use uncertainty/diversity criteria to actively select the most worth-annotating real utterances on the TTS-baselined model, refining the planning curve in Fig. 5 from "how many utterances to collect" to "which batch to collect," further reducing the collection cost to achieve quality targets.
