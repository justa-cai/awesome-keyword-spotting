## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

XR headsets (covering VR/AR/MR) are rapidly penetrating work and entertainment scenarios alongside the popularity of devices like Apple Vision Pro and Microsoft HoloLens. As bridges between the virtual and real worlds, the naturalness of their interaction methods directly determines user acceptance. The paper points out a clear industry trend at the outset: more and more headsets are abandoning controllers in favor of intuitive interactions such as voice, gestures, and gaze. Among these, voice interaction handles high-frequency tasks such as menu selection, locomotion control, and moving objects in coordination with gaze. However, the large-scale deployment of voice interaction has been bottlenecked by the robustness of the recognition layer—both ASR and KWS lack stability across real-world environments.

Compared to ASR, KWS (Keyword Spotting) does not require cloud connectivity, has low computational requirements, and avoids privacy leakage issues, making it a more reasonable carrier for always-on edge-side interaction. The paper reviews three generations of KWS technical routes in the Related Work section: ASR-based (transcribing to text then matching, flexible vocabulary but inheriting ASR's computational and privacy burdens), HMM-based (training an HMM for each keyword plus a filler model, using Viterbi decoding for matching, good performance but high computational overhead for multi-model Viterbi matching), and the mainstream deep KWS of the past decade (from fully connected and RNN to CNN architectures).

In the Introduction, the paper summarizes three pain points of traditional vocal KWS in XR scenarios, each corresponding to a real usage context:

1. **Inaccuracy in noisy environments**: Recognition accuracy degrades significantly on streets, in malls, and on public transportation. This is not because the model is inadequate, but because the information source of vocal KWS (air-conducted speech) is inherently polluted by environmental noise in the human voice frequency band.
2. **Complete failure in quiet settings**: When someone nearby is working or resting, and the user cannot speak due to privacy concerns or social embarrassment, vocal KWS drops to zero. This is not a problem of decreased accuracy, but of complete functional unavailability.
3. **False triggers caused by others' speech**: Headset microphones lack strong speaker directionality, so speech from nearby people is treated by the system as user commands, causing false wake-ups and false executions. In high-density human voice environments like meetings or classrooms, false triggers are more damaging to the user experience than missed detections.

These three pain points point to a structural contradiction: the information source of a single vocal modality is physically missing or polluted in certain scenarios, and no amount of optimization at the model layer can compensate for it. The solution must be found at the modality level—introducing a second information source with different failure conditions than the voice channel.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper systematically reviews the existing technology line of Silent Speech Interface (SSI) and points out the defects of its two types of solutions:

- **Contact-based SSI**: Magnetometers, capacitive sensors placed inside the mouth to capture tongue and oral cavity movements; ultrasound imaging, EMG, motion sensors, and strain sensors attached to the skin. These methods require skin contact or even oral invasion, are uncomfortable for long-term wear, and are difficult to integrate into headsets.
- **Non-contact SSI**: Camera-based solutions (C-Face on earphones, TieLent/SpeeChin on neckbands) have high power consumption and privacy risks—having a camera on the head constantly facing the face raises questions about social acceptability; active acoustic solutions (EchoWhisper using a phone, CELIP using a VR headset, EchoSpeech using glasses frames) use speakers to emit ultrasound and microphones to receive echoes, offering low power consumption and high performance, making them the most viable route for implementation. However, CELIP requires transducers to face the mouth directly and needs a pair of relatively large microphones and speakers; EchoSpeech places compact devices on the lower edge of the glasses, offering a more concealed form factor.

The more critical gap lies in the fact that **all these non-contact SSI systems are single echoic modality systems; their potential to fuse with conventional vocal KWS to achieve stronger performance and broader applicability has not been explored previously**. Single echoic systems also have their own weaknesses—later sections on Limitations will show that daily actions like walking and shaking the head can increase the echoic channel's WER by about 50% and 33%, respectively, and it also degrades under strong noise. That is, the two single-modality systems each have failure zones, but the failure conditions are different (vocal is afraid of noise and silence, echoic is afraid of vigorous movement). This is exactly the value space for fusion and the entry point of this paper.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

1. **Acquiring dual-modality without perceptual burden**: Headsets are extremely sensitive to power consumption, weight, and privacy, and cannot accommodate cameras or skin-attached sensors for a second modality. The challenge lies in whether existing small speakers and microphones can simultaneously acquire voice and lip movement information.
2. **Isolation of failed modalities**: Fusion is not a simple weighted average. In silent scenarios, the vocal pipeline will output invalid predictions like silence/unknown, and naive fusion would be dragged down by it. The fusion strategy must satisfy two goals simultaneously—fully utilizing the complementary information of both modalities to improve accuracy, while automatically degrading to single-modality decision-making when one side is unreliable.
3. **Computational slimming of the echoic model**: The predecessor work EchoSpeech used ResNet-18 (11.22M parameters, 1.84G MAdd) in the echoic channel, which is much heavier than the lightweight KWS network in the vocal channel. Directly porting it to headsets would bring additional power consumption and latency. The model needs to be reduced by one to two orders of magnitude without significant performance loss.
4. **Real-device landing and system-level verification**: It cannot stop at simulation comparisons; a complete system capable of real-time operation must be built on HoloLens 2, and system experiments must be conducted under three difficult scenarios: noise, silence, and interference from others.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall form of the system (Fig. 2) is a three-stage structure of "single microphone input, dual-pipeline parallel processing, end-stage fusion":

- **Hardware and Data Path**: Two pairs of existing small speakers/microphones are mounted on the HoloLens 2, connected to an ESP32; the ESP32 controls the speaker playback, collects microphone signals, and sends the audio to a PC (or the headset itself) via network for algorithmic computation. The detected keywords are then sent back to the application on the headset. During the experimental phase, algorithms run on a PC, which is a reasonable compromise of "real-device data acquisition, offline computation."
- **Modality Separation**: Unlike traditional systems that directly use raw audio for vocal KWS, this system first uses a bandpass filter to split the audio into two paths—low-pass (cutoff 10 kHz) to extract vocal speech, and high-pass to extract ultrasonic echoes, sending them into two separate KWS pipelines. Finally, the prediction vectors from both pipelines are sent to the fusion module to obtain a single output.

The premise for this architecture to work is a clean spectral fact: the fundamental frequency F0 of human speech roughly falls within 100-240 Hz, and even considering harmonics, the upper frequency limit used in the mel filter banks for conventional speech recognition is usually around 5000 Hz; whereas the ultrasonic used in this system is above 17 kHz. There is a large blank frequency band between the 100 Hz-5 kHz speech energy and the 17 kHz+ ultrasonic echoes. Therefore, **a single commercial microphone sampling at 48 kHz can losslessly accommodate both modalities**, requiring only one acquisition and filter separation. This is the physical foundation of the entire scheme's "low cost." The choice of the 17-23.5 kHz interval is also a trade-off: it needs to be compatible with the frequency response of 48 kHz commercial acoustic devices (within the Nyquist limit of 24 kHz) while being as high as possible above speech harmonics and most of the human ear's sensitive range.

The device layout (Fig. 3, Sec 3.2) follows the high-performance configuration verified by EchoSpeech on glasses frames: two speakers along the lower edge of the right lens, two microphones along the lower edge of the left lens, with sound holes facing down. Specific geometric parameters: the speaker and microphone closer to the nose are 7.2 cm apart; the distance between the two speakers and the distance between the two microphones are both 1.8 cm; the average vertical distance from the microphone plane to the horizontal midline of the mouth is 3.9 cm (measured average for 15 wearers). Because the internal device positions of commercial headsets do not match the system's needs, the authors used an ESP32 development board (ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1-N8R8) plus a self-designed add-on board to connect the two pairs of devices (speakers OWR-05049T-38D, microphones ICS-43434), all of which are ordinary mass-produced parts purchased online—this is important for reproducibility and the cost narrative.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**FMCW Transmit Signal**: Within each chirp, the frequency increases linearly with time, $f(t) = f_l + (f_h - f_l) \cdot t/T$, where $f_l$ and $f_h$ are the frequency lower and upper bounds, and $T$ is the chirp period (Fig. 4a). The system simultaneously transmits FMCW on two frequency bands: 17-20 kHz and 20.5-23.5 kHz, with a chirp period of 12 ms (the original text says "two microphones simultaneously emit," which is judged to be a typo for speakers based on context and device division of labor). The two frequency bands are offset by more than 0.5 kHz to allow the two echo paths to be separable in the frequency domain.

**Distance Resolution Based on Cross-Correlation**: For each chirp, the received echo is cross-correlated with the transmitted signal to obtain correlation values at different sample shifts. The sample shift is proportional to the path difference of "microphone-reflection medium-speaker," so the cross-correlation curve is essentially a reflection intensity distribution with distance as the abscissa. At a 48 kHz sampling rate, the distance resolution is **0.357 cm**—this precision is sufficient to resolve position changes of the skin around the mouth relative to the headset during speech, providing the quantitative guarantee for the echoic modality to distinguish different pronunciation actions.

**Echo Profile and Differencing**: An cross-correlation curve is calculated for each continuous chirp, stacked into a graph with "time on the x-axis and sample shift on the y-axis," which is the Echo Profile; then differencing along the time axis yields the Differential Echo Profile (Fig. 4 b-c). The physical significance of this differencing step is to filter out static reflections (the fixed geometry of the headset and face, static skin positions), retaining only the time-varying part—namely, lip movement features. The "static ultrasonic source interference can be ignored" mentioned in the Limitations section later also owes to this differencing processing; it is a natural anti-static interference mechanism at the feature level.

**WER and SNR**: Word Error Rate $\mathrm{WER} = (S+D+I)/N = (S+D+I)/(S+D+C)$ (Eq. 7), where $S/D/I/C$ are the number of substitutions, deletions, insertions, and correct words, respectively; Signal-to-Noise Ratio $\mathrm{SNR} = 10\log_{10}(P_s/P_n)$ (Eq. 8).

**Vocal Pipeline**: 10 kHz low-pass audio undergoes pre-emphasis, framing, windowing, FFT, mel-frequency warping, logarithm, and DCT to obtain MFCCs, which are fed into a CNN with a broadcast residual structure (i.e., Kim et al.'s BC-ResNet [19]) to produce prediction vectors. The vocal pipeline directly reuses SOTA without reinventing the wheel—the authors' engineering judgment is to spend innovation budget on the system's短板 (echoic channel and fusion layer).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Dual-Modality Band Reuse on the Same Microphone

This is the most cost-effective design in the entire paper. Unlike all multi-modal solutions requiring additional sensors (cameras, EMG, ultrasound imaging probes), the second modality in this paper is completely parasitic on the existing acoustic link: the speaker is already a standard component of the headset (emitting ultrasound is just a change of use), and the microphone receives both speech and ultrasonic echoes relying on band separation. The additional cost is only an ESP32 and four small transducers, with a power consumption magnitude far lower than the image acquisition and inference of camera solutions. This design directly responds to the literature gap of "unexplored potential for SSI and vocal KWS fusion" and keeps the system's form factor with the low invasiveness of EchoSpeech (devices hidden under the lower edge of the lenses).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Reliability-Driven Adaptive Fusion (reliability-based fusion)

This is the core contribution at the method level. The designstarting point stems from two goals of fusion: to fully exploit the complementarity of both modalities, but not to be polluted by failed modalities (the paper gives a specific example: during silent recognition, the vocal pipeline predicts silence, and this "prediction" should not participate in fusion). The natural idea is to first calculate a reliability metric for the prediction vector of each modality, and then decide "whether to use it and how to use it" based on this.

**Reliability Metrics** (borrowed from the N-best approach verified effective in audio-visual speech fusion by Potamianos et al. [34]):

N-best log-likelihood difference (Eq. 1):
$$L_{m,t} = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{n=2}^{N} \log \frac{P(o_{m,t}|c_{m,t,1})}{P(o_{m,t}|c_{m,t,n})}$$

N-best log-likelihood divergence (Eq. 2):
$$D_{m,t} = \frac{2}{N(N-1)} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{n'=n+1}^{N} \log \frac{P(o_{m,t}|c_{m,t,n})}{P(o_{m,t}|c_{m,t,n')}}$$

Where $P(o_{m,t}|c_{m,t,n})$ is the likelihood of the $n$-th ranked class in the N-best list for modality $m$ at time $t$ given observation $o_{m,t}$. The division of labor for the two metrics: $L_{m,t}$ measures the average likelihood gap between the best class and the remaining candidates, i.e., the modality's **class discrimination ability**; $D_{m,t}$ is the average of pairwise likelihood ratios within the N-best, supplementing the relative structure between candidate classes not covered by the first metric. Only by combining the two can the modality reliability be assessed more reasonably—looking only at how much the first place leads would misjudge as reliable in ambiguous situations where "the first place leads but the rest are a mess."

**Gating and Fusion**: First, a dual-threshold mechanism is used to determine whether each modality is usable (Eq. 3):
$$R_{m,t} = (L_{m,t} > t_{l,m,t}) \wedge (D_{m,t} > t_{d,m,t})$$

Then, four cases are handled (Eq. 5): when both are reliable, log-domain linear weighting is performed $P(o_{f,t}|c) = P(o_{v,t}|c)^{\lambda_{v,t}} \cdot P(o_{e,t}|c)^{1-\lambda_{v,t}}$; if only vocal is reliable, take vocal; if only echoic is reliable, take echoic; if both are unreliable, output "no credible result." The significance of this branch structure is to **unify "selection" and "fusion" in a single framework**, automatically taking the "only echoic" branch in silent scenarios without any manual mode switching.

**Fusion Exponent** (Eq. 4):
$$\lambda_{v,t} = \frac{1}{1+\exp(-\sum_{i=1}^{4} w_i\, a_{i,t}\, d_{i,t})}$$

Where $d_t = [L_{v,t}, D_{v,t}, L_{e,t}, D_{e,t}]$ is the vector formed by splicing the four reliability metrics of the two modalities, and $w_i$ are the weights given to the metrics (used to align the dimensional differences of reliability metrics for different modalities). Notably, the paper specifically emphasizes: **these weights are only related to the two KWS models participating in fusion and do not change with data**—meaning that changing the dataset does not require retuning, only changing the model requires re-determination. This is an engineering advantage of this manual fusion scheme in deployment.

**Special Handling of Auxiliary Classes** ($a_{i,t}$) is the most skillful detail of the RB fusion. "silence" and "unknown" are just auxiliary classification targets; when a modality gives these predictions, it should refer more fully to the information from the other modality, i.e., reduce the reliability weight of that modality within a limited range. But there is one direction that needs to be reversed: **silence given by the vocal modality needs special treatment**—because there is almost no case where "speech can be produced without any facial muscle movement," vocal silence itself is very valuable strong evidence for the echoic side (mouth didn't move, so the motion interpretation on the echoic side should also converge to silence). $a_{i,t}$ is usually a four-dimensional vector of all 1s, switching to specific values only when at least one modality gives silence/unknown.

**Parameter Determination**: The entire strategy requires 8 parameters (4 thresholds + 4 exponent weights), using a genetic algorithm to perform global search on the complex objective function of "fusion accuracy." To address the weaknesses of genetic algorithms being time-consuming and dependent on the initial population, two engineering reinforcements were made: using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) for initialization to maximize parameter space coverage; running a few iterations on a small dataset to get an initial solution, and using it to replace the best individual in the initial population. Additionally, four parameters $a_{v,s}, a_{v,u}, a_{e,s}, a_{e,u}$ (corresponding to the correction amounts when the two modalities give silence/unknown, respectively) are quickly determined using grid search after the eight parameters are set.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: MLP Fusion

The second fusion route completely abandons manual features, using a naive MLP with only input, hidden, and output layers to learn the mapping from two modality prediction vectors to the fusion result (Eq. 6):
$$P(o_{f,t}|c) = \mathrm{MLP}([P(o_{v,t}|c),\ P(o_{e,t}|c)])$$

The input is the concatenation of the output vectors from the two pipelines, and training uses a self-recorded dataset with strict data partitioning (training data is strictly isolated from subsequent experimental data). The training augmentation design is very targeted, with each sample randomly selected from four processes: (1) no processing to keep it clear; (2) superimposing DEMAND environmental noise scaled by random SNR (corresponding to noise scenarios); (3) superimposing Google Speech Commands human voice noise scaled by a fixed coefficient (corresponding to interference from others scenarios); (4) discarding part of the vocal data (corresponding to silent scenarios—directly injecting "vocal channel missing" into the training distribution). Then the whole thing is multiplied by a random factor of 0.95-1.05 to simulate random noise. The loss uses cross-entropy, and the optimizer uses Adam. During inference, the prediction vectors of the two modalities are concatenated and passed through the MLP, taking the class with the highest probability in the output vector. The four augmentations correspond one-to-one with the four groups of experimental scenarios in the following text, indicating that the authors designed the augmentation according to the idea of "train it how it will look ugly in the experiment."

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Ablation-Driven Echoic Model Lightweighting

EchoSpeech used ResNet-18 with 11.22M parameters and 1.84G MAdd in the echoic channel, which is more than an order of magnitude heavier than the model in the vocal channel. The authors used ResNet-18 as a baseline and performed a two-factor ablation of "width pruning × depthwise separable convolution": width reduced to 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 of the original, and each width made into two versions of regular convolution and depthwise separable (DS) convolution, totaling eight models (see Table 1 in the experimental section). The reason for pruning width is structural: ResNet-18 was originally designed for tasks with heavier information loads like image classification; the information content of echoic features (Differential Echo Profile) cannot support this capacity. Reducing width reduces parameters and lowers the risk of overfitting; DS convolution splits standard convolution into two steps, depthwise and pointwise, significantly compressing parameters and computation with similar performance. Finally, **ResNet-18-1/4-DS** (109.9K parameters, 24.74M MAdd) was selected as the echoic pipeline network—compressing parameters by more than 100 times relative to ResNet-18, with the average WER rising by only 0.91 percentage points (Table 1).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Vs. EchoSpeech [55]**: The latter is a single echoic modality glasses-form silent recognition with a complete ResNet-18 backbone. This paper advances in three dimensions: modality from single to dual, model from 11.22M down to 109.9K, and scenario from "silent recognition" to "handling noise/silence/interference from others." The echoic feature extraction (FMCW + Echo Profile + differencing) and device configuration of this paper follow the verified conclusions of this line, belonging to incremental work standing on the predecessor's shoulders.
- **Vs. CELIP [56]**: CELIP's ultrasonic lip reading requires transducers to face the mouth directly, and the devices are relatively large; this paper's devices are on the lower edge of the lenses, more concealed, and do not require precise alignment.
- **Vs. Audio-Visual Speech Fusion [34,42,57]**: The N-best reliability metrics of RB fusion are directly inherited from Potamianos et al.'s audio-visual integration work, but the object of action changes from observation-level fusion in continuous speech recognition to probability vector-level gating fusion in KWS, and reliability correction is made for the two auxiliary classes specific to KWS, silence/unknown—this is a migration transformation from ASR fusion to KWS fusion, not a simple copy-paste.
- **Vs. Single-Modality Deep KWS (BC-ResNet [19], TC-ResNet [6], DC-ResNet [53], etc.)**: These SOTA models on public datasets degrade significantly under noise (supported by citations [7,30,35] in the paper). This paper does not compare single-model accuracy with them, but directly builds the vocal pipeline on one of them (BC-ResNet), arguing that "SOTA at the model level cannot save modality-level defects," and supplementing with a second modality.
- **Vs. All Single-Modality Systems**: No manual mode switching. Users do not need to select "voice mode/silent mode" first; the fusion layer automatically selects the path through reliability gating—this benefit at the interaction level is often more valuable than recognition accuracy in system papers.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

- **Self-Recorded Dual-Modality Dataset**: Recorded by 15 participants (2 female, 13 male, aged 20-27) wearing the device. Each person read 10 command words 30 times each, 25 auxiliary words 5 times each (labeled as unknown, helping the system reject non-command words), and additionally recorded silence data where they did not speak and kept their mouths still. Sampling rate 48 kHz (can retain information up to 24 kHz, exactly covering the ultrasonic echo frequency band). According to the recording protocol, there are 15×10×30=4500 command words, 15×25×5=1875 auxiliary words, and the number of silence data entries is not reported in the paper.
- **Google Speech Commands [50]**: The most commonly used open-source dataset in the KWS field. This paper has three uses for it: (1) The vocabulary is directly migrated from it, and the vocal model is jointly trained on "this large dataset + self-recorded data"—the authors explicitly state that the purpose of doing this is to make the comparison experiments between single-modality and dual-modality more reliable (the vocal baseline has eaten enough data dividends, so the dual-modality's win is not due to insufficient training of the vocal pipeline); (2) v2 human voice as the interference source for others in Experiment 4 (superimposed with a fixed power scaling coefficient to align the average power difference of the vocal components between the two datasets); (3) Human voice noise augmentation source for MLP fusion training.
- **DEMAND [45]**: Multi-channel environmental noise library, used for noise superposition in Experiment 2 and environmental noise augmentation in MLP training (superimposed after scaling by target SNR).
- **Ablation Subset**: Experiment 1 (echoic model ablation) only used about 1800 speech samples from 5 participants, 80% training / 20% testing, using random seeds to control partitioning and augmentation, and each model variant was trained and tested ten times to take the average to ensure reliability.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **WER (Eq. 7)**: Combines scores for substitution, deletion, and insertion errors. The deep reason for choosing it lies in the insertion item: for KWS, insertion errors are directly equivalent to false triggers, and false triggers are more damaging to always-on headset interaction than missed detections—so the bar charts in Experiments 3 and 4 are color-coded by S/D/I, allowing readers to see separately the process of insertion errors being eliminated by the fusion strategy. This is closer to real pain points than KWS papers that only report accuracy.
- **SNR (Eq. 8)**: The independent variable for noise experiments, ranging from -10 dB to 10 dB, covering the range from "noise ten times stronger than speech" to normal office noise.
- **Params and MAdd (Table 1)**: Parameters and multiply-add operations are counted using torchstat, quantifying "whether it can fit into the headset" into comparable numbers—this is the second coordinate axis of the ablation experiment; looking only at WER cannot complete the "lightweighting" narrative.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Noisy Environment (Experiment 2, Fig 6)**: DEMAND noise is superimposed on the data at SNRs from -10 to 10 dB, comparing four curves: vocal single-modality, echoic single-modality, RB fusion, and MLP fusion (same color shadows represent standard deviation). Conclusion: Under all SNR conditions, the WER of both fusion methods is lower than that of the two single modalities. At the strongest noise SNR=-10 dB, MLP fusion reduces WER by 15.68% and 16.57% relative to vocal and echoic single modalities, respectively (Fig 6 caption). The significance of this number is that the fusion benefit is greatest at the point of strongest noise—because noise mainly hits the vocal channel, the echoic channel (ultrasonic near-field reflection) is basically unaffected by human voice frequency band noise in the air, and the fusion layer can continuously take information from the clean side.

**Six Noise Scenario Subdivisions (Fig 7a, with Fig 5)**: RB fusion is compared one-to-one in six specific scenarios (including Domestic, Public, Meeting, Metro, etc.). The results present a clear structure: **in the first three scenarios (e.g., Domestic, dominated by high-frequency noise), the vocal modality is better than echoic; in the latter three (e.g., Public, dominated by low-frequency disturbance), echoic is better than vocal**; and regardless of which side is stronger, the WER of RB fusion is lower than that of the two single modalities in any scenario. Fig 5 provides a comparison of the spectral distribution of Meeting and Metro noise to support the fact that "noise frequency distribution varies by scenario." The value of this set of results is explaining "why fusion is necessary instead of choosing a better modality"—the strengths and weaknesses of the two modalities flip with the noise spectrum, no single modality dominates in all scenarios, and only fusion can eat the upper envelope of both sides. Additionally, the authors point out that RB fusion was chosen for scenario subdivision because its average WER is slightly higher than MLP fusion, making it a stricter test.

**Silent Scenario (Experiment 3, Fig 7b)**: Constructed by replacing the vocal segments of the test data with equal-length random silence (echoic segments retained), completely eliminating the vocal channel from an information perspective. Results: Except for segments that were originally silence, all errors in the vocal modality become deletion errors—the speech pipeline is completely blind; the echoic modality performance is unaffected, with errors still mainly substitutions and deletions; the WER of both fusion strategies closely follows the echoic modality and is significantly lower than the vocal modality. The conclusion is that the fusion strategy can still produce accurate results in an environment where "only echoic is available," which is a capability that vocal-only systems cannot possess in principle—users can freely choose to speak or remain silent, and the system requires no additional intervention.

**Interference from Others' Speech (Experiment 4, Fig 7c)**: GSC v2 human voice is superimposed into the vocal channel of the self-recorded data with a fixed scaling coefficient (the echoic channel is not polluted—ultrasonic echoes come from near-field skin reflection, and the energy of others' speech is concentrated in the human voice frequency band, which is naturally filtered out by the high-pass filter. This is the physical mechanism of this system's resistance to interference from others). Results: The error rate of the vocal modality is close to half, mainly substitutions and deletions (others' words are heard as command words or true command words are drowned out); the echoic modality is still relatively reliable, with a certain proportion of substitution errors and very few deletions; the fusion modality significantly suppresses the high error rate of the vocal modality, with substitution errors greatly reduced and insertion errors almost zeroed out. Zeroing out insertion errors directly responds to Pain Point 3—in high human voice scenarios like meetings and classrooms, the system is almost never falsely triggered by human voice.

**System-Level Verification (Sec 4.7)**: The authors also developed a game to verify the experimental conclusions and provided a real-time demo video. Compared to vocal KWS methods, the dual-modality is more stable under various noises, can filter out interference from others, and supports silence; compared to silent speech methods, the dual-modality inherits the capabilities of SSI, and in scenarios where SSI degrades significantly (excessive noise, vigorous physical activity, ultrasonic interference), it can seamlessly switch back to vocal recognition—avoiding potential system failure and saving the need for manual mode switching. The paper does not report quantitative indicators such as latency and power consumption for this demo.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Table 1 gives a complete comparison of eight echoic model variants (WER ± standard deviation / parameters / MAdd):

| Model | WER | Params | MAdd |
|---|---|---|---|
| ResNet-18 | 5.06% ± 0.650 | 11.22M | 1.84G |
| ResNet-18-1/2 | 5.47% ± 0.466 | 2.820M | 468.4M |
| ResNet-18-1/4 | 5.81% ± 0.639 | 712.6K | 121.11M |
| ResNet-18-1/8 | 7.69% ± 0.528 | 182.0K | 32.28M |
| ResNet-18-DS | 5.33% ± 0.539 | 1.484M | 264.1M |
| ResNet-18-1/2-DS | 5.56% ± 0.714 | 394.0K | 79.99M |
| ResNet-18-1/4-DS | 5.97% ± 0.573 | 109.9K | 24.74M |
| ResNet-18-1/8-DS | 8.00% ± 1.260 | 33.22K | 8.93M |

Training configuration: SGD optimizer, learning rate linearly warm-ups from 0 to 0.1 for the first 50 epochs, then trained for a total of 1000 epochs with cosine annealing; augmentation includes random noise, random padding, and background noise superposition; each variant was run ten times with different random seeds to take the average.

Three layers of structure can be read from the table:

1. **Sweet spot at 1/4 width**: For the non-DS series, from 1/2 to 1/4, WER only rises from 5.47% to 5.81% (+0.34 percentage points), while parameters drop from 2.820M to 712.6K; but from 1/4 to 1/8, WER jumps from 5.81% to 7.69% (+1.88 percentage points), and the marginal cost increases sharply—pruning to 1/8 starts to damage expressive power. The DS series also deteriorates at 1/8 and the variance increases significantly (the standard deviation of 1/8-DS is the largest in the table), indicating that models that are too small cannot even maintain training stability.
2. **High cost-performance of DS convolution**: Under the same width, the MAdd of the DS version is about half or less of the non-DS version (e.g., 1/2 width 468.4M → 79.99M), with a WER cost of only 0.1-0.2 percentage points (5.47% → 5.56%).
3. **Global trade-off of final selection**: Relative to the original ResNet-18, ResNet-18-1/4-DS has parameters compressed from 11.22M to 109.9K (compression over 100 times), MAdd compressed from 1.84G to 24.74M (compression about 74 times), and the average WER rises by only 0.91 percentage points (5.06% → 5.97%, Table 1). The authors argue based on this that the model can be efficiently deployed on XR headsets—for a pipeline that needs to run always-on, a 74-fold reduction in computing power directly exchanges for power and latency budgets.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed and physically implemented a vocal-echoic dual-modality KWS system**: Built a complete system on Microsoft HoloLens 2 using two pairs of mass-produced small speakers/microphones plus an ESP32, expanding the applicable scenarios of KWS from "speaking in quiet environments" to noisy environments, silent environments, and environments with interference from others. The key insight is to utilize the frequency band blank between speech (100 Hz-5 kHz) and ultrasonic echoes (above 17 kHz) to reuse both modalities on a single 48 kHz microphone, without introducing cameras or skin-attached sensors.
2. **Ablation-driven echoic model lightweighting**: Through systematic ablation of "width pruning × depthwise separable convolution" (Table 1), the echoic channel was compressed from 11.22M parameter ResNet-18 to 109.9K ResNet-18-1/4-DS (over 100 times compression), with WER paying only 0.91 percentage points, significantly reducing the computing power requirements on the headset side.
3. **Two fusion strategies and their system verification**: The reliability fusion of the manual feature route (N-best log-likelihood difference/divergence gating + log-domain adaptive weighting, including reliability correction for silence/unknown auxiliary classes) and the learning route of MLP fusion, consistently outperformed single modalities in all noise SNR, six scenario, silent, and interference from others experiments (at the strongest noise point, MLP fusion reduced WER by 15.68%/16.57%, Fig 6).
4. **Reproducibility and Experientiality**: Complete set of open-source code (github.com/caizhuojiang/VE-KWS), demo video, and gamified verification, which is an important part of credibility for HCI/system work.

A transferable engineering judgment: When the failure conditions of two information sources are complementary (vocal is afraid of noise and silence, echoic is afraid of vigorous movement), fusion with reliability gating can simultaneously capture the upper envelope of both sides without setting manual mode switching; and the handling of details such as "down-weighting auxiliary class outputs (silence/unknown), but treating vocal silence as strong evidence specially" in the fusion layer often determines whether the fusion system adds or subtracts points in boundary scenarios.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Impact of Physical Activity**: Walking increases the echoic modality WER by about 50%, and head shaking increases it by about 33% (the vocal modality is only slightly affected, Sec. 5). The mechanism is not hard to understand: the geometry of the headset relative to the mouth shakes when walking, and whole-body motion also pollutes the "motion" signal in the Differential Echo Profile, and the static suppression at the feature level cannot control this low-frequency shaking. Fusion optimization including activity data can alleviate this (WER after fusion is still lower than vocal single-modality), but physical activity indeed weakens the advantage of this system relative to conventional vocal KWS.
- **Ultrasonic Mutual Interference**: Static ultrasonic sources of the same device have negligible impact in multiple directions and distances (differencing processing has a suppressing effect on static interference); but **moving** ultrasonic sources have significant impact—averaging an increase in WER of about 7% at a distance of 1 m, and stronger when closer (Sec. 5). Movement breaks the premise of differencing suppression (interference is no longer approximately static), which is a principled blind spot of the differencing scheme, not directly solved in the paper.
- **Auditory Risk at the Lower Edge of the Frequency Band**: The lower edge of the 17-20 kHz band is close to the upper edge of the audible threshold for young people; the paper collectively calls it ultrasonic but does not report any audibility human factors tests; whether there is perceptible whistling or fatigue for users around 20 years old is a verification that must be supplemented before productization.
- **Additional Hardware Not Zeroed**: Although all are mass-produced small parts, the system still requires an add-on board plus four transducers hung externally on commercial headsets, and has not achieved zero increment (this is also one of the future directions listed by the paper itself).

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Narrow Population Coverage**: Only 2 females out of 15 participants, aged 20-27. The sensitivity of echoic channel geometric parameters (e.g., the average vertical distance of 3.9 cm from microphone to mouth) to face shape, gender, and age was not analyzed in layers, and speaker invariance was not specifically evaluated.
- **Small Vocabulary Scale**: 10 command words plus 25 auxiliary words, all short command words from GSC; longer commands with more syllables and keyword detection in continuous speech flow were not tested.
- **Simulation-Style Difficulty Creation**: Noise is superimposed by DEMAND, interference from others is superimposed by GSC v2 human voice, and silence is simulated by replacing vocal segments with random silence, all of which are offline data-level simulations, without verification of real-scenario online recording; SNR is only tested down to -10 dB.
- **Constructive Bias in Silent Scenarios**: The most debatable point—all self-recorded data is spoken reading, and "silence" is simulated by deleting the vocal channel, while the echoic channel corresponds to lip movements during spoken reading. The lip movement amplitude and motion patterns of true silent pronunciation (moving the mouth without sound) are not the same as spoken reading (silent pronunciation usually has smaller and less complete amplitude), and the paper does not report the performance of the echoic model on the distribution of true silent pronunciation.
- **Missing System Indicators**: End-to-end latency, power consumption, and actual inference frame rate on ESP32/headset are not reported in the paper; "successful real-time demo" is only a qualitative conclusion; the runtime overhead comparison between RB fusion (genetic algorithm parameter search) and MLP fusion (online forward pass) is not reported; structural hyperparameters such as MLP hidden layer width and search time for genetic algorithms are not given.
- **Result Presentation Leans on Figures, Not Tables**: The large number of conclusions in Fig 6/Fig 7 are only curves and bar charts, without numerical tables for each scenario, making precise reproduction and horizontal comparison difficult.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

The paper lists three directions itself: studying the impact of introducing more modalities into KWS on performance and user experience, improving the performance of the echoic modality model (the current 1/4-DS 5.97% WER still has a gap relative to the vocal side SOTA, which also directly limits the upper limit of silent scenarios), and further reducing the introduction of additional hardware.

Combining the above analysis, there are several directions worth supplementing: First, motion robustness requires fundamental governance at the feature or geometric level (e.g., inertial sensor-assisted motion compensation, adaptive baseline differencing), rather than just relying on the fusion layer as a fallback; Second, suppression of moving ultrasonic source interference (interference cancellation in scenarios where differencing fails); Third, collecting real silent pronunciation data to re-evaluate the echoic channel, which is the key experiment for whether the silent selling point holds; Fourth, leveraging the property that RB fusion weights "are only related to the model and do not change with data" to do cross-scenario transfer and online adaptation; Fifth, combining with speaker verification (wake word + speaker verification) to upgrade the defense against "interference from others" from the frequency band physical layer to the voiceprint layer; Sixth, quantization and actual power consumption measurement for edge-side deployment, completing the last link of the "lightweighting" narrative.
