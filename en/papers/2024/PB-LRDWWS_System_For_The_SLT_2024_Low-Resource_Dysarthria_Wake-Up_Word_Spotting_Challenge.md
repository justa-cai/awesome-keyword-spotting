- **Date**: September 2024 (arXiv:2409.04799, v2 updated on December 6, 2024; System paper for the SLT 2024 Low-Resource Dysarthria Wake-Up Word Spotting Challenge)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04799
- **Keywords**: Dysarthric Speech, Wake-Up Word Spotting, Keyword Spotting, Prototype Classification, HuBERT Fine-tuning, Supervised Contrastive Learning, Low-Resource Personalized Modeling

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Dysarthria is typically caused by cerebral palsy, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), or other neurological diseases. Patients suffer from impaired control over their articulatory organs, resulting in speech that is indistinct, lacks fluency, and exhibits acoustic characteristics significantly different from typical speech. Moreover, this variability is highly individualized—the paper explicitly states in Section 1.3 that the articulation patterns of dysarthric speakers exhibit substantial variability due to differences in age, etiology, severity, and speaking style. Meanwhile, these neurological conditions are often accompanied by physical paralysis, making it difficult for patients to interact with smart devices using keyboards or touchscreens. Therefore, speech becomes a critical channel for them to access technological convenience equally; speech-controlled devices are not merely a luxury for this group but a necessary assistive tool.

The paper’s justification for task positioning is noteworthy: Dysarthric Speech Recognition (DSR) systems allow patients to control smart devices with simple command words or phrases. However, keeping a DSR system running continuously incurs two engineering costs—increased power consumption and a higher false alarm rate. Therefore, the paper advocates using a Keyword Spotting (KWS) system as the "initiator" for interaction between dysarthric patients and smart devices: a lightweight wake-up stage filters out the vast majority of non-wake-up speech, and only after wake-up is triggered does the heavier recognition or dialogue process start. This positioning aligns with the motivation of typical KWS (saving power, low false alarm rate), but the contradiction lies in the fact that the mainstream research route for typical KWS is minimal architecture combined with low power consumption (as cited in Section 1.1, DS-CNN achieves 95.4% accuracy on the Speech Commands dataset, along with subsequent deployment work on embedded devices and two-stage streaming keyword detection methods). This route is entirely built on the assumption that "speaker speech is typical and data is sufficient," whereas the dysarthria scenario overturns both assumptions—speech is atypical, and registered speech for each target speaker is very limited.

The SLT 2024 LRDWWS Challenge is the first competition specifically established for the dysarthric KWS task. The task involves detecting and recognizing ten common wake-up words: participants can only use limited registered speech from each target dysarthric speaker to build a KWS system, and then use this system to classify the evaluation speech from the same speaker. To make the task feasible, the challenge made several adjustments to lower the difficulty: registered and evaluation speech were recorded in controlled environments, ensuring no background noise; the task focuses on non-streaming speech. Even so, the core difficulties remaining include: severe class imbalance between keywords and non-keywords in the registered data; the non-keyword class is not defined by specific words or phrases (it is an open anti-class); and the significant individual differences inherent in dysarthric speech itself.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

- **Typical KWS Architectures are Incompatible**: Lightweight architectures like DS-CNN pursue a balance between power consumption and accuracy, relying on large-scale, typically articulated keyword data for training. The variability of dysarthric speech means that a speaker-independent fixed classifier is unlikely to cover the distribution of the patient population. Although the paper does not directly provide failure metrics for such models on dysarthric data, we can infer from the baseline system's total score of 0.3112 on Test-A (where FRR is as high as 0.2725, Table 2) that errors are concentrated on "missed wake-ups," which is a direct manifestation of the deviation between dysarthric and typical speech distributions.
- **Personalized DSR Relies on Large Amounts of Fine-tuning Data**: To achieve optimal performance, personalized DSR models usually require sufficient fine-tuning on the target speaker. Dysarthric patients can provide only a small amount of registered speech; directly applying this route easily leads to overfitting or even training failure. The paper realistically records the "Model Crash" experiment of SIC-1 (DC train + CTC) in Table 1, indicating that class imbalance combined with recognition-type loss directly causes training collapse. This is not a theoretical concern but an empirical fact.
- **Unverified Gaps in Transferring PB-DSR to KWS**: The authors' previous PB-DSR work (Interspeech 2024, Reference [15]) mainly relies on Prototype Classification (PB-C) to allow existing DSR models to quickly adapt to unseen dysarthric speakers without extensive fine-tuning, and this work suggests that "fine-tuning with target speaker speech first, then applying PB-C" yields the best performance. However, PB-DSR faces command word recognition (CTC loss, sequence recognition), while the KWS task has structural differences such as keyword/non-keyword class imbalance and undefined non-keyword classes. Whether PB-DSR experience can be directly transferred, and how loss functions and data augmentation should be re-selected during transfer, remain open questions.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The first challenge is how to establish reliable wake-up word detection capability for a previously unseen dysarthric speaker under conditions of extremely limited registered speech—utilizing limited registered data for personalization while avoiding overfitting and training collapse. The second challenge is how to combine and select training losses (CTC, cross-entropy, supervised contrastive learning) and data augmentation (TTS synthesis, multi-stage data merging) when the non-keyword class has no vocabulary definition and classes are highly imbalanced; the paper needs to provide evidence chains through ablation studies. The third challenge is the choice of classification method: directly predicting with the fine-tuned model, using Prototype Classification (PB-C), or using KNN-style nearest neighbor classification (KNN-C). The matching relationship between three inference methods and different loss combinations needs to be systematically answered. The paper ultimately uses a system of "HuBERT feature extractor with three-stage fine-tuning plus prototype classification," achieving a score of 0.009801 on the final Test-B of the challenge and securing second place (Section 5 Conclusion).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The PB-LRDWWS system (Figure 2) consists of two components: a **dysarthric speech content feature extractor** responsible for extracting features and building prototypes, and a **prototype-based classification method (PB-C)**. The inference process is divided into two steps: the first step builds prototypes—using the feature extractor to extract features from the target dysarthric speaker's registered speech, and averaging the sample features of each class to obtain the class prototype; the second step performs classification—using the same extractor to extract test features from evaluation speech, calculating the cosine similarity between the test features and each of the eleven prototypes (ten wake-up word classes plus one non-keyword class), and assigning the class of the prototype with the highest similarity as the classification result.

The core motivation for this design is explicitly stated in Section 2.1: the effectiveness of the PB-LRDWWS system depends on the **representativeness of the prototypes**. Classification relies solely on simple metric learning (calculating cosine similarity); only when the prototypes themselves are distinguishable and representative can evaluation speech be correctly classified. Since prototypes are the average of intra-class sample features, a robust feature extractor is the foundation of the entire system—this is why the paper devotes a large portion of its篇幅 to "how to fine-tune a good feature extractor" rather than focusing on the design of the classification head. This chain of motivation is worth dissecting: the extreme individual differences in dysarthric articulation patterns mean that the same wake-up word for different speakers is almost impossible to align in the raw acoustic space; the representations provided by the SSL pre-trained model HuBERT have already learned the abstract structure of speech content on large-scale unlabeled speech. The goal of fine-tuning is to further shape this representation space into one that "clusters by wake-up word content and remains invariant to speaker differences"; averaging within classes to construct prototypes is equivalent to performing parameter-free class center estimation in this space, and cosine similarity is a scale-invariant metric. The entire method compresses the cost of "personalization" from training a large model to "one average vector per class," which is the most economical form of personalization under low-resource constraints.

The construction of the feature extractor adopts **three-stage fine-tuning** (Figure 1), forming a progressive curriculum from typical speech to the dysarthria domain to the target speaker:

- **Stage 1 (SIC Model)**: Fine-tune the pre-trained HuBERT using non-dysarthric speech (Control data) to obtain a speaker-independent control model SIC. The significance of this step is to let HuBERT first learn the classification task itself of "ten wake-up words plus one non-keyword," stabilizing the task structure on clean, typical, and relatively abundant data.
- **Stage 2 (SID Model)**: Continue fine-tuning on top of SIC using speech from multiple dysarthric speakers (Uncontrol data, note that it **does not include** the target speaker) to obtain a speaker-independent dysarthric model SID. This step is domain adaptation: letting the model learn the common features of dysarthric speech.
- **Stage 3 (SDD Model)**: Fine-tune on top of SID using the target dysarthric speaker's own speech (Target data) to obtain a speaker-dependent dysarthric model SDD, whose HuBERT part serves as the final content feature extractor. This step is true personalization. However, since the previous two stages have already learned the task and domain, the target speaker data only needs to complete the "final step of convergence," significantly diluting the risk of overfitting.

The sequential design of the three stages implies a tightly coupled logic: if one directly fine-tunes the pre-trained model with the small amount of data from the target speaker, the model has to learn three things simultaneously: "what the task is, what dysarthric speech looks like, and what this speaker looks like," which the data simply cannot support. The three stages split these three tasks; the first two are solved using large-scale data, and the last one is what small data is good at. The data in Table 1 supports this design—even if the training data does not include speakers from Dev4, the SID stage brings significant improvement (see the ablation section).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Evaluation Metrics** (Officially defined by the challenge, Equation (1)): The total score is the sum of the False Rejection Rate and the False Acceptance Rate,

$$\text{Score} = FRR + FAR = \frac{N_{FR}}{N_{wake}} + \frac{N_{FA}}{N_{non-wake}}$$

where $N_{wake}$ is the number of samples containing keywords in the evaluation set, $N_{non-wake}$ is the number of samples not containing keywords, $N_{FR}$ is the number of samples containing keywords but not correctly detected, and $N_{FA}$ is the number of samples not containing keywords but judged as keywords. Lower scores are better.

**Cross-Entropy Loss** (Equation (2)): The model distinguishes between ten keyword classes and one non-keyword class, totaling eleven classes:

$$L_{CE} = \sum_{i \in I} \left( y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - \hat{y}_i) \right)$$

where $i \in I = \{1, ..., N\}$ is the speech sample index, $y_i$ is the true label, and $\hat{y}_i$ is the model's predicted label. Note that the paper writes this as an expansion of two terms in binary form.

**CTC Loss** settings (Section 3.2 Loss settings): The model dictionary contains special tokens `<sos>`, `<eos>`, `<pad>`, and blank, as well as ten keyword IDs (0-9) and one non-keyword ID (-1). That is, CTC models wake-up word spotting as a micro-recognition task, where each wake-up word is an output symbol.

**Supervised Contrastive Learning (SCL) and AddSCL Combination** (Equations (3)(4)(5)): Within a training batch, samples with the same label form positive pairs, and samples with different labels form negative pairs. The SCL loss is:

$$L_{SCL} = \sum_{i \in I} \frac{1}{|P(i)|} \sum_{p \in P(i)} -\log \frac{\exp(x_i x_p)/\tau}{\sum_{a \in A(i)} \exp(x_i x_p)/\tau}$$

where $x_i$ is the feature extracted by HuBERT for speech $i$, $P(i)$ is the set of indices of all positive samples for sample $i$ (samples representing the same word as $i$), $A(i)$ is the set of indices of all samples except $i$, and $\tau$ is the temperature hyperparameter, set to 0.07 in the paper. AddSCL refers to adding SCL to the main loss: $L_{Total1} = L_{CTC} + L_{SCL}$ (Equation (3)), or $L_{Total2} = L_{CE} + L_{SCL}$ (Equation (4)). The mechanism of SCL is to apply pressure directly in the feature space: pulling similar features closer and pushing dissimilar features apart. This is geometrically isomorphic to the inference method of prototype classification ("average within class, compare distances between classes"), which is the deep reason why the paper includes SCL in its investigation.

**Geometry of Prototype Classification**: Each class prototype $c_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i \in C_k} x_i$ (average of intra-class features), and the class determination for test feature $x$ is $\arg\max_k \cos(x, c_k)$. The classifier has no learnable parameters; all discriminative power comes from the quality of the organization of the feature space itself—this is the mathematical root of the design judgment that "the feature extractor determines everything."

**Prototypes Use Only the First Frame Feature**: Section 3.2 Classification settings explicitly records that, following the practice of PB-DSR, the paper compared two settings: building prototypes using full HuBERT features versus using only the **first frame** of HuBERT features. It was found that the latter was **consistently** better, so the system adopts the first frame feature to build prototypes. The paper does not report the specific numbers for this comparison, nor does it explain the reason. A reasonable speculation (marked as this paper's speculation, not the paper's conclusion) is: the fine-tuning configuration follows fairseq's `base_10h.yaml`, where the classification head is trained on frame-level features, and the sentence-level label is bound to a specific frame. The alignment between the first frame feature and the classification geometry might be the best; additionally, dysarthric speech has unstable duration, and averaging all frames might mix blurred, elongated segments into the prototype, thereby polluting the class center.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Domain Curriculum Design of Three-Stage Progressive Fine-tuning

The innovation does not lie in "fine-tuning HuBERT" itself, but in the **sequencing of fine-tuning**: typical speech (SIC) → multi-speaker dysarthric speech (SID) → target speaker (SDD). Each stage solves a problem with the highest uncertainty, and the subsequent stage inherits the solution of the previous stage as initialization. This is essentially stacking curriculum learning and domain adaptation on top of SSL representations: SSL provides general speech content representations (the paper uses the pre-trained weights of `chinese-hubert-base` released by TencentGameMate), SIC provides task structure, SID provides domain commonalities, and SDD completes speaker personalization. Compared to "one-step fine-tuning using target speaker data" or "only fine-tuning with multi-speaker dysarthric data and then directly applying PB-C," the three-stage approach minimizes the burden on limited registered data—the leap from SIC to SID in Table 1 (e.g., from 0.23846 for SIC-6 to 0.15653 for SID-1) proves that the second stage learned the common features of dysarthric speech, and this batch of data did not contain the Dev4 speaker at all, indicating that transferable domain knowledge was learned rather than memorization.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Combination Screening of Loss Functions and Data Augmentation and Mechanism Analysis

The paper systematically examines all meaningful combinations of two types of augmentations and three types of losses, and explains seemingly contradictory experimental phenomena using **differences in task nature** (Section 4.1): Adding TTS synthetic data to the training set can alleviate the impact of class imbalance on CTC training (SIC-1 crashes directly, but after adding TTS, SIC-2 can train normally, with a Dev4 score of 0.49389), but it harms performance under cross-entropy (SIC-5's 0.27611 degrades to SIC-4's 0.31347 after adding TTS, Table 1). The explanation given by the paper is: CTC is used for recognition tasks and can benefit from diverse samples; cross-entropy is used for classification tasks and is sensitive to huge differences within the same class. TTS data is synthesized using FastSpeech2 trained on 174 speakers from AISHELL-3, covering all Chinese keyword speech, belonging to typical articulation. When mixed into dysarthric classes, it increases the variance of the intra-class distribution. Recognition-type loss is insensitive to this (diverse samples are beneficial for aligning path diversity), while classification-type loss suffers directly. This explanation elevates the question of "whether data augmentation is effective" from an empirical issue to a principled issue of "whether the augmented data matches the task assumptions of the loss function," which is one of the most valuable findings in the paper's methodology to paraphrase. SCL brings improvement under both main losses (from 0.49389 to 0.39375 under CTC, and from 0.27611 to 0.23846 under CE, Table 1), and the reason is similarly attributed to its ability to directly shape the feature space.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Matching Rules Between Loss Functions and Classification Methods

The paper compares three classification settings (Section 3.2): PB-C (build prototypes by averaging within classes, take the maximum cosine similarity); KNN-C (calculate the cosine similarity between the test feature and the feature of **every** registered speech sample of the target speaker, and take the label of the most similar sample, i.e., 1-NN); Model prediction (directly predict the class using the corresponding SDD model). The results in Table 2 reveal a clear matching rule: under CTC + AddSCL fine-tuning, KNN-C is optimal (Test-A 0.0626, Test-B 0.011812); under CE fine-tuning, PB-C is optimal (Test-A 0.0624, Test-B 0.009801). The paper's mechanistic explanation is: SCL acts directly on sample features, pulling similar samples closer and pushing dissimilar samples apart, so the feature of each registered sample itself is already sufficiently discriminative, and nearest neighbors can benefit directly; whereas cross-entropy as a classification loss does not directly shape feature geometry, single sample features may be noisy, and constructing prototypes by averaging within classes instead plays a role in smoothing and denoising for stability. This rule indicates that **inference methods should be matched with the geometric properties of the training loss**, rather than comparing which classifier is better in isolation—looking at any single row of Table 2 alone would lead to one-sided conclusions.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to typical KWS (DS-CNN, two-stage streaming detection): The latter pursues lightweight low power consumption, speaker independence, closed-set vocabulary, and typical articulation assumptions; PB-LRDWWS goes the opposite way—using a heavyweight SSL model like HuBERT for features, personalizing in a speaker-dependent manner, specifically targeting atypical articulation, and explicitly stating that the task is non-streaming. What is sacrificed is exactly what typical KWS values most: edge-side power consumption, in exchange for usability in dysarthria scenarios. Compared to personalized DSR (gradual fine-tuning of acoustic models): This paper inherits the fine-tuning idea but converges personalization to the "building prototypes" step, requiring only feature extraction and cosine similarity during inference. Compared to the authors' own PB-DSR: PB-DSR mainly relies on PB-C to achieve training-free rapid adaptation and uses CTC loss; after migrating these insights to KWS, this paper re-did a systematic comparison at the loss level (finding that the combination of CE with SCL is the optimal point), added the engineering detail of first-frame prototypes, and turned the three-stage fine-tuning from a suggestion in PB-DSR into an explicit process. Compared to the challenge baseline (Table 2): The baseline scored 0.3112 on Test-A (FAR 0.0387, FRR 0.2725) and 0.130306 on Test-B (FAR 0.028639, FRR 0.101667). The best system in this paper scored 0.009801 on Test-B (FAR 0.004801, FRR 0.005000)—converting the numbers in Table 2, the total score is about one-thirteenth of the baseline, FRR dropped from 0.101667 to 0.005000 (about one-twentieth), and FAR dropped from 0.028639 to 0.004801 (about one-sixth), indicating that the improvement mainly comes from reducing "missed wake-ups," hitting the pain point of difficult recognition of dysarthric speech.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

All data comes from the official datasets released in the three stages of the LRDWWS Challenge (Section 3.1), and **speakers in all datasets do not overlap**. Stage 1 released two datasets: LRDWWS train.zip, containing control speaker speech (DC) and speech from multiple dysarthric speakers (DUC); LRDWWS dev.zip, containing speech from four dysarthric speakers (Dev4). Stage 2 released LRDWWS test.zip, containing speech from ten dysarthric speakers (Test-A). Stage 3 released LRDWWS test b.zip, containing speech from ten dysarthric speakers (Test-B), and the evaluation speech of Test-B is **unlabeled**—only submitted to the challenge evaluation system for scoring. Data partitioning follows the settings of the baseline repository: the split of DC and DUC is consistent with the Baseline; for Dev4, Test-A, and Test-B, the registered speech of each target dysarthric speaker is used for training and validation, and evaluation speech serves as the test set. Regarding TTS data augmentation, FastSpeech2 multi-speaker TTS was trained using speech from 174 speakers in AISHELL-3 to synthesize all Chinese keyword speech, which was added to the training set; another merged training set, Merge train (TTS data + DC training set + DUC training set), was constructed. **The paper does not report the speech duration, number of sentences, or total number of speakers (specific number of people in DC/DUC) for each dataset**, which is an information gap for reproduction. Training hyperparameters (Section 3.2 Basic training settings): Fine-tuning pre-trained HuBERT under the Fairseq framework, modifying the `base_10h.yaml` configuration, learning rate $10^{-5}$, warmup steps 32,000, and stopping if the training loss does not decrease for 10 consecutive epochs.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric uses the total score Score = FRR + FAR officially defined by the challenge (Equation (1)). The rationale for selection can be understood from the error cost structure of wake-up word tasks: A high FRR means the system does not respond when the patient says the wake-up word, locking the interaction channel, which is fatal for dysarthric users who rely on speech as their only interaction method; a high FAR means the system frequently triggers falsely, causing the subsequently mounted DSR/dialogue modules to be awakened repeatedly for no reason, resulting in a double loss of power consumption and user experience. Adding the two with equal weights means the task requires suppressing both directions simultaneously, not allowing one to be sacrificed for the other—this also explains why the paper reports the decomposition of FAR and FRR simultaneously (Table 2). The extreme asymmetry of the baseline system's FAR and FRR (0.0387 vs 0.2725 on Test-A) indicates that on data with class imbalance and a majority of non-keywords, the model naturally tends to under-report keywords, and the official metric's equal-weight design exactly penalizes this bias.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table 2 provides all comparisons. Baseline (fine-tuning setting Baseline, no classification setting details): Test-A score 0.3112 (FAR 0.0387, FRR 0.2725), Test-B score 0.130306 (FAR 0.028639, FRR 0.101667). This paper's two fine-tuning settings each pair with three classification settings:

**Fine-tuning Setting 1 (Fine-tune SID-3 + CTC + AddSCL)**, i.e., performing stage 3 fine-tuning on SID-3 using Test-A speaker data: PB-C scored 0.0643 (FAR 0.0043, FRR 0.0600) on Test-A and 0.012249 (FAR 0.004749, FRR 0.007500) on Test-B; KNN-C scored 0.0626 (FAR 0.0051, FRR 0.0575) on Test-A and 0.011812 (FAR 0.004312, FRR 0.007500) on Test-B, which is the best among the three classification methods for this setting; Model prediction scored 0.0645 (FAR 0.0045, FRR 0.0600) on Test-A and 0.014312 (FAR 0.004312, FRR 0.010000) on Test-B.

**Fine-tuning Setting 2 (Fine-tune SID-2 + CE)**: PB-C scored 0.0624 (FAR 0.0049, FRR 0.0575) on Test-A and 0.009801 (FAR 0.004801, FRR 0.005000) on Test-B, which is the **optimal point among all combinations** and the final submitted score; KNN-C scored 0.0696 (FAR 0.0046, FRR 0.0650) on Test-A and 0.017197 (FAR 0.004697, FRR 0.012500) on Test-B, which is the worst under the CE setting; Model prediction scored 0.0648 (FAR 0.0048, FRR 0.0600) on Test-A and 0.014851 (FAR 0.004851, FRR 0.010000) on Test-B.

Observing horizontally, there are three points worth expanding. First, all six combinations represent an order-of-magnitude improvement over the baseline, and the trends on Test-A and Test-B are completely consistent (Test-B results are returned by the official evaluation system, not local evaluation), indicating that the conclusions are not overfitting to a specific set of speakers. Second, the scores of all methods on Test-B are significantly lower than on Test-A (e.g., PB-C drops from 0.0624 to 0.009801). The paper does not deeply analyze the reason, which may be related to the fact that the Test-B speakers' conditions or recording conditions are relatively more standardized, belonging to an **unreported** explanation gap in the paper. Third, looking at the error decomposition, the FAR (0.004801) and FRR (0.005000) of the optimal system are already close to balanced, indicating that the system no longer relies on bias to achieve a low total score. As an SOTA reference, this system ranked second in the challenge's Test-B (Section 5), but **the score and method of the champion system are not reported**, making further comparison impossible.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

Table 1 (Speaker-independent model comparison on Dev4, sorted by score descending) provides four key findings:

**Finding 1: Class imbalance causes CTC training to crash directly, while cross-entropy remains stable.** SIC-1 (DC train + CTC) resulted in Model Crash, while SIC-5 (DC train + CE) trained normally to get 0.27611. The reason is that the training data has an imbalance between keyword and non-keyword classes, and CTC's alignment mechanism becomes unstable under this imbalance. This explains why the paper's main line eventually shifted to CE—engineering-wise, "stable training" is a harder constraint than "theoretically more elegant."

**Finding 2: TTS augmentation is a lifesaver for CTC but poison for CE.** After adding TTS, CTC went from crashing to 0.49389 (SIC-2), but CE degraded from 0.27611 to 0.31347 (SIC-4 vs SIC-5). The paper explains this using "recognition tasks benefit from sample diversity, while classification tasks are sensitive to large intra-class differences" (Section 4.1), the mechanism of which has been analyzed above. This cross-effect is the most cautionary experimental conclusion in the entire paper: **the sign of the benefit of data augmentation depends on the task assumptions of the loss function**, and the two must be ablated jointly, not evaluated separately.

**Finding 3: SCL is effective for both main losses.** Under CTC, SIC-2 to SIC-3 improved from 0.49389 to 0.39375; under CE, SIC-5 to SIC-6 improved from 0.27611 to 0.23846. This is attributed to SCL pulling similar samples closer and pushing dissimilar samples apart, directly improving the representativeness and distinguishability of features—these are exactly the two dimensions of prototype quality. However, a detail not expanded upon in the paper is worth noting: after entering the SID stage, SID-1 (with AddSCL, 0.15653) and SID-2 (without, 0.15625) are almost identical, indicating that the feature restructuring brought by the domain adaptation stage may have partially covered the geometric advantages established by SCL in the SIC stage, and the benefit of SCL has stage boundaries.

**Finding 4: The SID stage learns commonalities of dysarthria rather than memorization.** In the three groups of comparisons SIC-6→SID-1 (0.23846→0.15653), SIC-5→SID-2 (0.27611→0.15625), and SIC-3→SID-3 (0.39375→0.12507), the Uncontrol data did not contain Dev4 speakers, but the Dev4 scores dropped significantly (converting Table 1 numbers, the drop is about one-third to two-thirds), proving that the second stage learned dysarthric common features that can generalize to new speakers. Among the three groups, SID-3 (based on SIC-3, following the Merge train + CTC + AddSCL route) became the best model in Table 1 with 0.12507, but in the complete pipeline of the final Table 2, the CE route (successor of SID-2) actually won out, indicating that the optimal choice on Dev4 and the optimal choice on the final Test are **not consistent**—if one only looks at Table 1 to select the model, one would choose the wrong direction, which is also the value of the paper presenting both two-stage evaluations.

**Finding 5: Matching rules between loss functions and classification methods** (Table 2, analyzed in detail above): CTC+SCL pairs with KNN-C, CE pairs with PB-C. Combined with the finding that "building prototypes with full features is inferior to building with the first frame" (Section 3.2, numbers not reported by the paper), these together constitute the complete conclusion on the inference side.

## Main Contributions

1. **Constructed and submitted the PB-LRDWWS system**, solving the dysarthric KWS task with the combination of "three-stage fine-tuned HuBERT feature extractor + prototype classification," achieving a score of 0.009801 on the final Test-B of the SLT 2024 LRDWWS Challenge and securing second place, which is an order-of-magnitude improvement relative to the baseline of 0.130306 (Table 2).
2. **Provided paired ablations of data augmentation and loss settings during the fine-tuning period**: For the first time (in this task), it quantified and revealed that the interaction effect direction between TTS synthetic data and CTC/CE is opposite, and provided a mechanistic explanation based on task nature (recognition benefits from diversity, classification is sensitive to intra-class variance).
3. **Established matching rules between loss functions and classification methods**: SCL shapes feature geometry and thus complements nearest neighbors (KNN-C), while CE does not shape feature geometry and thus complements prototypes that average and denoise (PB-C), providing transferable selection criteria for the inference configuration of subsequent prototype-based methods.
4. **Verified the effectiveness of the three-stage progressive fine-tuning curriculum**: The sequence of typical speech → multi-speaker dysarthric → target speaker brought significant cross-speaker generalization even when the registered data did not contain evaluation speakers (three groups of SIC→SID comparisons in Table 1).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Contradiction between Inference Cost and Task Original Intent**: KWS is described in the paper's own motivation as a power-saving "initiator," but the backbone of the system is a HuBERT base-level SSL model, requiring full forward passes for both registration and inference. The paper does not report model parameters, inference latency, or power consumption, making the feasibility of edge-side deployment completely unassessable—for a task whose reason for existence is low power consumption, this is a structural gap.
- **Modeling of the Non-Keyword Class Remains a Closed-Set Treatment of an Open-Set Problem**: The non-keyword class is not defined by a specific vocabulary; the system simply trains and builds prototypes for it as the eleventh class. In real usage, the distribution of non-keywords is infinitely open. Whether an average prototype can hold the anti-class boundary is not tested by any perturbation experiments beyond the evaluation set, and the long-term robustness against false wake-ups is questionable.
- **TTS Augmentation and CE Route are Mutually Exclusive, Blocking a Path**: TTS data is harmful under CE (Table 1), meaning the most effective synthetic data expansion method cannot be used for the winning CE route. Class imbalance on the CE main line can only be digested by the data itself, lacking active intervention means.
- **First-Frame Prototype is a Purely Empirical Trick**: The paper acknowledges its consistent advantage but provides neither numbers nor explanations (Section 3.2). The lack of a clear mechanism means that transferability across models, languages, and recording conditions is not guaranteed.
- **CTC Crash Problem is Only Circumvented, Not Solved**: The root cause analysis of CTC training failure due to class imbalance (SIC-1) stops at the phenomenon level, without exploring fundamental solutions such as gradient clipping, class weighting, or resampling. The final choice was to "change the loss," avoiding the problem.
- **Personalization Still Requires Stage 3 Fine-tuning**: Although the requirement for registered data volume is lowered to a very low level, each new speaker still requires a complete fine-tuning (learning rate $10^{-5}$, warmup 32,000 steps, early stopping after 10 epochs), which is far from "plug-and-play without training." The paper does not report how many registered speech samples are needed to sustain the SDD stage.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Missing Data Scale Information**: The duration, number of sentences, and number of registered/evaluation samples per person for DC, DUC, Dev4, Test-A, and Test-B are all unreported. The volume of TTS synthetic data and the total volume of Merge train are also unreported. Third parties cannot precisely reproduce the results, nor can they even judge the specific degree of "low resource."
- **Lack of Horizontal Comparison with Peer Systems**: The paper only compares with the official baseline. The method and score of the first-place system, as well as the routes of other participating teams, are not reported, making it impossible to position the relative advantages and disadvantages of the method in the entire technical landscape.
- **Insufficient Statistical Strength of Ablations**: All results are from single runs, with no variance from multiple random seeds or significance tests. Dev4 has only four speakers, and differences of the order of 0.0003 in Table 1, such as 0.15653 vs 0.15625 (SID-1 vs SID-2), may completely fall within the noise interval, making ranking based on this risky.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity Not Investigated**: The SCL temperature is fixed at 0.07 with no scanning experiments; learning rate and warmup follow a single configuration; the comparison between first frame and full features lacks numerical support.
- **Coarse Granularity of Error Analysis**: There is no stratified error analysis by wake-up word class, dysarthria severity, or speaker, making it impossible to answer key engineering questions such as "which words are hardest" and "which patients benefit the most." The phenomenon of Test-B scores being generally lower than Test-A is also not explained.
- **Evaluation Conditions Detached from Real Conditions**: The settings of controlled recording, no noise, and non-streaming are simplifications by the challenge. The paper did not perform any extrapolation stress tests towards noise, far-field, or streaming. The applicability boundary of the conclusions actually only covers the challenge settings themselves.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Lightweight and Deployment Closed Loop**: Research on distillation, pruning, or quantization of the HuBERT feature extractor to truly land the already lightweight inference chain of "prototype + cosine" on the edge side, returning to the original demand of power saving for KWS. The two-piece architecture of SSL representation plus prototype in the paper is exactly an ideal form for distillation (the teacher is responsible for features, the student only needs to preserve the cosine geometry).
- **Synthetic Augmentation within the Dysarthria Domain**: Replacing AISHELL-3 typical speech synthesis with TTS or voice conversion (style transfer from healthy speech to dysarthric speech) trained on dysarthric speech is expected to lift the mutual exclusion constraint of "TTS is harmful to CE," making synthetic augmentation compatible with the optimal loss route.
- **Open-Set Non-Keyword Modeling**: Changing the non-keyword from the "eleventh class" to an out-of-distribution detection or anomaly detection framework (e.g., setting a threshold for the maximum similarity to the prototype to reject judgment), which is closer to real false wake-up scenarios.
- **Training-Free or Few-Shot Adaptation**: Exploring the zero-fine-tuning upper limit of skipping stage 3 and building prototypes solely based on registered speech, as well as the registered sample count-performance curve, to clarify where the lower bound of "low resource" lies. Meta-learning or adapter injection are also candidates for compressing personalization costs.
- **Joint Design of Loss Geometry**: Since SCL and CE respectively shape (or do not shape) feature geometry, research can be conducted on the joint optimum of CE + SCL + prototype regularization, or metric learning losses directly targeting "prototype separability," moving the matching rules on the inference side to the training side.
- **Systematic Extrapolation Towards Real Conditions**: Adding three-axis stress tests of noise addition, far-field reverberation, and streaming detection, and reporting results stratified by disease severity, to test whether the three-stage curriculum still holds outside the challenge settings.
