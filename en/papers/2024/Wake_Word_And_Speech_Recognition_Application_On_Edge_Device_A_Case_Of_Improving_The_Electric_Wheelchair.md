## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The starting point of this paper is not algorithmic innovation, but a very specific assistive engineering problem: the upper limb health and operational burden of wheelchair users. The epidemiological data cited in the paper is quite alarming—according to the study cited in [1], it is estimated that 70%–100% of manual wheelchair users will suffer from varying degrees of injury or pain in the upper limb region after using them for a period of time. The three main sites of injury are: the wrist (incidence rate 40%–66%), the elbow (5%–16%), and the shoulder (30%–60%). In addition, the incidence rate of injuries caused by blisters, abrasions, and cuts is 18%, and the probability of carpal tunnel syndrome is as high as 27%–90% (cited in [2]). These numbers explain why "freeing the hands" is not just a bonus for wheelchair users, but a rigid health need.

Electric wheelchair joysticks and buttons solve the physical problem of manual propulsion, but the paper points out two flaws in them: first, mechanical wear and tear; the moving parts of the controller will age and fail with long-term use; second, operation still requires a considerable degree of alertness and motor skills—for users with hand tremors, grasping difficulties, or high-level paraplegia, joysticks are equally inaccessible. Voice control therefore becomes a natural path, and the leapfrog progress of speech recognition technology in the past decade (the paper specifically mentions that the public release of large language models such as ChatGPT has further lowered the acceptance threshold for voice interaction for the general public) has made this path realistic.

However, moving voice control to wheelchairs involves an architectural contradiction very familiar in the field of KWS (Keyword Spotting): speech recognition needs to be "always on" to be useful, but an always-on microphone means continuous computation and privacy exposure. The paper cites a 2019 Microsoft report [10] stating that 41% of voice assistant users are concerned about trust, privacy, and passive listening. Wheelchairs are personal devices;around-the-clock listening in the user's home or hospital ward makes this contradiction sharper than with smart speakers. The solution given by the paper is also the classic solution in KWS—a two-stage gate: a lightweight, fully local wake word model stays in listening mode constantly, and only when it is triggered is the heavy-weight speech command recognition activated. This minimizes continuous computation and compresses the audio window that might be uploaded to the cloud (Google Speech API) to the short period "after wake-up." The paper also adds an argument on engineering ethics: the user's physical safety should be sufficient to offset (offset) remaining privacy concerns—the cost of a wheelchair losing control and hitting a wall is far greater than the cost of a small segment of command audio leaking.

Finally, there is a platform-level constraint: the paper argues that the Raspberry Pi is currently the most suitable edge platform for local deep learning-based speech recognition [19]—low cost, efficient, small size, and relatively high computing power; but even so, running full-scale transformer-level models on the Pi is still not feasible, so one must settle for lightweight and effective neural networks.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper outlines a clear line of technological evolution in the introduction and points out deficiencies one by one:

- **First-generation solutions with Arduino and dedicated speech recognition modules**. Vijay (cited in [3]) used an Arduino paired with an HM2007 speech recognition module; Umchid (cited in [4]) used an Arduino Uno R3 paired with a Greetech Voice Recognition Module V.3; Tan (cited in [5]) also attached an external voice module to the Atmega 328P, but used an HC-05 Bluetooth module for connection. The common shortcoming of this generation of solutions is that dedicated voice modules are essentially hardware for template matching with small vocabularies, usually supporting only dozens of isolated commands, requiring close-talk, being sensitive to the speaker, and completely lacking a wake word gate—commands spoken in any environment could directly trigger wheelchair movement, which is fatal in terms of safety.
- **Second-generation solutions introducing IoT and cloud APIs**. Malik (cited in [6]) integrated the Google Web Service API into an Android application, where voice commands must first be spoken to the phone, recognized by the API on the phone, and then the classification result sent to the Arduino via Bluetooth; Anwer (cited in [7]) and Rakib (cited in [8]) similarly placed the speech recognition engine on the mobile application. This generation of solutions has improved recognition accuracy, but architecturally "the brain of the wheelchair is in someone else's phone"—users must carry and operate the phone with them (which is itself a burden for users with mobility impairments), recognition depends on the network, and voice data continuously passes through third-party cloud services, meaning privacy issues are not only unresolved but amplified.
- **Lack of a wake word as a safety layer**. None of the above solutions are equipped with an independent wake word system. The paper explicitly states: To improve the safety of the wheelchair, the speech recognition system should only be activated after a designated wake word is invoked, to avoid accidental triggering of wheelchair movement by words or sentences in background speech or casual conversation. This is the most important architectural judgment in the entire paper.
- **Command space remains limited to directional commands**. Existing work basically only supports directional commands like "left, right, forward, backward." The paper proposes extending to locational commands—a single sentence like "go to the kitchen" allows the wheelchair to move to a specified location, which is much more efficient and practical for wheelchair users than continuously shouting directional commands.
- **Blank in edge-side model selection**. Although literature proves that lightweight models (LSTM) can perform real-time speech emotion recognition on a Raspberry Pi 3 with 86% accuracy (Nimisha et al., cited in [17]), CNN+LSTM voice-controlled wheelchairs can reach 95% (Sutikno, cited in [9]), and LSTM command recognition on Android phones can reach 98.2% (Bakouri, cited in [18]), the authors claim that before this paper, there has been no precedent for combining these capabilities into a complete chain of "wake word + command recognition on the edge body" and verifying its feasibility ("to the best of our knowledge, this is a first for such integration").

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the core question this paper seeks to answer is: **Can a local wake word detector and an on-demand activated speech command recognizer run simultaneously on a Pi 4-level edge device, and can the entire chain be proven usable using a conceptual prototype wheelchair?** This is specifically broken down into four sub-challenges: (1) Computing power challenge—the model that stays in listening mode constantly must be light enough for the Pi 4 to run continuously; the paper's answer is LSTM (cited in [13] proves that LSTM has the lowest wake word error rate under clean conditions, and cited in [20] states that LSTM outperforms RNN and GRU in complex tasks and long-range dependencies); (2) Data challenge—the wake word is a self-made "Hey SellTron," and there is no existing dataset on the market, so positive and negative samples must be built from scratch (this takes up a considerable portion of the methodology); (3) Safety challenge—the wake word gate plus the failure path design for invalid commands ensures that any misrecognition does not directly result in wheel rotation; (4) Validation challenge—quantify "usability" across five dimensions: controlled distance, simulated noise, multiple speakers, multiple commands, and latency.

## Methodology

(Following the reading approach for application-oriented papers, the following is organized along three lines: system architecture, wake word module, and control chain.)

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

**Hardware Chain (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3)**. The system uses the Raspberry Pi 4 as the central processing platform, powered by a 20,000 mAh power bank; the audio input is a Deity V4 Mini microphone connected via USB; a Maker Pi HAT is plugged into the GPIO pins of the Pi 4, its role being to simplify wiring with the maker drive (motor driver board); the maker drive connects to two DC motors on the left and right, and is itself powered by 3 AA batteries (the paper notes that a power bank can also be used for power). Due to project scope and conditions, the mechanical part was not made into a real wheelchair, but a small prototype vehicle (mini prototype) was used for proof of concept—this compromise itself explains why many experiments later were only conducted at a fixed distance of 50 cm and under simulated noise.

**The software chain is a two-stage gated pipeline (Fig. 10 Overall Flowchart + Fig. 4 UML Diagram)**. After the system starts, it initializes the microphone, then opens a **2-second continuous recording stream**, with each sample entering the recording queue; after the sample is converted to MFCC features, it is sent to the locally trained wake word model for prediction. If the model determines it is not the wake word, the system returns to the listening stage and continues the loop; if it is determined to be the wake word, the software switches to the speech command stage—at this point, the Google Speech API is initialized and waits for user input; if the user speaks a valid command, the corresponding action is executed, otherwise the speech command system exits and returns to wake word listening.

The cleverness of this architecture lies in the **asymmetric allocation of computing load**: the only thing running constantly 7×24 is "LSTM forward inference" (local, lightweight, no network), while the heavy continuous large-vocabulary speech recognition only runs on demand within the short window after wake-up. Why is it designed this way? Because wake word detection is a binary classification problem with an extremely small category (is/is not "Hey SellTron"), which a small model can handle; although wheelchair commands are a restricted vocabulary, the paper also wants it to be compatible with open-ended expressions, so handing it over to an engine like Google, which has been trained on massive amounts of data, is the easiest way (the results in Fig. 14 also confirm this: speech command recognition is more robust to noise than the self-trained wake word, which the paper attributes to the fact that the Google model's training volume far exceeds the self-built dataset). The cost is the introduction of cloud dependency in the command stage—this is an explicit trade-off made by the paper in the architecture, which is not solved in this paper, but limited to the short window after wake-up.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The paper presents mathematical details in the form of "diagrams plus text descriptions," without providing derivation formulas. Here is a summary of the standard content cited:

**LSTM Cell (Fig. 5)**. The paper uses the metaphor of a "transport highway" to explain the cell state: it is the straight line running through the top of the LSTM structure, theoretically serving as the "memory" of the network, carrying information from the early part of the sequence to the later part, thereby alleviating short-term memory problems (cited in [11]). Fig. 5 also draws out multiple point-wise operations and gates: these gates themselves are independently trained small neural networks that decide which data in the sequence to learn, which to retain, and which historical information to forget because it is irrelevant; they collaborate to perform addition, retention, and deletion operations on the cell state, and achieve this with only a small amount of linear interaction, so that information flowing in the cell state is not excessively distorted. (Completing according to the standard definition of LSTM: Forget gate $f_t=\sigma(W_f\cdot[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_f)$, Input gate $i_t=\sigma(W_i\cdot[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_i)$, Candidate memory $\tilde{C}_t=\tanh(W_C\cdot[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_C)$, Cell state update $C_t=f_t\odot C_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde{C}_t$, Output gate $o_t=\sigma(W_o\cdot[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_o)$, Hidden state $h_t=o_t\odot\tanh(C_t)$. The paper does not provide the above content in formula form.)

**MFCC Features**. The paper's original description is to convert each audio sample into Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC), calling it "the picture representation of sound"—that is, turning time-series audio into a time-frequency graph that the network can digest. The data processing performed by the listening system on the recording during operation is strictly consistent with that during training ("matches with the understanding of the AI model"), which is a necessary discipline to prevent training/inference feature distribution drift during deployment.

**Spectrogram Augmentation**. Three types of perturbations are applied to MFCC samples: warping in the time domain, masking blocks of consecutive frequency channels, and masking blocks of utterance in time—i.e., the standard three-piece set of SpecAugment. The "why" given by the paper is: deliberately let each sample randomly lose information, forcing the model to work harder to learn, thereby becoming more robust to sample deformation after training. For the reality that the self-made wake word has only a few hundred original recordings, this step is actually the key amplifier that allows the dataset to hold up.

**Loss Function**. Training uses `BCEWithLogitsLoss` from the PyTorch `torch.nn` module, which stably combines sigmoid and binary cross-entropy numerically: $L=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \left[y_i\log\sigma(z_i)+(1-y_i)\log(1-\sigma(z_i))\right]$. The reason for choosing binary cross-entropy comes directly from task modeling: wake word detection is formalized as a binary classification of "wake word vs. non-wake word" (Section II-D explicitly states that the training set is two-class data with class labels). The model structure is defined by the LSTM class passed in via command-line arguments using the template of `torch.nn`—the paper does not report the specific values for the number of layers, hidden dimensions, learning rate, and number of epochs.

**Accuracy Formula (Eq. 1)**: $\text{Accuracy}=\frac{\text{Number of Successful Attempts}}{\text{Total Number of Attempts}}\times100\%$, this metric is uniformly used for all tests involving accuracy.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Wake Word Module—Data Engineering for Self-Made Wake Words

Most of the technical content of the entire wake word module lies on the data side, because "Hey SellTron" is a word specially created by the paper for the project, and no public data exists. The three design decisions are worth unpacking for "why":

**Wake Word Selection**. The reason "Hey SellTron" was selected is written quite plainly by the paper: this word is not used in any other context (reducing the prior probability of false triggering), and the "s" sound in it makes it more unique compared to ordinary words. A unique phoneme combination means less acoustic overlap with daily conversation—this is the design logic shared by all commercial wake words ("Alexa", "Hey Siri"). The cost is that it must be manually recorded entirely and variants generated using software.

**Symmetric Construction of Positive and Negative Samples (Section II-D, Fig. 6, Fig. 7)**. There are 30,000 negative samples (non-wake word class), divided into three categories: random human voices taken from a subset of the Mozilla open-source Common Voice corpus (about 15,000 samples); silent environment sounds are recorded by leaving the microphone in a quiet room for 30 minutes and then cut into 2-second segments; noisy environment sounds are recorded by playing environment sounds of a noisy park and a crowded café on a JBL speaker for 30 minutes—three categories of environment sounds totaling 1 hour of recording, sliced and copied to expand to 15,000 samples. Positive samples (wake word class) are divided into three categories: self-recorded 500 samples (recorded directly using the Pi's own microphone, deliberately not using speaker playback to avoid channel coloring), with different pronunciations and pitches; TTS recordings collected from multiple TTS websites, using different accents and intonations to increase diversity; real-person recordings collected from various people outside the research group. There are two places in this construction that reflect engineering intuition: first, the **2-second slicing unit** is strictly aligned with the 2-second recording stream during runtime, ensuring consistency between training and inference distributions; second, **environmental sounds are specifically placed in the negative samples** (silent + park + café), allowing the model to see what "non-speech noise looks like" during the training phase, directly corresponding to the tests under simulated noise of 40–75 dB later (Fig. 12–14). The total number of positive and negative samples is 30,000, which is severely imbalanced against the few hundred original positive samples, which also explains why the training evaluation uses the F1 score as the main metric.

**Training Process and Overfitting Protection (Section II-E, Fig. 8)**. Initialization phase (orange part of the flowchart) reads the .json file to obtain sample labels and paths, converts to MFCC, performs spectrogram augmentation, encapsulates into a PyTorch dataset object and inserts it into the data loader, defines model parameters according to input arguments, establishes the LSTM class using the `torch.nn` template, and defines BCEWithLogitsLoss. Training phase (blue part) runs with user-defined learning rate and number of epochs, calculating and printing the accuracy, loss, precision, and F1 of the training set batch by batch; after training, the same metrics are evaluated using an independent validation set. Two protective measures: first, the code only saves weights to the checkpoint when the validation score improves compared to the historical best, avoiding saving a bunch of useless models; second, early stopping strategy—when the model achieves an F1 score of 0.99 for more than 5 consecutive epochs, training stops; if the score is always below 0.99, early stopping is not triggered. The paper explicitly positions early stopping as a preventive measure against overfitting. Using F1 rather than accuracy as the criterion for early stopping implicitly implies that under extreme class imbalance, accuracy would be dominated by negative samples and become distorted (this is an interpretation, not explicitly stated in the paper).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Control Chain—Gating, String Matching, and Locational Commands

**Wake Word Gating (Safety Layer)**. The first principle of the entire control chain is: the speech recognition system is only activated after the wake word is invoked, to avoid accidental triggering of wheelchair movement by words or sentences in background sounds or casual conversation. This is a design that elevates KWS from a "convenience feature" to a "safety interlock"—in the listening loop (Fig. 10), all audio that is not the wake word is discarded locally and does not enter command recognition at all.

**Command Matching (Fig. 9 Speech Command System Flowchart)**. The speech-to-text function of the Google Speech API converts the recognized words into strings, stores them in variables, and performs logical comparison with predefined command strings; this is a continuous loop until a correct match is obtained, then exits, and subsequently executes the action corresponding to the matched command—for example, if the string "move forward" is matched, the motor is programmed to move forward. That is, the "intelligence" of the command layer is outsourced to Google's acoustic/language model, and the local side only performs deterministic string equality matching. This part of the logic is extremely simple, auditable, and will not cause erroneous actions.

**Command Set (11 commands in total)**: Four directional commands: Turn Right, Turn Left, Go Forwards, Go Backwards; six locational commands: From Bedroom to Kitchen, From Kitchen to Living Room, From Bedroom to Living Room, From Living Room to Kitchen, From Kitchen to Bedroom, From Living Room to Bedroom; and Exit. The motor action for each command is prewritten and mapped (prewritten and mapped). Here, the true technical content of the locational commands must be pointed out: it is not navigation, but **pre-programmed motor action macros**—the reason the prototype vehicle can "go from the bedroom to the kitchen" is because the open-loop action sequence of that route was hardcoded in advance, with no sensor feedback. The paper itself admits this in the future outlook: to truly automate locational commands, LiDAR must be added for indoor mapping (cited in [15]), so that the wheelchair knows the exact location of each room, otherwise automation errors are inevitable.

**Failure Path Design**. There is a branch in Fig. 10 that is easily overlooked but crucial for safety: if the user does not speak a valid command after waking up, the system does not retry or guess, but directly exits the speech command system and returns to the wake word listening state. That is, the worst consequence of any misrecognition is "returning to a safe static standby state," rather than executing an erroneous action.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

- **For Arduino plus voice module solutions [3][4][5]**: Moving recognition from dedicated template matching hardware to deep learning LSTM on the Pi 4, recognition no longer depends on close-talk isolated words; more importantly, it adds the wake word safety gate that this generation of solutions completely lacks.
- **For mobile phone plus cloud API solutions [6][7][8]**: The recognition brain returns to the edge device on the wheelchair body itself, not requiring users to operate the phone; the wake word is processed locally throughout, and cloud dependency is compressed to the short window of commands after wake-up.
- **For Bakouri's Android-side LSTM solution [18]**: The model selection is also LSTM, but the deployment target changes from the user's phone to the wheelchair body itself—for users with mobility impairments, "not relying on another device that needs to be operated" is itself an accessibility improvement.
- **For Sutikno's CNN+LSTM solution [9]**: This paper does not use a unified model to handle everything, but a hybrid architecture—LSTM is only responsible for wake word binary classification, and large-vocabulary command recognition is outsourced to Google Speech API. This is a pragmatic choice of exchanging "external capabilities" for development costs, with the cost being cloud dependency in the command stage.
- **For general wake words like Google Assistant** (baseline in experiments): General wake words are面向 (oriented towards) massive users and massive vocabularies, while this paper's model is trained exclusively for the single word "Hey SellTron"—in Fig. 12, it surpasses Google Assistant under park noise, which the paper attributes precisely to this.
- **Command Space**: Expanded from directional commands in the literature to locational commands, although the implementation is an open-loop macro, conceptually it puts "wheelchair understanding spatial semantics" on the agenda.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Training data (Section II-D): Negative samples 30,000 = Common Voice human voices about 15,000 + environmental sounds 15,000 (30 minutes of silence and 30 minutes of noise totaling 1 hour of recording, 2-second slicing and copying to expand); Positive samples are self-recorded 500 samples + multi-accent recordings from TTS websites + several real-person recordings from outside the research group (the specific number of TTS and real-person recordings is not reported in the paper). The testing protocol is defined by five tables (Tables I–V): Distance test in a quiet controlled room, at seven distances of 10/20/50/100/150/200/300 cm, 5 groups × 20 times = 100 attempts per distance (Table I); Noise test in simulated noisy park and café environments (JBL speaker playing royalty-free environmental sounds, volume knob controlling sound level), fixed at 50 cm, 40–75 dB (the original table number is incorrectly marked as "TABLE I", should be Table II); Multi-speaker test for 10 people (50% male, 50% female), quiet controlled environment, fixed at 50 cm (Table III); Command accuracy and compliance test is 5 groups × 20 times = 100 attempts for each command (Table IV); Latency test repeats each item 10 times to take the average (Table V).

### Definition and Reasons for Selection of Evaluation Metrics

The main metric is the success attempt accuracy of Eq. (1). The reason for selection can be deduced from the scenario: the wheelchair is a task-oriented system, and users care about "whether it did the right thing after I spoke," so the paper separately defines a **compliance test** in addition to accuracy—for example, giving the command "Turn Right," the system makes a right turn recorded as success, and makes an erroneous action recorded as failure; it measures "whether the execution is correct after correct recognition," decoupling recognition errors from execution errors. Precision, loss, precision rate, and F1 score are also tracked during the training and validation phases (Section II-E). Latency is measured in seconds, reporting the average supplemented by minimum/maximum values (Table VII). It is worth noting that the paper does not report separated statistics for false wake-up rate and missed wake-up rate, nor ROC/DET curves; all results are folded into a single accuracy number—for a safety-critical wake word system, this is a clear metric gap.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Distance Dimension (Fig. 11)**: In a controlled laboratory environment, the wake word model achieves about 90% accuracy in the distance range from 10 cm to 200 cm; speech command recognition is higher than 80% under the same settings. The input volume is the loudness of natural greeting/issuing commands. The value at 300 cm is not reported in numerical form by the paper (only visible in the diagram).

**Noise Dimension (Fig. 12, Fig. 13, Fig. 14)**: In the park environment (Fig. 12), the model in this paper maintains above 80% accuracy under simulated park noise of 55 dB, and starts to struggle as the noise amplitude increases further; and the wake word model performs better than Google Assistant—the paper attributes this to the model being specifically trained for "Hey SellTron." In the café environment (Fig. 13), the wake word model only maintains about 80% accuracy when background noise is below 50 dB, indicating that the model is more sensitive to conversational noise, while Google shows strengths under human voice background noise. Speech command recognition (Fig. 14) maintains above 80% accuracy when background noise is below 65 dB, and is generally better than the self-trained wake word model—the paper attributes this to the Google model being trained on massive amounts of data.

**Multi-Speaker Dimension (Table VI, labeled as 5 attempts)**: Accuracies for 10 people are: Male 20%, 60%, 100%, 60%, 80%; Female 100%, 60%, 20%, 40%, 60%. The paper admits that accuracy fluctuates greatly between individuals (20% to 100%), the most likely reason being the differences in timbre, pitch, and loudness of each person's voice, and the model was only trained on a very small speech sample (highly personalized), failing to cover all voice variants; the direction for improvement is to significantly expand the speech dataset and maintain a balance of male and female data. It needs to be pointed out that there is an internal contradiction in the original text: the text in Section II-H states that each candidate is required to test 3 times to ensure consistency, but the title of Table VI says "FOR 5 ATTEMPTS" and the accuracies in the table are all multiples of 20%, which is consistent with the 5-attempt metric, so the table should prevail.

**Command Accuracy and Compliance (Fig. 15)**: The accuracy of each speech command is higher than 90%, the only exception is "turn left," which is slightly lower at 89%. The analysis of the cause of failure is very specific: in the inaccurate attempts, the system misrecognized "turn left" as "turn light," and the paper speculates that this is influenced by smart home "turn on light" type corpora. Once the command is correctly recognized, the prototype executes any valid command without a single failure—i.e., command compliance is 100% (the original words in the conclusion chapter are "the system recorded 100% command compliance accuracy after recognition").

**Latency (Table VII, Fig. 16)**: Wake word activation averages 0.73 s (minimum 0.33 s, maximum 1.10 s); speech commands average about 1.5 s. The paper judges this to be an acceptable response time for wheelchair applications.

**Horizontal Comparison with Literature Numbers**: Nimisha et al.'s real-time speech emotion recognition on Raspberry Pi 3 is 86% [17], Sutikno's CNN+LSTM voice-controlled wheelchair is 95% [9], Bakouri's LSTM five-command on Android is 98.2% [18]; this paper's wake word is about 90% (10–200 cm, Fig. 11a), command recognition is above 80% (Fig. 11b, Fig. 14), command compliance is 100%. The numbers are not as good as the latter two, but note the difference in metrics—[18] is a closed-set recognition of 5 commands on a mobile phone, while this paper is an 11-command set including 6 locational commands plus multi-dimensional evaluation of noise/distance/multi-speaker, the difficulty is not homogeneous, and direct ranking is meaningless.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Strictly speaking, ablation experiments (comparative removal of network structure, features, and augmentation strategies one by one) are not reported in the paper—there are no control group experiments to isolate "whether spectrogram augmentation is useful," "whether environmental sound negative samples are useful," "how much TTS data contributes," which is the biggest gap in method verification. However, the paper's multi-dimensional controlled tests play a role of partial approximate ablation:

- **Noise Type Comparison (Fig. 12 vs Fig. 13)**: Both are simulated noises, under park noise 80% accuracy can be maintained up to 55 dB, under café (conversational noise) it can only be maintained up to 50 dB—controlling noise level, only changing noise type, the conclusion is that the model is significantly more fragile to voice-type background noise. This echoes the negative sample construction: environmental sounds in training are half park/half café, but conversational noise and wake words both belong to human voice, making them harder to separate in feature space; while Google Assistant is actually stronger in human voice backgrounds.
- **Distance Scan (Fig. 11)**: Within 10–200 cm, the wake word is stable at about 90%, indicating that the 2-second window plus microphone gain does not have an obvious signal-to-noise ratio collapse within typical usage distances; the specific attenuation magnitude at 300 cm is not reported by the paper.
- **Speaker Scan (Table VI)**: Same environment, same distance, only changing the person, accuracy swings from 20% to 100%—turning the "model is highly personalized, lacking speaker generalization" from a guess into a fact with data, and directly supporting the conclusion that "the dataset must be expanded."
- **Decoupling of Recognition and Execution (Fig. 15 and Compliance Test)**: Splitting the error source into "mishearing" (turn left→turn light, 89%) and "doing wrong" (0 times) two segments, proving that the deterministic string matching plus pre-programmed action part in the chain is completely reliable, and all risks are concentrated on the acoustic recognition side.

## Main Contributions

1. **First (as claimed by the authors) integration of an independent wake word system into a voice-controlled edge device wheelchair prototype**, verifying the feasibility of the two-stage gated architecture of "local lightweight wake word constant listening + on-demand activated speech command recognition" on Pi 4-class hardware—this is the core contribution claim of this paper.
2. **A data engineering methodology for self-made wake words**: Under the premise of no publicly available data, positive samples are constructed using "self-recorded 500 samples + multi-accent TTS from multiple websites + real-person recordings from outside the group," and negative samples are constructed using "Common Voice 15,000 samples + 1 hour of environmental sound slicing and copying 15,000 samples," combined with MFCC, spectrogram augmentation, and F1 early stopping to complete training—a roadmap that can be directly copied by any small team doing custom wake words.
3. **Introduction of the concept of locational commands and implementation of an 11-command set**: Elevating wheelchair commands from directional primitives to spatial semantic tasks at the level of "from bedroom to kitchen" (although the implementation is an open-loop macro), and explicitly pointing out the sensor prerequisites for true navigation.
4. **Quantitative benchmark for edge feasibility**: Wake word accuracy above 80% below 50 dB noise, command recognition above 80% below 65 dB, command compliance 100%, wake-up latency average 0.73 s (Table VII), commands about 1.5 s (Fig. 16)—providing a set of landing numbers for subsequent work to compare against.
5. **Case study value**: The paper's conclusion explicitly positions this paper as able to enrich the design and development literature for voice-controlled wheelchairs and similar applications, i.e., it provides a verified system integration reference architecture, not a new algorithm.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Wake word model is highly personalized**: The main force of positive samples is 500 self-recordings, and the model generalizes poorly to voices outside the training set (the 20%–100% fluctuation in Table VI is direct evidence). The paper itself prescribes the remedy: significantly expand the dataset, balance male and female samples, and cover more accents.
- **Locational commands are open-loop macros**: There is no sensor feedback, "From Bedroom to Kitchen" is just a pre-written motor sequence, and it fails as soon as the environment changes. The paper admits that ultrasonic and LiDAR must be added to achieve obstacle avoidance and indoor mapping (cited in [14][15]) to truly automate locational commands.
- **Command stage relies on Google Speech API**: There is no local command recognition fallback in the architecture, and the command function is unavailable when the network is disconnected; voice command audio needs to pass through third-party cloud services, and privacy concerns are compressed but not eliminated. The paper does not report tests in disconnected scenarios, nor does it discuss the call costs and quotas of the API.
- **Prototype hardware is not commercially representative**: The motors of the mini prototype are only sufficient for proof of concept, and the paper explicitly points out that commercial wheelchairs require extremely high precision and motors must withstand human weight; the dynamics of small wheels are also different from real wheelchairs, and the open-loop duration of locational commands will amplify errors at actual scales.
- **Key engineering parameters are opaque**: The number of layers, hidden dimensions, learning rate, number of training epochs, model file size, memory and CPU usage, and power consumption of the LSTM are not reported by the paper—this makes it difficult to precisely reproduce the results and also impossible to evaluate the true resource margin of the system on the Pi 4.
- **No obstacle avoidance capability**: No matter how accurate the voice chain is, a wheelchair that cannot perceive obstacles cannot be said to be safe, and the paper lists this as a must-do item for the future.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Lack of separated statistics for false positives/misses**: The safety metrics of a wake word system should be split into false wake-up rate (background conversation mistakenly triggering) and rejection rate (true wake-up not responding) to be reported separately; this paper folds everything into a single accuracy; and the very meaning of the wake word gate is to prevent false triggering, so this metric gap forms a tension with the paper's own safety claims.
- **Contradiction between text and tables**: Section II-H writes that each candidate is tested 3 times, but the title of Table VI and the granularity of the values (multiples of 20%) point to 5 attempts; also, the noise environment test table is incorrectly compiled as "TABLE I" in the original text (same number as the distance test table, should be Table II). Both indicate insufficient polishing of manuscript details, and the cited numbers need to be calibrated for metrics.
- **Limited sample size and scene realism**: The multi-speaker test is only 10 people; the noise environment uses a speaker to play royalty-free environmental sounds for simulation, rather than the reverberation and multi-source noise of a real park/café; the command test is fixed in a 50 cm quiet room, without cross-validation with the multi-distance results of Fig. 11 (e.g., how is the command accuracy under 200 cm plus 60 dB noise, unknown).
- **Incomplete baseline coverage**: The external baseline is only the wake word of Google Assistant, and there is no external baseline for speech command recognition; there is no re-testing and comparison with the 95%, 98.2% of literature [9][18] under the same protocol.
- **Insufficient statistical robustness**: Except for latency reporting minimum/maximum values, there are no confidence intervals or variance reports for other accuracies, and the accuracy number of a single 100 attempts cannot distinguish between statistical fluctuations and real differences.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The directions given by the paper in Section IV: First, install ultrasonic and LiDAR sensors to achieve obstacle avoidance—this is a necessary condition for commercialization, able to prevent countless accidents of crashing into objects and walls; the indoor mapping capability of LiDAR is also the premise for the locational command concept to hold, allowing the wheelchair to know the exact location of each room and minimize automation errors. Second, replace with commercial-grade motors that can withstand human weight and ensure precision. Third, continue to train the wake word model with a larger dataset so that it can understand wake words under completely different accents—the paper points out that large companies have proven that high-accuracy wake words are feasible under sufficient data and computing power. Fourth, consider OpenAI's Whisper for candidate models: in cited in [16], Wechsler has already achieved real-time speech recognition and transcription of Whisper on the Raspberry Pi Model B, which means that moving command recognition back to the local side and getting rid of Google API dependency has technically opened a gap.

Outside the paper, three more can be added from the KWS engineering perspective: replace LSTM with ready-made open-source wake word frameworks (such as Porcupine, openWakeWord type solutions) or more modern streaming architectures, in exchange for lower constant computing power; localize command recognition as a small vocabulary model (11 commands do not need a large vocabulary engine at all), simultaneously solving the three problems of latency, disconnection, and privacy; and introduce a formal evaluation protocol for false wake-up rate, so that safety claims have corresponding data backing.
