- **Date**: September 2024 (arXiv:2409.08605, v3 updated on January 11, 2025)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08605
- **Keywords**: Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN), keyword spotting, LiCo-Net, Gram polynomials, learnable activation functions, streaming wake-word detection, insertion position ablation

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS, also known as wake-word detection) is a persistent entry point for voice assistants in smart devices: devices need to continuously listen on streaming audio, and only "wake up" the subsequent large model speech stack upon detecting a predefined keyword, thereby saving power. This imposes three engineering constraints on KWS: computational power and memory overhead must be minimized (since running constantly means every extra multiplication burns electricity continuously), false accepts must be kept to a minimum (random wake-ups are a catastrophic user experience issue), and false rejects must also be kept to a minimum (failing to wake up ruins the experience). The paper presents this triangle of "accuracy, speed, and efficiency" right at the beginning, which is the common starting point for all edge-side KWS work. It is worth emphasizing the coupling relationship among these three constraints: the false alarm rate and false rejection rate are naturally trade-offs. The only way to move in a better direction along the same trade-off curve is to improve the model's expressive efficiency—learning more discriminative features with the same amount of parameters and computation. Therefore, the history of architectural innovation in edge-side KWS is essentially a history of squeezing "expressive power per parameter." Any new component wanting to enter this field must first answer "what exactly do you gain under the same budget."

From the perspective of model evolution, KWS in the deep learning era has cycled through three major architecture families: CNNs (Residual CNNs, DS-CNNs, Compressed TDNNs), RNNs (LSTMs with max-pooling loss, Convolutional Recurrent Networks), and Self-Attention (Keyword Transformer, Streaming Mobile Attention). However, the paper points out an easily overlooked commonality: the "atomic components" of these architectures are all MLPs—learnable linear layers plus fixed non-linear activations (e.g., ReLU). That is to say, the KWS architectural innovations of the past decade have only changed "how to connect," never "what the connection itself is."

The Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN) proposed in 2024, a network that replaces "weights are numbers" with "weights are small curves," happens to touch this atomic component: it merges learnable linear layers and fixed activations into a set of learnable non-linear activation functions. KAN has improved both performance and interpretability in tasks involving symbolic formula representation. However, the paper also cites the conclusion from "KAN or MLP: A fairer comparison"—that naively replacing MLPs with KANs performs worse in most domains. Exceptions exist: U-KAN is effective on the U-Net backbone for medical image segmentation, and KAN is effective for time series prediction. So, what about speech? Speech signals are fine and complex, and KWS requires efficient modeling. Whether KAN is an aid or a burden was a question no one had seriously answered in 2024. This is the problem space of this paper.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

First, naive replacement causes "channel width collapse" under parameter budgets. Each KAN neuron carries O(d) times more parameters than an MLP (where d is the number of basis functions, which can be understood as the number of coefficients needed to describe that "small curve"). KWS is a task extremely sensitive to parameter budgets. Once MLPs are replaced with KANs under the same parameter count, the network must significantly reduce the channel width w to maintain the total parameter count unchanged—Table I in this paper shows that the original LiCo-Net has w=72, which drops to only 34 after being fully replaced with GKAN, a reduction of more than half. Channel width directly determines the expressive capacity of features. This collapse has been proven harmful in most domains, and edge-side speech models will be even more sensitive.

Second, there is no prior knowledge telling you which layer of a deep network KAN should be inserted into. Shallow layers of deep networks perform low-level local feature modeling (in KWS, this is frame-level local acoustic context), while deep layers perform high-level abstract feature modeling (subword, discriminative information of words). KAN's curve fitting ability and parameter density may perform very differently in these two roles. However, previous KAN application literature (medical segmentation, time series prediction) did not systematically treat "insertion position" as a variable for ablation, opting instead for direct overall replacement. The lack of guidance in the position dimension is the biggest cognitive gap when transferring KAN to new modalities.

Third, comparative experiments suffer from structural confounding. Adding a layer of KAN to a network simultaneously changes three things: structural depth, parameter count, and activation function type. Without structurally aligned controls, the observed gains cannot be attributed—was it due to KAN's learnable activation, or simply because there was one extra layer? Most previous KAN application papers did not handle this confounding.

Fourth, there was basically no systematic exploration of KAN in the speech domain at the time. The paper claims "to the best of our knowledge, this is one of the earliest explorations of using KAN for speech processing tasks," and this blank itself is the deficiency to be filled.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Summarized in one sentence: Find the correct way to integrate KAN on a streaming 1D CNN KWS architecture with strictly constrained parameters—avoiding channel width collapse caused by parameter density, placing KAN in the job position where it is truly good at, and using structurally aligned ablations to prove that the gain indeed comes from KAN itself, not from the extra layer. Specifically broken down into four sub-challenges: (1) Which of the two categories (replacement integration and addition integration) and six integration schemes is effective; (2) Whether the gain persists under harsh acoustic conditions such as noise; (3) Whether the conclusions hold at small model scales of 100K/200K parameters (edge-side models are often at this scale); (4) How to design fair controls to exclude interference from structural differences.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall pipeline follows the classic "subword classification plus WFST decoding" paradigm. The input is 40-dimensional log-Mel filter bank energy, calculated per frame every 10ms with a window length of 25ms—this is the de facto standard frontend configuration for KWS. A frame shift of 10ms means the model runs inference 100 times per second, which is why all subsequent discussions must obsess over parameter counts. Features are fed into an encoder for subword classification (subwords are modeling units closer to spelling segmentation than phonemes), with a total of 13 classes: 11 subwords plus SIL (silence) and FILLER (any speech other than keywords). Choosing 40-dimensional Mel features instead of higher dimensions is also a consideration of parameter budget—feature dimensions directly determine the size of the weight matrix in the first layer, and 40 dimensions are a seasoned compromise between accuracy and overhead. Note that every stage of the entire pipeline (feature frontend, convolution, WFST accumulation) is frame-by-frame causal, with no future frame dependency. This is the engineering meaning of "streaming": wake-up latency is compressed to the frame level. Subword posterior probabilities are then passed to a WFST decoder (Weighted Finite State Transducer, a state graph that accumulates subword sequences into keyword probabilities), estimating the probability of predefined keywords being spoken at each moment. The paper does not directly argue why subword plus WFST was chosen over whole-word classification. By domain convention, subword modeling allows "volume up" and "volume down" to share "vo/lume" subword data and facilitates changing keyword combinations without modifying the model, which is the mainstream choice for streaming KWS. This point is not elaborated in the paper and is considered a default premise.

The encoder selected is LiCo-Net (Linearized Convolution Network). The motivation for choosing it is clearly stated in the paper: simple, relatively interpretable, with residual connections, designed for streaming KWS and hardware efficiency, and the 1D CNN family has proven competitive at small parameter counts. In other words, the authors deliberately chose a "clean" experimental platform—simple enough structure so that attribution after inserting KAN would not be drowned out by complex structural noise. LiCo-Net consists of five stacked LiCo-Blocks (Fig. 3 caption), each block having three Conv1D layers: the first layer is a spatial convolution with kernel size K>1 (experimentally fixed at K=5), responsible for "low-level" modeling with local context; the second and third layers are pointwise convolutions with kernel size 1, where the first pointwise convolution expands the hidden layer dimension by an expansion ratio e (expansion ratio), allowing the network to perform "high-level" modeling in a wider hidden space. A Conv1D is attached at the end of the network to output subword probabilities. This division of labor—"spatial convolution manages low-level, pointwise convolution manages high-level"—is the coordinate system for all subsequent KAN insertion position experiments.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

Mathematical form of KAN: KAN(x) = (Φ_{L−1} ∘ Φ_{L−2} ∘ … ∘ Φ_0)(x), where L is the number of KAN layers, and Φ_l is an n_{l+1}×n_l matrix—but note that each element ϕ_{l,i,j} of this matrix is not a number, but a learnable activation function. Each activation function is represented by a basis function expansion: ϕ = w_b·b(x) + w_s·Σ_m c_m·B_m(x). Translated into plain language: in an MLP, an edge has only one learnable scalar weight multiplied and then passed through a fixed ReLU; in KAN, an edge is an entire learnable curve, which is composed of three parts—a basis function b(x) (e.g., silu(x), i.e., x·sigmoid(x), providing a fixed basic non-linearity), a set of B-spline bases B_m (piecewise polynomials, responsible for the plastic part of the curve) paired with learnable coefficients c_m, plus two predefined scalars w_b and w_s controlling the proportion of the two parts. The design of the w_b·b(x) term here is clever: it guarantees a reasonable shape for the curve that has been repeatedly verified by deep learning (silu), and the learnable part only needs to fit the "residual shape" on this basis. The training starting point is no worse than MLP, which is an important engineering detail for KAN's stable convergence. The original KAN uses B-splines, while this paper uses Gram polynomials (a family of orthogonal polynomials on discrete data points), i.e., GKAN.

Form of convolutional KAN: y_{C_out,i} = Σ_{d=0}^{C_in−1} Σ_{a=0}^{k−1} ϕ_{C_out,a,d}(x_{d,i+a}). Comparing with ordinary 1D convolution y_{C_out,i} = Σ_d Σ_a w_{C_out,a,d}·x_{d,i+a}, the difference is only in one place: the scalar weight w is replaced by the curve ϕ. Therefore, the intuitive understanding of convolutional KAN is very clean—the "weight slot" in the convolution kernel changes from multiplying a number to passing through a learnable curve. The receptive field, sliding method, and streaming causality all remain unchanged. This is also the fundamental reason it can be seamlessly embedded into LiCo-Net: structurally it is plug-and-play, changing only the action of "multiplication."

Two key implementation choices and reasons: (1) The basis function uses Gram polynomials instead of the original B-splines, because the authors' own preliminary experiments and cited literature have verified the superiority of the Gram version in image classification, with the full order fixed at 3; (2) This choice is not supported by systematic ablation (basis function family comparison is not reported in the paper), belonging to empirical decision-making. Fixing the order at 3 also makes sense: the higher the order, the more the curve can bend, and the more parameters there are. Third-order can already express shapes with one bend and one return, which is likely sufficient for smooth distributions like speech features. Higher orders would exacerbate the parameter density problem. The mathematical essence of parameter cost: each curve requires O(d) coefficients (d is the number of basis functions), so the parameter count of each GKAN neuron is O(d) times that of an MLP, making the network "dense."

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Replacement Integration — First Do a "Naive Comparison"

The first set of experiments replaces the spatial convolution and pointwise convolution in the LiCo-Block respectively or entirely with GKAN, obtaining three new architectures (Fig. 3): GKAN MLP (only spatial convolution replaced), MLP GKAN (only pointwise convolution replaced), and GKAN (all replaced). The design intent of this step is to answer the most primitive question—who is stronger, KAN or MLP, in a head-to-head fight under the same parameter budget? The experiment aligns the total parameter count of each architecture in the range of 394K-415K (Table I) by reducing the channel width w, making the comparison at least fair in terms of parameters. The conclusion is that among the three replacements, only MLP GKAN (replacing KAN at the high-level modeling position) barely holds its own against the baseline, while the other two are significantly worse. This "seemingly failed experiment" is actually one of the most important pieces of evidence in the entire paper: it rules out the naive replacement route and directs attention to the clue of the "high-level position."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Addition Integration — Treat KAN as a "Plugin" Rather Than a "Replacement"

The second set of experiments does not change the original structure but inserts an additional GKAN Conv1D layer (input and output dimensions are both w×1) next to the pointwise convolution, in three positions (Fig. 4): GKAN pre (before pointwise convolution), GKAN post (after pointwise convolution), and GKAN mid (parallel to pointwise convolution). The expansion ratio is uniformly e=6. The motivation for this route comes directly from the lessons of the first set of experiments: since replacement causes channel width collapse, retain the original width of MLP and introduce KAN in an "incremental" way. The effect is that channel width is preserved—Table II shows that the w of the three addition schemes is 62-64, only slightly smaller than the original 72, and the parameter count 396K-412K is almost identical to the baseline 394K. The results show that GKAN post becomes the optimal configuration in the entire paper, while GKAN mid fails miserably (expanded in the experimental section later).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Structural Alignment Ablation — Isolate the Variable of "Activation Function"

The authors realized that GKAN post's victory over the original LiCo-Net was not entirely fair: an extra layer was inserted, changing both structure and depth. So, a control was added (Fig. 6): an ordinary Conv1D layer was also added to the original LiCo-Block, in a position exactly consistent with GKAN post, called MLP post (399K parameters, w=70, aligned with GKAN post's 396K, w=62). Thus, MLP post and GKAN post have exactly the same structure, and the only difference is whether that layer uses convolution with fixed activation or convolution with learnable activation—the attribution chain of gain is thus closed. This ablation design is the most worthy part of the methodology to learn: any paper claiming "adding a module increases performance" should pass this test.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Differences from original KAN (Liu et al., 2024): The original focuses on the philosophy of B-splines and overall replacement of MLPs, while this paper switches to Gram polynomials and abandons overall replacement for local insertion. Differences from "KAN or MLP: A fairer comparison" (2024): That paper's conclusion is that when parameters are aligned, KAN cannot beat MLP in most tasks. This paper does not overturn this conclusion but refines it—KAN is not a global multiple-choice question, but a local fill-in-the-blank question; placing it in the right position (low-dimensional high-level features) yields benefits. Differences from U-KAN (medical segmentation) and KAN time series prediction: Those works replace the entire backbone, while this paper keeps the LiCo-Net backbone intact and only performs surgery inside the block, thus providing fine-grained conclusions on "where to insert." Differences from KWS own literature: CNN/RNN/attention routes all change the connection topology, while this paper is the first to change "the connection itself," and explicitly does not aim to sweep SOTA, but to provide a KAN integration methodology.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Positive samples: Four common English phrases as keywords—take a picture, volume up, volume down, play music. Data comes from crowdsourcing and is de-identified, split into training/testing sets by speaker independence (speech from the same speaker does not cross sets). The training set has approximately 15K, 25K, 22K, 24K clips for the four keywords respectively, from 14,957 speakers; the evaluation set has approximately 4K, 6K, 5K, 6K clips respectively, from 3,801 speakers; the average duration of each clip is 2.5 seconds. Speaker-independent splitting here is not optional but a hard requirement—KWS deployed online will face unseen speakers. If speech from the same person spans the training and testing sets, the measured FRR will be severely underestimated, and the model will have effectively "memorized" the timbre of these people. The 14,957 training speakers are relatively sufficient compared to the number of keyword clips, indicating that the diversity of speakers in positive samples is guaranteed. Negative samples: Speech from LibriSpeech containing no keywords, with train-clean-360 and train-other-500 used as training negatives, and train-clean-100 plus dev/test clean/other used as evaluation negatives (the public corpus scales corresponding to subset names are approximately 360 hours and 500 hours, specific numbers are not given in the text). Both positive and negative training sets underwent background noise addition and speed perturbation augmentation. Training details: 8 GPU cards, batch 400, Adam (β taken as 0.9 and 0.98), learning rate 0.001, for a total of 10 epochs; cross-entropy loss weights SIL, FILLER, and 11 subwords with weights 1, 2, 8 respectively; subword labels are obtained using forced alignment from a pre-trained acoustic model (using an existing model to cut out which subword each frame belongs to). The reason for giving a class weight of 8 to subwords can be deduced: subword frames account for a small proportion in the data but are precisely the categories that determine false rejects; weighting is to protect them in optimization.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metrics are False Rejection Rates (FRR) at four False Alarm (FA) operating points: FA/h = 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0. The reason for selection lies in the fact that KWS is essentially a detection task rather than a classification task: looking at a single accuracy is meaningless; one must draw a trade-off curve between "how often it wakes up falsely" and "how often it fails to respond when called." FA/h = 0.1 means an average of only one false wake-up is allowed in ten hours, which is the most stringent tier for productized wake-words. Reporting four operating points is equivalent to sampling key segments on the DET curve (Detection Error Trade-off curve) (Fig. 5 directly gives the DET curves for two keywords, take a picture and volume down). The reason for reporting four fixed tiers rather than summary metrics like area under the curve is that real products will only select one operating point for deployment. Researchers need to allow readers to directly compare the height of two curves at any interested operating point; summary metrics would instead mask local crossings (as seen in Table IV for the 100K tier, where crossings between tiers indeed appear). Lower FRR is better, and comparison is only meaningful under the same FA/h tier. This metric system is the standard practice in the industry for KWS, much stricter than the top-1 accuracy in academia.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Replacement Integration (Table I, parameters aligned at approx. 394K-415K)**: Original LiCo-Net (w=72, 394K) has FRRs of 2.108/1.718/1.213/0.866 at the four tiers; GKAN MLP (w=52, 415K) has 2.584/2.047/1.429/1.077, worse at all tiers; MLP GKAN (w=42, 394K) has 2.049/1.588/1.256/0.923—the most stringent 0.1 tier is slightly better than the baseline (relative improvement approx. 2.8%), but the 0.5 and 1.0 tiers are slightly worse (1.256 vs 1.213, 0.923 vs 0.866), a mix of wins and losses; GKAN full replacement (w=34, 402K) has 2.544/2.077/1.419/1.075, significantly worse at all tiers. The way to read this table is: placing KAN at the low-level modeling position (spatial convolution) is a guaranteed loss, placing it at the high-level modeling position (pointwise convolution) can at best tie, while channel width is compressed from 72 to 34-52. From this, the core inference of the entire paper is drawn: GKAN is better at high-level feature modeling, and integration in the later stages of deep networks is more promising.

**Addition Integration (Table II)**: GKAN pre (w=64, 408K) has 2.226/1.863/1.272/0.974, slightly worse than the baseline; GKAN post (w=62, 396K) has 1.772/1.525/1.073/0.758, significantly better than the original LiCo-Net at all four tiers, with relative improvements of approx. 16%, 11%, 12%, 12% respectively; GKAN mid (w=64, 412K) has 3.446/2.537/1.939/1.444, the 0.1 tier worsens by approx. 60% compared to the baseline, making it the worst configuration in the entire paper. The authors' explanation for the GKAN mid disaster is: the difference in how MLP and GKAN model input features is too large, and parallel integration (fusion of two-way outputs) interferes with each other, making it unsuitable for parallel use. The authors also provide a more general intuitive argument: KAN has several times more parameters per neuron, making the network "dense," so it is more suitable for modeling complex patterns in low-dimensional spaces—the high-level feature space after pointwise convolution has low dimension but high semantic concentration, which is exactly KAN's comfort zone; whereas the high-dimensional raw features processed by spatial convolution would cause parameter costs to spiral out of control. This assertion of a "comfort zone" can be dug into one layer deeper: raw Mel features have high dimensions and statistical distributions close to continuous smooth energy surfaces, where linear operations like pointwise multiplication plus a fixed non-linearity are sufficient to characterize; while the high-level features output by pointwise convolution are low-dimensional semantic vectors compressed and purified by the network, with more curved and complex inter-class boundaries, which happen to be shapes that learnable curves are good at carving—whether KAN's capacity is spent on the blade or in the air depends on what kind of features it faces. This also explains why the same GKAN layer has vastly different fates when inserted before or after.

**Noise Robustness (Table III)**: The evaluation set adds random background noise, with signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) following a Gaussian distribution centered at 12.5dB, truncated at [0, 25] dB. MLP original (w=72, 394K) has FRRs of 8.748/7.017/4.903/4.024 at the four tiers; GKAN post (w=62, 396K) has 7.748/6.167/4.332/3.434, with relative improvements of approx. 11%-15%, and consistent advantage at all tiers. This shows that the gain on clean speech is not overfitting to quiet conditions; it still holds under harsh acoustics and is even more valuable (FRR absolute values multiply by several times under noise, so the same proportional improvement corresponds to more actual wake-ups saved).

**Small Model Scale (Table IV, expansion ratio e=5 for 100K tier, e=6 for 200K tier)**: Approx. 200K parameter tier, MLP (w=50, 204K) clean is 2.55/2.04/1.39/1.09, GKAN post (w=42, 196K) is 2.16/1.83/1.41/0.97—the 0.1 tier has a relative improvement of approx. 15%, only the 0.5 tier basically ties with 1.41 vs 1.39, overall dominant; under noisy conditions 10.51/8.46/6.18/4.82 vs 10.13/7.74/5.44/4.25, GKAN post is also comprehensively better. Approx. 100K parameter tier, MLP (w=36, 104K) clean is 3.23/2.72/1.99/1.56, GKAN post (w=30, 101K) is 3.64/2.68/1.87/1.44—the 0.1 tier is actually worse by approx. 13%, the latter three tiers are slightly better, and under noisy conditions they are also mixed (12.94 vs 12.56, 7.58 vs 7.68, 5.94 vs 6.17). The paper's own wording is "comparable," and provides an explanation: the damage of channel width reduction is greater for small models—the model is already narrow, and squeezing w from 36 to 30 to make room for KAN parameters causes expressive capacity loss to exceed the expressive gain brought by KAN curves. This boundary condition is critical: the gain interval of GKAN post is above approx. 200K parameters; do not use it on smaller models.

It should be noted that this paper does not conduct horizontal comparisons with other SOTA architectures (such as TC-ResNet, MatchboxNet, BC-ResNet); the baseline is only LiCo-Net itself—this is consistent with the paper's positioning of "researching KAN integration methodology rather than sweeping benchmarks," but means it is impossible to judge the position of GKAN post relative to the current strongest KWS architectures from this paper alone.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Core ablation (Table V, Fig. 6): MLP post (adding an ordinary Conv1D to the original LiCo-Block in the same position as GKAN post, 399K parameters, w=70) vs GKAN post (396K, w=62). Under clean conditions, MLP post is 2.06/1.658/1.12/0.832, GKAN post is 1.772/1.525/1.073/0.758; under noisy conditions, MLP post is 9.3/7.118/5.124/3.943, GKAN post is 7.748/6.167/4.332/3.434, with a relative improvement of approx. 17% at the noisy 0.1 tier. Note a detail: MLP post itself is stronger than the original LiCo-Net (clean 0.1 tier 2.06 vs 2.108), indicating that "adding a layer" indeed brings its own gain—this precisely confirms the necessity of structural alignment ablation; and only after the structures are completely aligned does the KAN version still win by a margin, thus attributing the gain to the learnable activation function itself. Three conclusions from the ablation: (1) Adding a layer brings gain, but KAN adding a layer brings greater gain; (2) The gain is amplified under noise; (3) Combining with the failure of GKAN mid in Table II, the integration method of KAN is more important than "whether to use KAN." In addition, position ablation (pre/post/mid, Table II) and scale ablation (100K/200K, Table IV) can also be considered as generalized ablations, locating the two sweet spots of "inserted after pointwise convolution" and "above 200K parameters" respectively. The paper does not report ablations for the basis function family (Gram vs B-spline) and polynomial order (beyond 3). Reading the entire ablation chain together, the evidence of the entire paper can be organized into a clean syllogism: replacement experiments locate the high-level position, addition experiments find the post sweet spot, and structural alignment ablation excludes confounding to complete attribution—each step is built on the unexpected results of the previous step, and this progression rhythm itself is a good template for ablation design.

## Main Contributions

1. To the authors' knowledge, this is one of the earliest explorations of using KAN for speech processing tasks, filling the application blank of KAN in the speech modality and providing a direct reference system for subsequent researchers.

2. Systematic integration method ablation: Six integration schemes (GKAN MLP, MLP GKAN, GKAN, GKAN pre, GKAN post, GKAN mid) across two categories (replacement, addition) are compared horizontally under the same parameter budget, same data, and same training recipe, plus a structurally aligned MLP post control, forming a complete methodology template for "how to verify adding new modules."

3. Derived a transferable core judgment: KAN (GKAN) is most effective when modeling high-level complex features in low-dimensional spaces—specifically for KWS, this means inserting after the pointwise convolution (GKAN post), comprehensively reducing FRR at four false alarm tiers with almost the same parameter count (396K vs 394K), and holding true under noise and small scales down to 200K parameters. This judgment that "where to place is more important than whether to use" has guiding value for the implementation of KAN in other modalities.

4. Clearly delineated failure boundaries: Guaranteed loss at low-level positions, severe failure with parallel fusion (GKAN mid 0.1 tier FRR worsens by approx. 60%), and disappearance of gains below 100K parameters—negative results are also contributions, helping later generations avoid pitfalls.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

Parameter density is the original sin. KAN's O(d) times more parameters per neuron means that any integration scheme must make a trade-off between "channel width" and "curve expressiveness." The optimal GKAN post in this paper also compresses w from 72 to 62; this trade-off has been proven to be a net loss at the 100K tier. Looking deeper, this means KAN's gains are essentially locked in an intermediate bandwidth interval: when the model is too large, MLP itself has enough capacity to learn complex decision boundaries, and the marginal收益 of curve-ification diminishes; when the model is too small, parameter density kills channel width first. KWS is precisely a domain where both extremes are common—speaker models have tens of K parameters, while mobile phones can relax to several M—the applicable window of GKAN post needs users to weigh clearly before design. A more substantial problem is: the paper does not report inference latency, compute (FLOPs), or actual memory usage at all—KWS is a persistent task running 100 frames per second. The hardware cost of KAN curve evaluation (even if just third-order polynomials) relative to a single multiplication, and the impact of parameter density on cache friendliness, likely offset the few points of gain in FRR; these key engineering numbers are not reported in the paper, and LiCo-Net was selected precisely because it is "hardware efficient." This tension is not addressed head-on by the paper. Secondly, KAN's signature interpretability (plotting learned activation curves to see physical meaning) is completely unfulfilled in this paper—the paper does not report any activation function visualization analysis, effectively abandoning KAN's most unique selling point relative to MLP. Thirdly, the choice of Gram polynomials as basis functions and order 3 relies only on preliminary experiments and external literature, lacking self-ablation; sensitivity of basis function family and order is not reported.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

The data is an internal crowdsourced set, four English keywords, 14,957 training speakers, not public, cannot be reproduced, and no generalization verification was performed on public benchmarks like Google Speech Commands. Training ran for only 10 epochs, and the number of random seeds and variance were not reported—for KWS with small gains (a 0.3 point FRR gap at the clean 0.1 tier), without multiple seed statistics, significance is questionable; the paper does not report confidence intervals. The baseline is only the LiCo-Net family, with no comparison to any external SOTA architecture, making it impossible to answer "how much of this gain remains when moved to a stronger backbone." Noise evaluation only uses one SNR distribution (centered at 12.5dB, truncated at [0,25]), without segmented analysis by frequency band or noise type (steady-state noise vs. impulsive knocks), nor far-field reverberation conditions. DET curves are only shown for two of the four keywords. In addition, FRR was not tested at loose tiers above FA/h=1.0, where product ends occasionally use high false-alarm tolerance tiers for sensitivity tuning.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the natural extension of the paper's conclusions and the above limitations, directions worth pursuing include: migrating the recipe of "inserting KAN at high-level low-dimensional positions" to other speech tasks (directions named by the paper itself—speech recognition, acoustic event detection, etc.) and other modalities, to verify whether this position rule is universal; performing systematic ablation on basis function families and orders (Gram vs B-spline vs Radial Basis, orders 2 to 6) to find the optimal curve capacity for accuracy vs. parameters; using pruning or distillation to compress the parameter density of GKAN layers, attempting to salvage tiers below 100K; positively quantifying the real latency and energy consumption of KAN curve evaluation on target hardware (DSP, Cortex-M) to answer the must-answer question for edge-side "is it worth it"; leveraging the interpretability of learnable activations to perform wake-word customization or subword-level error analysis, monetizing KAN's unique advantages; and re-running on public benchmarks, with multi-language keywords, and under far-field multi-noise conditions, to supplement statistical significance and external baselines. Among these directions, hardware quantification and interpretability monetization are the two thresholds determining whether KAN in KWS is a "paper-level curiosity" or an "engineering-level option." There is also a question the paper has not touched upon but is worth asking: in subword-level modeling, which subwords' discrimination does KAN actually improve (is it consonant-dense "ture" or vowel-starting "a")? Locating the gain to linguistic units can both verify the explanation of "high-level feature modeling" and potentially guide keyword selection in reverse—such fine-grained attribution analysis is not reported in the paper, left for subsequent work.
