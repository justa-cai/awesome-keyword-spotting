# Relational Proxy Loss for Audio-Text based Keyword Spotting

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Youngmoon Jung, Seungjin Lee, Joon-Young Yang, Jaeyoung Roh, Chang Woo Han, Hoon-Young Cho (Samsung Research, Seoul)
- **Date**: June 2024 (arXiv v1 submitted on June 8, 2024, Interspeech 2024 paper format)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.05314
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, text-based enrollment, deep metric learning, relational knowledge distillation, proxy-based loss, acoustic word embedding, open vocabulary

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) aims to identify pre-specified keywords within a continuous audio stream, serving as a persistent trigger entry for voice assistant devices. Typical examples include "Alexa," "Hi Bixby," and "Okay Google." The paper categorizes KWS into two main types: fixed-keyword KWS (where users can only use vendor-provided wake words, corresponding to references [1-3]) and flexible (user-defined) KWS (corresponding to references [4-13]), which allows users to register and utter arbitrary keywords. Flexible KWS is further divided into two routes based on the enrollment modality: audio enrollment (where users need to verbally repeat the target keyword several times, references [4-7]) and text enrollment (where users only need to type the keyword spelling, references [8-13]).

The appeal of the text-enrollment route lies in user experience: it does not require users to read the target word multiple times; a single keyboard input suffices for enrollment. As demand for user convenience grows, attention to this route is continuously increasing. However, it introduces an inherent cross-modal challenge: during the enrollment phase, the system sees text, while during the usage phase, the system hears audio. Matching occurs between a Text Embedding (TE) and an Acoustic Embedding (AE). The paper explicitly names this "text for enrollment, audio for testing" task as **audio-text based KWS**.

The technical foundation of mainstream text-enrollment KWS is Deep Metric Learning (DML) [14]: it trains both acoustic and text encoders to bring the AE and TE of the same word closer together in the embedding space, while pushing apart the AE and TE of different words. Runtime acceptance or rejection of an audio segment depends on its similarity metric relative to the enrolled text. Therefore, the shape of the DML loss function directly determines the discriminative upper bound of the entire system. This is the engineering context for Samsung Research's work: wake-word systems must be deployed on edge devices (the paper explicitly states that architecture choices are constrained by deployment compute capabilities), feature an open vocabulary, and remain robust under real-world acoustic conditions such as watermark noise and reverberation. The loss function is one of the few levers that can improve performance purely through training without increasing inference costs. This is why all the work in this paper focuses on loss design.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper reviews existing audio-text KWS works along two main lines and points out their respective limitations.

The first line is audio-text alignment. Reference [11] uses cross-attention, and [13] uses self-attention modules to extract alignment information (referred to as "pattern") between audio and text. A binary classifier is then trained using cross-entropy to determine whether the aligned audio and text inputs represent the same keyword. Nishu et al. [12] propose a dynamic sequence partitioning algorithm for alignment, and the aligned audio and text embeddings are trained using a contrastive loss. The focus of this line is "how to align the two modalities at the sequence level," and the loss function itself is relatively naive.

The second line is DML loss function design, which is also the main line of this paper. He et al. [8] propose a multi-view learning method, using triplet loss to jointly learn acoustic and text embeddings. Jung and Kim [9] extend this to proxy-based DML, using text embeddings as proxies and proposing Asymmetric Proxy Loss (AsyP). Reference [10] further proposes AdaMS, turning the scale and margin parameters in the DML framework into keyword-class-learnable parameters, alleviating the problem of manual hyperparameter tuning.

The core shortcoming pointed out by the paper can be summarized in one sentence: regardless of which line, the comparison between AE and TE is point-to-point (P2P)—calculating similarity or distance pair by pair, completely ignoring the structural relationships within the embedding set, i.e., which words are close to each other, at what angles they are arranged, and how they are distributed around class centers. Park et al.'s [15] Relational Knowledge Distillation work has already emphasized that the structure within the embedding space is an important component of what the trained model has learned. In other words, the TE set generated by the text encoder for the entire vocabulary carries rich inter-word structural information (e.g., words with similar phoneme compositions or spellings are closer), which is a free, prior-supervised signal that is wasted in all P2P training.

Furthermore, hyperparameters $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\lambda$ in the AsyP/AdaMS family determine the class boundaries and violation penalty strength in the embedding space. They are crucial for training but require manual optimization. Although AdaMS turns them into learnable parameters, they are still learned independently per class, and the relational structure between classes does not enter the training objective.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

The key challenge to be solved by this paper can be summarized as: how to upgrade "point-to-point comparison" to "structure-to-structure comparison," transferring the inter-class structural relationships contained in the text embedding set to the acoustic embeddings, while not destroying the pair-wise discriminative ability established by P2P training. This breaks down into three layers of challenges. First, how to define and measure structural relationships—requiring the design of a relational potential function that can characterize the geometry of the embedding set, is differentiable, can be computed efficiently within a mini-batch, and solves the problem of inconsistent dimensions between the outputs of two independent encoders. Second, how to combine structural loss with P2P loss—whether pure structural loss is sufficient to support training independently, whether multiple structural losses interfere with each other, and which combinations are optimal, all of which need to be answered via ablation studies. Third, engineering constraints—Samsung's deployment scenario has hard limits on encoder size (the acoustic encoder has only 2.2M parameters). Any performance improvement cannot come at the cost of expanding the model; it must come from the training criterion.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The system adopts a classic dual-encoder architecture, with both sides outputting unified 512-dimensional embeddings. This is the prerequisite for directly calculating cosine similarity between AE and TE, and the foundation for all subsequent structural comparisons.

The acoustic side uses ECAPA-TDNN [27] (derived from the Speaker Verification task's Emphasized Channel Attention, Propagation and Aggregation TDNN structure), with 256 filters in the convolutional layers and a total of 2.2M parameters. After statistical pooling, it outputs a 512-dimensional AE. The paper does not elaborate on the reasoning for this choice, but it can be inferred: this structure has been fully validated on speaker verification tasks for its fine-grained discriminative ability between "same identity/different identity." The task shape of word-level acoustic embedding is highly isomorphic to this (both map variable-length speech segments into discriminative vectors); statistical pooling aggregates frame-level features into fixed-length segment-level vectors, naturally adapting to word-level inputs.

The text side consists of a pre-trained Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenizer [28] and a two-layer 512-hidden-unit bidirectional LSTM, totaling 10M parameters. The tokenizer is implemented using HuggingFace's character-level tokenizer [29] trained on an internal corpus of 50M English texts, with a vocabulary of 100 tokens. Text is tokenized and mapped to a 1024-dimensional vector sequence via a trainable lookup table, fed into the bi-LSTM; the bidirectional output at each time step is concatenated, followed by global average pooling, and then passed through a fully connected layer to obtain a 512-dimensional TE. Notably, the text side has more parameters than the acoustic side (10M vs. 2.2M), but the text encoder runs only once during the enrollment phase and is not part of the resident inference path on the edge device. Therefore, the scale pressure is concentrated on the acoustic encoder side—this explains why the architecture scales are asymmetric on both sides. The paper explicitly states that these design choices depend on the deployment compute available on the target device.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

First, let us formalize the P2P baseline. Let the mini-batch be denoted as $X = \{(a_i, t_i, y_i) | i = 1..N\}$, where $a_i$ is the acoustic embedding of the $i$-th speech segment, $t_i$ is the text embedding of the corresponding text, and $y_i \in \{1..K\}$ is the word label. The AsyP loss (Equation 1) is defined as the sum of the anchor-positive sample term and the anchor-negative sample term:

$$L_{AsyP} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{1}{\alpha}\text{ELSE}_{j\in P_i}\left(\alpha(\lambda - S(t_i, a_j))\right) + \text{MSP}_{k\in N_i}\left(\beta(S(a_i, t_k) - \lambda)\right)\right]$$

where $P_i$ is the set of indices for same-class positive samples, $N_i$ is the set of indices for different-class negative samples, and $S(\cdot,\cdot)$ is cosine similarity; ELSE is the extended LogSumExp [14], and MSP is the Mean Softplus [16]. The first term pulls the anchor text $t_i$ closer to same-class acoustic samples $a_j$, and the second term pushes the anchor acoustic $a_i$ away from different-class texts $t_k$. TE here acts as a proxy, but in the second term, it plays the role of a negative sample rather than an anchor—this is the meaning of "asymmetric." The baseline P2P system AdaMS builds upon AsyP by turning $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\lambda$ into class-learnable parameters, with hyperparameter initial values fixed at $\alpha=2$, $\beta=50$, $\lambda=0.1$.

**RPL Unified Framework**: Let $X_{an}$ denote the set of all $n$-tuples derived from $X$, for example, $X_{a2} = \{(a_i, a_j) | i \neq j\}$, $X_{a3} = \{(a_i, a_j, a_k) | i \neq j \neq k\}$; the text side $X_{tn}$ is defined similarly. The goal of RPL is to "quantify the relational energy of a given $n$-tuple via a relational potential function $\phi$," training the acoustic encoder to replicate the relational structure of the text encoder, aligning corresponding tuples of the two modalities using a regression loss.

**Distance-based RPL-D (Equations 2-4)**:

$$L_{RPL\text{-}D} = \sum_{(t_i,t_j)\in X_{t2},\ (a_i,a_j)\in X_{a2}} h_\delta\left(\phi_D(t_i,t_j),\ \phi_D(a_i,a_j)\right)$$

where $h_\delta$ is the Huber loss [17] with a scaling factor of 1, aiming to reduce the difference in relational energy measured by both sides. Other RPL variants reuse the same $h_\delta$. $\phi_D$ is defined as normalized Euclidean distance:

$$\phi_D(a_i,a_j) = \frac{1}{\mu_a}\|a_i - a_j\|_2,\qquad \mu_a = \frac{1}{|X_{a2}|}\sum_{(a_i,a_j)\in X_{a2}}\|a_i - a_j\|_2$$

$\mu_a$ is the average distance between all AE pairs in the batch, serving a normalization role. Why normalization is necessary: AE and TE come from two independent encoders, and there is no reason for their output distances to have the same dimension. Directly comparing raw distances would cause the regression loss to be dominated by the side with the larger scale. Dividing by the respective modality's average pairwise distance pulls both sides to the dimensionless level of "relative distance," comparing only structure, not absolute scale. $\phi_D(t_i,t_j)$ on the text side is defined in the same way.

**Angle-based RPL-A (Equations 5-6)**: Given a triplet, calculate the angle formed by the three embedding points in space. To simplify computation, the paper does not take the arccosine but directly calculates the cosine value using the dot product:

$$\phi_A(a_i,a_j,a_k) = \cos\angle a_i a_j a_k = \frac{u_{ij}\cdot u_{kj}}{\|u_{ij}\|\|u_{kj}\|},\quad u_{ij}=a_i-a_j,\ u_{kj}=a_k-a_k$$

$$L_{RPL\text{-}A} = \sum_{X_{t3}, X_{a3}} h_\delta\left(\phi_A(t_i,t_j,t_k),\ \phi_A(a_i,a_j,a_k)\right)$$

Why introduce angles in addition to distances: Euclidean distance only characterizes the "closeness" between two points, but cannot characterize the "angular arrangement" among three points. Angle relationships are sensitive to the overall radiation structure of the embedding space (which word is in what direction relative to which word), providing an orthogonal complement to distance information. Using cosine values instead of angle values itself saves the computation of arccosine and maintains the simplicity and differentiability of the dot product form.

**Prototype-based RPL-P (Equations 7-8)**: Borrowing the idea of Prototypical Networks [18], the AE prototype for class $k$ is defined as the mean of within-class samples:

$$c_a^k = \frac{1}{|X^k|}\sum_{a_i\in X^k} a_i$$

Since each keyword class is represented by a unique TE, the TE itself corresponds to the class center; but for notational consistency, the above formula is also applied to TE. The final loss is:

$$L_{RPL\text{-}P} = \sum_{x_i\in X}\sum_{k=1}^{K} h_\delta\left(\phi_D(t_i, c_t^k),\ \phi_D(a_i, c_a^k)\right)$$

RPL-P transfers the structural information of "distance between proxies (TEs)" into "distance from AE to its corresponding class center." The difference between RPL-P and RPL-D lies in the reference frame: RPL-D uses any sample pair within the batch as reference, while RPL-P uses class centers as reference, explicitly constraining each AE to approach the class center geometry prescribed by the TE structure.

**Degenerate Case Analysis** (end of Section 2.2 of the paper, reflecting the authors' detailed investigation of loss behavior): The above derivations assume that the classes of TE within the mini-batch are distinct. When classes overlap, RPL degenerates into known losses—in RPL-D, $\phi_D(t_i,t_j)=0$, and the loss becomes pulling $a_i$ and $a_j$ closer, behaving similarly to cross-entropy for AE classification; RPL-P behaves like prototype loss [18] when labels match and $\phi_D(t_i,c_t^k)=0$. This shows that RPL is not a newly invented constraint out of thin air, but a natural generalization of unifying cross-entropy and prototype loss into the larger framework of "structure transfer."

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Paradigm Shift from Point-to-Point to Structure-to-Structure

This is the most core idea in the entire paper. Traditional DML treats AE and TE as two piles of isolated points, calculating similarity pair by pair; RPL treats each as a point set with internal structure, letting the structure of the AE set approximate the structure of the TE set (Figure 1 (a) P2P vs. (b) S2S). The motivation comes directly from Park et al.'s [15] Relational Knowledge Distillation: the structure of the embedding space is a key component of what the trained model has learned. Specifically for this task, the structure on the TE side has a clear source—the text encoder gives closer embeddings to words with similar phoneme compositions or spellings. This "vocabulary map" is a free supervised signal that P2P methods have never utilized. RPL is essentially a cross-modal relational distillation: the teacher is the relational structure generated by the text encoder, and the student is the embedding geometry of the acoustic encoder.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Three Complementary Relational Potential Functions (Distance, Angle, Prototype)

A single geometric metric can only cover one slice of the structure. The paper designs three orthogonal slices: RPL-D manages relative distances between samples, RPL-A manages angular arrangements of triplets, and RPL-P manages distances from samples to class centers. Ablation (Table 1) proves that the three are not redundant: P2P baseline 71.9% AP, adding RPL-D to 76.7%, adding RPL-A to 77.3%, adding all three to 79.4%—each structural information has an independent contribution. Why all three are needed: distance structure constrains the order of neighbor closeness, angle structure constrains directional arrangement, and prototype structure constrains the centripetal nature of class clusters. The three characterize different degrees of freedom of embedding geometry; losing any one would leave a deformation space that can be accommodated by other degrees of freedom.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Auxiliary Loss (AL) Borrowed from Cross-Paradigm

The auxiliary loss design in Section 3.3 of the paper demonstrates a beautiful engineering intuition: the training loss does not need to focus solely on the AE-TE relationship; the multi-task loss of speech-enrollment KWS (PATN [7]) can be brought over to give the acoustic encoder an extra meal. The three auxiliary losses are: $L_{pc}$ (prototype-center matching loss), based on the centroid calculation from Equation (7), which is the average Euclidean distance between each prototype (TE) in the batch and its corresponding AE class center, aiming to make $t_2$ and $c_{a2}$ in Figure 2(c) coincide to zero—because both are embeddings representing that word class in their respective modalities and should be close; $L_{mono}$ (cross-entropy for frame-level senone classification), where the softmax layer is inserted on frame-level features before global average pooling, supplementing the acoustic encoder with pronunciation structure supervision at the senone level; $L_{triplet}$ (word-level triplet loss), using 512-dimensional AE as input. $L_{mono}$ and $L_{triplet}$ act solely on the acoustic encoder, equivalent to letting the acoustic encoder simultaneously learn the native task of "acoustic word embedding" for speech-enrollment KWS. Table 2 shows: RPL alone 79.4%, adding $L_{pc}$ to 80.9%, adding $L_{mono}+L_{triplet}$ to 81.0%, adding all four to 82.5%. The authors' interpretation is that these auxiliary losses amplify the model's ability to distinguish subtle differences between classes.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to AsyP/AdaMS [9][10]: The latter is pure P2P, constraining only AE-TE pair-wise similarity, with hyperparameters manually set or learned per class; RPL, while retaining AdaMS as a P2P component, superimposes an S2S component. The supervision signal expands from "point similarity" to "structural similarity," representing a generational difference in training criteria. Compared to the audio-text alignment route [11-13]: The paper explicitly states that it does not compare with alignment methods, because the goal of this paper is to improve the DML loss function rather than the alignment mechanism. The two lines are orthogonal and can theoretically be superimposed—this is both a clear definition of its own contribution boundaries and leaves room for future combinations. Compared to Relational Knowledge Distillation RKD [15]: RKD transfers structure between teacher-student networks in the same modality (visual model distillation), while RPL moves this idea to a cross-modal scenario (TE to AE), and the anchor for structural comparison is the text embedding acting as a class proxy rather than the per-sample output of specific samples. Coupled with the task-specific property that "TE is naturally a class center," it constructs a prototype-based structural loss.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The training set uses King-ASR-066 [19]: 2.6k English native speakers, 16kHz sampling, recorded in home/office environments. All sentences were first forced-aligned using Montreal Forced Aligner [20] to cut into word-level segments, totaling 4.6k hours of speech. The training set consists of phrases of one to five words, totaling 211,676 classes. Data augmentation: clean speech is convolved with synthetic Room Impulse Responses (RIR) from OpenSLR [21], and various noises such as babble, car, and music are added, with SNR randomly selected between -3 and 25 dB.

The evaluation set uses WSJ [22] (obtained via Kaldi's WSJ recipe [23]): the development set is {dev93, dev93-5k}, and the test set is {eval92, eval92-5k, eval93, eval93-5k}; the former contains 16,839 word-level segments (3,289 unique words) and the latter contains 18,274 word-level segments (3,239 unique words). To simulate real-world scenarios, test speech is also convolved with OpenSLR synthetic RIR and overlaid with MUSAN noise [24], with SNR from 5 to 25 dB, resulting in noisy and reverberant versions equal in quantity to the original sets. During evaluation, 1.3M positive pairs and 13M negative pairs are randomly sampled from the test set.

Training configuration: Implemented in TensorFlow, AdamW optimizer, initial learning rate 1e-4, halved every 20 epochs, weight decay 1e-5, for a total of 100 epochs, completed in about one day on two A100s. Each mini-batch contains 500 utterances, from 250 different keywords, with exactly 2 utterances per word—this "fixed 2 samples per class" batch construction is precisely to satisfy the assumption in Section 2.2 that "classes do not overlap within the batch." Input features are 40-dimensional log Mel filter bank coefficients, 25ms frame length, 10ms frame shift, with per-utterance mean normalization. To ensure fair comparison, all systems (including baselines) share the same data, architecture, and training settings, with only the training criterion differing.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Two metrics: Equal Error Rate (EER) and Average Precision (AP). EER is chosen because it measures error rate independent of decision threshold (the value where False Acceptance Rate equals False Rejection Rate), which is the standard reporting method in KWS literature [11-13], directly corresponding to the two types of costs in wake-word systems (the balance point between false wake-ups and missed wake-ups). AP is chosen because it examines the quality of the entire ranking—the extent to which positive pairs are ranked ahead of negative pairs after sorting all audio-text pairs by similarity. It is the standard reporting method in acoustic word embedding literature [8,9,25,26] and better reflects discriminative power at the ranking level for flexible KWS with "open vocabulary, where thresholds may vary by scenario." One metric looks at single-point decision, the other at global ranking, forming a complement; reporting both also facilitates alignment with the traditions of the two respective literature streams.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Table 3 presents the main comparison. PATN [7] (speech enrollment, P2P): EER 7.58%, AP 74.5%. AsyP [9] (text enrollment, P2P): EER 7.04%, AP 71.3%. AdaMS [10] (text enrollment, P2P): EER 6.99%, AP 71.9%. This paper's RPL (text enrollment, P2P+S2S): EER 6.30%, AP 79.4%. This paper's RPL+AL (text enrollment, P2P+S2S): EER 5.84%, AP 82.5%.

Three key readings. First, pure RPL (without auxiliary loss) reduces EER from AdaMS's 6.99% to 6.30% and increases AP from 71.9% to 79.4%, while architecture, data, and batch composition are all aligned, with only the training criterion differing. Therefore, this 7.5 percentage point AP improvement can be cleanly attributed to the structural loss itself (the paper marks a relative improvement of 10.43%). Second, RPL+AL shows a relative EER improvement of 22.96% compared to PATN and a relative AP improvement of 15.71% compared to AsyP—surpassing the speech enrollment system without requiring users to endure repeated word reading for enrollment, which is a key argument for the commercial landing of the text enrollment route. Third, the paper deliberately does not compare with the alignment methods [11-13], citing different research lines. This also means there is no experimental answer to "whether RPL superimposed on an alignment framework still yields gains," which the authors leave as future work.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

The RPL ablation in Table 1 has three layers of findings. First layer: adding each of the three RPLs individually to P2P is effective (71.9% to 76.7%/77.3%), and RPL-A alone is slightly stronger than RPL-D (77.3% vs. 76.7%), indicating that angle structure has the most information in this set of settings. Second layer: superposition order is sensitive—adding RPL-A to "P2P+RPL-D" yields 77.0% (a decrease instead of increase), while adding RPL-P yields 78.3% (an increase); adding RPL-D or RPL-P to "P2P+RPL-A" does not increase (77.2%); but opening all four losses is optimal at 79.4%, suggesting that the value of RPL-A is fully released only when both RPL-D and RPL-P are present (the paper's original wording: comparing the 79.4% and 78.3% systems, RPL-A enhances "P2P+RPL-D+RPL-P"). This non-monotonic superposition behavior implies tension among the three geometric constraints, which the paper does not analyze in depth. Third layer: using only the three S2S losses, removing P2P, drops AP to 66.8%—even lower than the P2P baseline of 71.9%. This "pure structure doesn't work" negative result is very informative: the pair-wise alignment constraint of AE-TE is the foundation; structure transfer can only be incremental. The structure of TE is, after all, a prior from the text encoder's perspective; the discriminative details of the acoustic space itself must be anchored by P2P similarity supervision. The conclusion is that using P2P and S2S simultaneously is superior to either alone, and opening all four losses is optimal.

The auxiliary loss ablation in Table 2 shows that the three paths of gain are nearly additive: adding $L_{pc}$ alone improves by 1.5 percentage points (79.4% to 80.9%), adding $L_{mono}+L_{triplet}$ alone improves by 1.6 percentage points (79.4% to 81.0%), and adding all together improves by 3.1 percentage points to 82.5%, basically linear superposition. This shows that the phoneme-level and word-level supervision within the acoustic encoder and the AE-TE relational supervision act at different levels and do not compete for capacity—one manages "how to encode the pronunciation structure of this sound segment well," and the other manages "how to make it correspond to the text."

## Main Contributions

1. Proposes Relational Proxy Loss, upgrading the DML training of audio-text KWS from point-to-point comparison to structure-to-structure comparison. In the context of this task, it is the first to introduce the idea of relational knowledge distillation into cross-modal acoustic-text embedding learning, activating the inter-word structural supervised signal within the TE set that had been idle.
2. Designs three complementary relational potential functions (RPL-D distance, RPL-A angle, RPL-P prototype) and proves that their combination with P2P achieves optimality (Table 1: AP from 71.9% to 79.4%, relative improvement 10.43%).
3. Proposes and validates a cross-paradigm auxiliary loss scheme, grafting the multi-task loss of speech-enrollment KWS (prototype-center matching, frame-level senone classification, word-level triplet) onto text-enrollment training, gaining another 3.1 percentage points (Table 2: 79.4% to 82.5%).
4. On the noisy and reverberant WSJ evaluation, RPL+AL surpasses speech-enrollment PATN (relative EER improvement 22.96%) and text-enrollment AsyP/AdaMS (relative AP improvement 15.71%) with EER 5.84% and AP 82.5%, proving that the text-enrollment route can achieve performance without compromise (Table 3).
5. Provides valuable negative results: pure S2S (66.8%) is worse than pure P2P (71.9%), defining the correct usage of structural loss as superposition rather than replacement, and drawing a safety boundary for subsequent cross-modal metric learning work.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, the assumption of non-overlapping classes in mini-batch. The derivation of RPL relies on TE classes being distinct within the batch; the paper ensures this by using a batch construction of "250 words x 2 sentences," but the cost is that the breadth of class coverage in each batch is hard-constrained by the batch size, and the training recipe is sensitive to batch composition. If replaced with a conventional batch with overlapping classes, the loss degenerates into cross-entropy or prototype loss (the paper admits this), and the intensity of structure transfer will be diluted. Second, the combinatorial complexity of triplets. $X_{a3}$ is the set of all triplets, the scale of which grows cubically with the number of samples in the batch. The paper does not report the actual sampling strategy for RPL-A or the decomposition of training overhead for each loss (total training is about one day on two A100s, but not split down to the loss level). Third, one-sided dependence on the source of structure. RPL assumes that the TE structure itself is reliable supervision, but the quality of the text encoder's "vocabulary map" depends on the tokenizer and bi-LSTM trained on internal 50M English texts. The paper does not report whether the text encoder is jointly trained or frozen (not reported), nor does it provide a sensitivity analysis of TE structure quality. Fourth, the shape and weight of the regression loss: the scaling factor of the Huber loss is fixed at 1, and the four losses are added with equal weight. The paper does not report ablation on structural loss weights or loss shapes.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First, gaps in the comparison scope: it only compares with DML-route AsyP/AdaMS and speech-enrollment PATN, deliberately avoiding concurrent systems in the audio-text alignment route [11-13], citing different main lines. However, this leaves a blank regarding "how it actually compares to the strongest current systems for the same task." Second, representativeness of the evaluation domain: although MUSAN noise and synthetic RIR (SNR 5 to 25 dB) are added on WSJ, WSJ itself is read speech, and the diversity of speakers, channels, and accents is far less than the trigger scenarios of real far-field devices; there is no evaluation on recordings from real devices. Third, statistical robustness: the paper does not report variance from multiple random seed repetitions, and the sampling of 1.3M positive pairs and 13M negative pairs is only described as a single random selection. Fourth, missing deployment-side metrics: besides EER and AP, the runtime latency, model size increase, and streaming feasibility most concerned in actual deployment are not reported—RPL is a training-time loss and theoretically does not increase inference cost, which is one of its core selling points, but the paper does not provide quantitative numbers for the inference side (not reported). Fifth, lack of error analysis: the AP improvement is large (71.9% to 82.5%), but which types of words (short words, phonetically similar words, multi-word phrases) benefit the most, and what the failure cases look like, are not reported by the paper.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The direction pointed out by the authors themselves: integrate RPL into the audio-text alignment framework—the alignment route currently uses naive loss functions to train binary classifiers, and RPL, as a plug-and-play structural regularizer, has clear space for superposition. Starting from the gaps in this paper, there are three other directions. First, structure transfer can be made bidirectional: currently it is unidirectional from TE to AE. Letting TE also receive acoustic structure information from the AE side may correct parts of the TE structure that conflict with acoustic reality. Second, extend relational potential functions from sample pairs and triplets within the batch to vocabulary-level structure across batches, alleviating the constraint of the non-overlapping class assumption on batch construction. Third, perform mechanistic analysis on the non-monotonic superposition behavior of RPL-A, understanding the source of tension among the three geometric constraints, which may derive more optimal combination weights or curriculum-based superposition orders. For engineering reproducers, the most transferable judgment from this paper is: when two modal encoders are aligned using metric learning, the internal structure of the embedding set on one side (especially the side with stronger structural priors and lighter inference costs) is a free and underutilized supervised signal; generalizing this judgment to similar tasks such as speech retrieval and cross-modal matching is more valuable than reproducing the specific loss form.
