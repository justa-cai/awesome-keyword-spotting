## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) requires locating the start and end times of predefined keywords in a continuous audio stream and providing their class labels. It is a special case of Sound Event Detection (SED): it must detect the onset and offset of events while correctly identifying their associated classes. Unlike conventional KWS, the scenario addressed in this paper is **few-shot open-set keyword spotting**: each keyword class is represented by only a very small number (few-shot) of speech instances. Test sentences may or may not contain keywords, and the system must excel in both "accuracy" and "low false alarm rate." This setting corresponds to real-world product requirements—when users customize wake-up words, they are often unwilling to record more than three or five times, making it impossible to retrain a discriminative model for each new word.

Under the condition that discriminative models cannot be trained, template matching (query-by-example) is the natural choice, and Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) is the most classic distance metric among them. It compares sequences by allowing small temporal shifts between them, essentially being a relaxed generalization of Euclidean distance along the time axis. DTW applications in audio span bioacoustic event detection, music information retrieval (multi-pitch estimation), and keyword spotting. The classic approach extracts hand-crafted speech features (MFCC or HFCC) from isolated training samples as templates for sub-sequence DTW. Recent advances use neural networks to learn discriminative embeddings with temporal resolution as templates, significantly improving performance even when training samples are extremely limited (References [10][11], where [11] refers to the TACos embeddings proposed by the same author).

The domain pain point is: **the computational cost of template matching scales linearly with the number of templates**. Standard DTW aligns two sequences of length N and M, with a complexity of O(N·M). When detecting C classes with K query samples per class, the naive approach requires performing sub-sequence DTW between all samples of all classes and the target sequence, raising the complexity to O(N·M·C·K). The target sequence is continuous speech (each validation/test set in the experiments is about 10 minutes long), so N is very large. This makes the runtime grow linearly with the number of samples per class K. The paper explicitly states that this is "potentially highly impractical or even infeasible" in practice.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

- **Acceleration techniques do not target the K factor**: Existing DTW acceleration methods include masking large areas of the cost matrix with constraints (e.g., Itakura constraint, Sakoe-Chiba band), pruning hopeless paths, and parallelizing the accumulation of the cost matrix along the diagonal direction for GPU acceleration. These techniques optimize the constant factor or parallelism within a "single DTW," but do not touch the linear amplification structure of "performing K full DP scans on the long target sequence for K samples per class." When K increases, the acceleration they provide is overwhelmed by multiplicative overhead.
- **Fréchet mean compression washes out intra-class variability**: Another approach is to query only a single Fréchet mean (the "average sequence" in the DTW sense) per class, reducing complexity to O(N·M·C). However, there are two problems: First, computing the Fréchet mean under the DTW metric is NP-complete (Reference [19]), so in practice, heuristic approximations like DBA (DTW Barycenter Averaging) or COMASA are used. Second, and more critically, using the mean as a query sample significantly degrades detection performance because the variability provided by individual samples—different speaker characteristics, different contexts, different pronunciation durations and rhythms—is not fully captured. The mean is a "smoothing" operation, while keyword discrimination恰恰 relies on those details that are smoothed out.
- **Cluster mean schemes are still incomplete**: References [23][24] propose clustering samples of the same class first, then computing the Fréchet mean for each cluster. This essentially reduces the number of query samples from K to the number of clusters. Each class still requires multiple templates (multiple DTWs), and the paper explicitly states that "the variability provided by individual samples is still not fully retained"—cluster averaging is still a lossy compression, just with a smaller compression ratio.
- **No intermediate form between the two routes**: Either retain all samples and pay O(K) times the runtime, or compress into a single template and suffer precision loss. The gap where "performance is comparable to per-sample querying, while runtime is close to single-mean querying" was blank before this paper.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The core tension is the **dilemma between preserving variability and computational efficiency**. The specific challenges the paper aims to solve can be broken down into three layers: (1) Mathematically, how to make the "one DTW per class" computational method retain the contribution of all K samples in an information-theoretic sense, rather than performing lossy compression like the mean; (2) Structurally, how to reorganize the computation into "parallelizable element-wise parts" and "strictly sequential DP parts," shifting the K-factor overhead from sequential DP to parallelizable computation; (3) Supporting structures, the prerequisite for element-wise merging multiple cost matrices is that all samples share the same time grid, which requires a mechanism to align samples of different lengths to a unified coordinate system, and this mechanism itself must not introduce new precision loss. The standard for success is clearly stated by the paper: performance is "very close" to per-sample querying, and runtime is only "slightly slower" than the Fréchet mean scheme.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall process of multi-sample DTW consists of four steps (Fig. 1), executed independently for each keyword class:

- **Step 1 (Compute Reference Template)**: Compute the Fréchet mean for each class to serve as a class-specific reference template, whose role is to "connect" the cost matrices of all query samples of the same class. In the experiments, the DBA algorithm implemented in ts-learn is used for initialization, with a Sakoe-Chiba band of radius 1, and a fixed length equal to the average length of the corresponding query samples; subsequently, a "modification" is applied to the resulting standard Fréchet mean (see Technical Innovation 2). The paper specifically notes: when using multi-sample DTW, the Fréchet mean algorithm can theoretically be chosen arbitrarily; this specific choice was adopted because it "significantly improved performance (see Table I)."
- **Step 2 (Transform Samples)**: Transform all query samples of this class to the same time dimension as the reference template. The method is to align each query sample with the reference template, and replace the corresponding entry of the reference template with the "arithmetic mean of all entries matched to that position"—equivalent to applying one iteration of DBA to the reference template for each query sample to correct it. The corrected result is the transformed version of that sample.
- **Step 3 (Compute Cost Matrix)**: First, stack the cost matrices between all transformed samples and the target sequence into a three-dimensional cost tensor, then take the element-wise minimum along the sample dimension, collapsing the tensor into a single standard cost matrix. The significance of this step is: the final DTW path can **switch** between the cost matrices of different query samples.
- **Step 4 (Apply DTW)**: Run standard (sub-sequence) DTW on the merged cost matrix for each class to obtain the similarity score for that class. This step is identical to when using the Fréchet mean as a query.

The deep motivation of this design is a **redistribution of computational load**. The runtime of DTW consists of two parts: computing local costs (element-wise, no data dependency, naturally parallelizable) and the DP scan for accumulated costs (each cell depends on left/upper neighbors, strict sequential dependency, difficult to parallelize). For long target sequences, the DP scan is the major part. Since the local costs for K samples cannot be saved (saving them loses variability), this part of the computation is retained, but the K cost matrices are folded into one, so that the most expensive sequential DP runs only once per class. The overhead of the K factor is downgraded from "K sequential DP runs" to "K parallelizable local costs plus one parallelizable element-wise minimum." This is the entire principle behind the runtime approaching the single-mean scheme.

Another design motivation is the **lower envelope idea for information fidelity**. The mean route performs "averaging" (lossy compression) on variability, while multi-sample DTW performs "point-wise optimal selection" (lossless or even gain) on variability: at every intersection of template time grid points and target time grid points, the cost takes the cheapest one among the K samples. Intra-class variability is not wiped out but is completely enclosed in a lower envelope matrix for the DP to freely utilize.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

**Local Cost and Merging**: Suppose class c has K query samples. After Step 2, the k-th transformed sample is $q^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$ (L is the reference template length, d is the feature dimension), and the target sequence is $x \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$. The local cost uses cosine distance:

$$c_k(i,j) = 1 - \frac{q^{(k)}_i \cdot x_j}{\lVert q^{(k)}_i \rVert \, \lVert x_j \rVert}$$

The K cost matrices are stacked along the sample dimension to form a cost tensor, and the element-wise minimum is taken to obtain the merged cost matrix:

$$C(i,j) = \min_{k=1,\dots,K} \; c_k(i,j)$$

**DP Recursion**: Sub-sequence DTW is performed using a non-standard step set $\{(1,1),(1,2),(2,1)\}$ (the path can advance 1 or 2 steps on the template axis and 1 or 2 steps on the target axis at each step, i.e., allowing skipping of every other frame entry in either sequence):

$$D(i,j) = C(i,j) + \min\{D(i-1,j-1),\, D(i-1,j-2),\, D(i-2,j-1)\}$$

Sub-sequence DTW allows the path to start and end freely on the target sequence. The class similarity score is the accumulated cost of the optimal path (lower cost means higher similarity).

**Lower Bound Property (Why performance can catch up to or even surpass per-sample querying)**: The optimal DTW path for any single sample $k$ is still a feasible path in the merged matrix $C$, and the local cost at each step of this path does not increase after taking the minimum. Therefore,

$$D^{*}_{\text{multi}} \;\le\; \min_{k} \; D^{*}_{k}$$

That is, the optimal path cost obtained by multi-sample DTW is a lower bound of the optimal costs of all single samples. Per-sample querying ultimately takes the minimum of the optimal costs of each sample, while the reachable path space of multi-sample DTW is strictly larger—the path can switch from one sample to another midway, equivalent to generating new pseudo-samples using "arbitrary template combinations." This is exactly the paper's explanation for "performance is sometimes even slightly better than per-sample querying": new artificial samples are generated, and certain test pronunciations match the recombined template (e.g., "beginning of Sample A followed by end of Sample B") closer than any single real sample.

**Fréchet Mean and DBA**: The definition of the Fréchet mean is $\mu^{*} = \arg\min_{\mu} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathrm{DTW}(\mu, q^{(k)})$. This optimization problem is NP-complete under the DTW metric. DBA uses alternating optimization: fix the mean to perform DTW to obtain the matching relationship between sample entries and mean entries (expectation step), then update the entry at each mean position to the arithmetic mean of all entries matched to that position (maximization step). Step 2's sample transformation is a direct reuse of a single DBA iteration—using the reference template as the initial value, a single query sample as the data, and taking one update step.

**Complexity Analysis**: Steps 1 and 2 are performed only once, belonging to training overhead, with no cost during inference. In Step 3, the complexity of computing K cost matrices is the same as per-sample querying, O(N·M·C·K), but it is fully parallelizable; the element-wise minimum step from tensor to matrix is "relatively expensive but extremely easy to parallelize" (the experiment compared serial and parallel implementations). In Step 4, DP is performed on the merged matrix, with complexity O(N·M·C). The paper's conclusion is stated precisely: after extracting the merged cost matrix, the DP complexity drops from O(N·M·C·K) to O(N·M·C); the total complexity is nominally still O(N·M·C·K), but the **constant is much smaller** than the per-sample scheme—because the most expensive long-sequence DP is executed only once per class. In contrast, the Fréchet mean scheme has a true complexity of O(N·M·C). The paper admits this is the theoretical lower bound for runtime ("can be assumed optimal"), and multi-sample DTW can only approximate it, not reach it.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Cost Tensor and Element-wise Minimum Aggregation

This is the core contribution of the paper. In traditional thinking, "merging multiple templates" has only two options: merge features first then calculate distance (mean, lossy), or calculate distance separately then merge scores (per-sample, expensive). This paper takes a third path: **calculate distance first, merge at the cost matrix level, then perform only one DTW**. The difference between the three options can be characterized by information flow: the mean performs arithmetic averaging in feature space, which forces all samples to contribute the same linear combination at the same position before matching, canceling out speaker differences in feature space; element-wise minimum performs point-wise selection in cost space, where each time grid point independently selects the best-matching sample, and different grid points can choose different samples—thus granting the DP path the freedom to switch between templates of different speakers.

The benefits of this design are two-fold. The first is the performance lower bound guarantee (as stated above, $D^{*}_{\text{multi}} \le \min_k D^{*}_k$), ensuring it is no worse than per-sample querying. The second is combinatorial gain: the switchable path space of K samples covers an exponential number of template recombination methods, equivalent to implicitly encoding a pseudo-template set much larger than K using K real samples. This is the mathematical expression of "capturing variability." The paper's original wording is "generating new artificial samples by enabling arbitrary template combinations."

The reason for choosing "minimum" over other element-wise aggregation functions like "mean/maximum" is also logical: DTW seeks the minimum cost path, and taking the minimum maintains interpretability consistency (the merged matrix is still a valid cost matrix, requiring no modification to DP); and the minimum guarantees the lower bound property. Taking the mean would re-introduce the smoothing problem, and taking the maximum would become a "worst-case sample perspective," both of which are contrary to the goal.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Two-Step DBA to Construct "Alternating" Fréchet Mean

The second contribution of the paper is a modification procedure for the standard Fréchet mean, which stands independently and can be used alone (the "altered Fréchet mean" row in Table I). The process is divided into two steps: first, calculate the standard Fréchet mean using standard DBA (based on the subgradient implementation in Reference [25], Sakoe-Chiba band radius 1, length fixed to the average size of query samples); then **apply DBA a second time**, but using sub-sequence DTW and the non-standard step set {(1,1),(1,2),(2,1)}, with the local metric still being cosine distance.

The principle is worth expanding: the non-standard step set allows skipping every other frame entry during matching, so the update in the second DBA no longer replaces all entries of the mean one by one, but "absorbs new information every other entry." The resulting modified mean exhibits an "alternating structure of original mean entries and new entries" on the time axis (the right half of Fig. 2 clearly shows this alternating texture). The paper states: this allows a single template to **essentially encode two replaceable templates**—even positions are template one, odd positions are template two, and a DTW path can choose to follow only one set of entries by skipping frames. In other words, the modified mean packs two candidate pronunciations into the same template using "time multiplexing," capturing significantly more variability than the standard mean.

The significance of this modification is validated in two usage scenarios: as an independent query template, it significantly outperforms the standard Fréchet mean (see the experimental section); as the Step 1 reference template for multi-sample DTW, it provides a better alignment coordinate system. The paper explicitly states that this choice "significantly improved performance (see Table I)." The deep "why" is: the information bottleneck of the mean lies in "only one entry can be placed at each position." The alternating structure doubles the effective capacity of the template, and the way it doubles (selective skipping) is compatible with the step set of the downstream DTW, requiring no additional decoding logic.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Single-Iteration DBA for Sample Time Warping

Step 2 solves a problem that is easily overlooked but crucial for Step 3: element-wise minimum requires the template axes of the K cost matrices to be fully comparable, i.e., the i-th row of the k-th sample and the i'-th row of the k'-th sample must correspond to the "same pronunciation stage," otherwise point-wise selection becomes a misaligned comparison. Original query samples have varying lengths (different numbers of frames for different speakers saying the same word), so direct stacking cannot align them. The paper's solution is elegant and cheap: using the reference template as an intermediary, perform a single DBA iteration for each query sample separately—replace each entry of the reference template with the "arithmetic mean of all entries in that sample matched to this position." Each transformed sample automatically inherits the length of the reference template (the transformation "creates from the reference template" by replacing entries one by one rather than regenerating), and the K samples thus all fall onto the same time grid.

Why use a "single iteration" instead of iterating to convergence? Because the goal of Step 2 is not to find the mean (that is the job of Step 1), but to find a version that preserves the sample's individuality but whose time structure conforms to the reference coordinate system. A single iteration uses the reference template as the initial value and a single sample as the data, with moderate update magnitude, allowing most acoustic details of the individual (the variability to be preserved) to survive; iterating to convergence would instead pull the sample towards the reference template, re-introducing smoothing. This step also explains the dual role of the "reference template" in the architecture diagram: it is both the query template for the mean route and the alignment anchor for the multi-sample route.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Vs. Per-sample Querying**: In terms of information, the lower bound property of multi-sample DTW guarantees it is no worse; in terms of computation, it compresses K serial DP runs on long sequences into one per class, leaving the K factor only in the parallelizable part. The essence of the difference is that the "min at the score level" is moved to the "min at the cost level," shifting the position of the min operator forward by a whole DP.
- **Vs. Fréchet Mean Route (including DBA/COMASA)**: The mean performs lossy compression in feature space, while multi-sample DTW performs a lossless lower envelope in cost space; the former truly reduces complexity to O(N·M·C) but at the cost of precision, while the latter keeps the nominal complexity unchanged but greatly reduces the constant and is parallel-friendly.
- **Vs. Clustered Multi-Mean Schemes [23][24]**: Clustering is still the idea of "compress first, then match"; the number of pseudo-templates equals the number of clusters, and switching can only occur at the cluster mean granularity; multi-sample DTW does not require presetting the number of clusters, and switching can occur at every frame, making the expressible pseudo-template space a continuous recombination rather than a finite set.
- **Vs. soft-DTW**: soft-DTW uses a smoothed minimum to gain differentiability for training networks, and its original intent is not acceleration; multi-sample DTW maintains the hard minimum and exact DP, with the goal of inference efficiency. The two are orthogonal, and a combination can even be envisioned (see Future Work).
- **Vs. Classic DTW Acceleration (masking/pruning/diagonal parallelization)**: These methods compress the cost of a single DTW, while multi-sample DTW compresses the number of DTW executions; the paper also acknowledges in Future Work that the runtime of all methods can be further reduced via GPU or parallelized cost matrix computation, "since it affects all methods equally, it need not be adopted for comparison"—indicating that the two types of acceleration are completely orthogonal and stackable.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The experiments were conducted on **KWS-DailyTalk** (Reference [11], built on the conversational speech synthesis dataset DailyTalk [27]), which is a few-shot open-set KWS dataset. The training set contains 5 isolated samples for each of 15 keywords: "afternoon, airport, cash, credit card, deposit, dollar, evening, expensive, house, information, money, morning, night, visa, yuan"—obvious financial/travel scenario vocabulary (withdrawal, exchange, hotel), and includes "credit card," a two-word keyword, posing higher requirements for time warping. The validation and test sets consist of 156 and 157 utterances, respectively, each containing zero or more keywords, with each keyword appearing approximately 12 times in each split. Training samples are taken from conversations **different** from the validation/test sentences, ensuring isolation of speakers and contexts—this directly tests the cross-speaker generalization of templates and is the root cause of the "variability capture" problem. In terms of total duration, the training set is only 39 seconds (75 isolated samples), while the validation and test sets are each about 10 minutes long. The 5-shot setting aligns highly with real-world custom wake-up word scenarios; the 10-minute continuous speech length amplifies N, ensuring that runtime differences are not averaged out.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The primary metric is the **micro-averaged event-based F-Score**, using the standard implementation of the SED evaluation package (Reference [28]), with precision and recall reported simultaneously. The rationale can be deduced from the nature of the task: (1) KWS-DailyTalk is an event detection task in continuous speech, where detection must hit both time boundaries (onset/offset) and class; event-level metrics naturally penalize detections where the "class is correct but boundaries are wrong," which frame-level metrics cannot see; (2) The 15 keywords appear approximately 12 times each in the validation/test sets. The number of events per class is relatively balanced but the total is small. Micro-averaging aggregates events from all classes, avoiding macro-averaging being dominated by noise from rare classes, and also aligns with the product perspective of "overall false alarm rate"; (3) Precision/recall are reported side-by-side because the decision threshold of template matching systems directly controls the trade-off between the two. Looking only at the F-score would mask the movement of the trade-off point (as seen later, the collapse of recall is the main failure mode for Fréchet means).

**Runtime as a first-class citizen metric** is a distinctive feature of this paper's experimental design: Table I directly lists runtime in the main table, defined as "the time to apply the DTW method after extracting all features," measured on an Intel i7-8700 CPU (3.20 GHz). Excluding feature extraction is to isolate the comparison object—the paper compares DTW-level strategy differences, not feature calculation differences. Fig. 3 further provides curves of runtime varying with the number of shots per class (2 to 10).

Post-processing and tuning details: Both systems use sub-sequence DTW, cosine similarity, and the non-standard step set {(1,1),(1,2),(2,1)}; detection results are post-processed—overlapping events keep only the one with the highest DTW similarity score, and detections shorter than 50% of the corresponding reference template length are deleted; all hyperparameters (including the decision threshold of the KWS system) are optimized on the validation set. Post-processing exists because sub-sequence DTW produces multiple overlapping candidate paths for the same keyword occurrence, and the non-standard step set allows frame skipping, requiring regularized output.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Comparisons were conducted on a grid of two DTW features × four DTW strategies (Table I, all using five shots):

**Two Features**. One is **HFCC** (human factor cepstral coefficients): References [7][8] show it outperforms other hand-crafted features like MFCC on query-by-example KWS, calculated based on spectrograms with a window of 25 ms and a step of 10 ms. The biggest advantage of HFCC is that it **requires no training at all**, i.e., take-and-use, which the paper calls an "ad-hoc KWS method"—this is also the target scenario for multi-sample DTW as a training-free matcher. The other is **discriminative embeddings**: An embedding model based on the TACos loss proposed in Reference [11], which inputs spectrograms of 250 ms short windows (50% overlap) into a convolutional network to output an embedding sequence with the same time dimension as the input spectrogram; the network is trained to simultaneously predict the keyword to which the segment belongs and the position of the segment in the keyword recording (by minimizing angular interval loss); recording-level embeddings are obtained by time-overlapped averaging of segment embeddings; during training, **time-reversed segments** were also used as additional hard negative samples to reduce false alarms. The specific structure, parameter count, and training hyperparameters of the embedding network are not reported in the paper (pointing to Reference [11]).

**Finding 1: Embeddings outperform HFCC across the board.** Embeddings win in all experimental combinations (Table I). For example, under the per-sample strategy, the test F-score is 69.48% vs. 58.82%, a difference of 10.66 percentage points. This is consistent with the conclusion of Reference [11]—discriminative training makes intra-class similarity high and inter-class similarity low, providing a better basis for DTW matching.

**Finding 2: Standard Fréchet Mean is the main performance bottleneck, especially catastrophic under HFCC.** Under HFCC features, the standard mean drags the test F-score down from 58.82% to 42.44% (a drop of 16.38 percentage points), where recall collapses from 55.25% to 36.46%—nearly four-fifths of real events could not be matched by the mean template. Under embedding features, the drop is much smaller (69.48% to 59.26%, a drop of 10.22 percentage points). The paper's explanation hits the nail on the head: HFCC is not a discriminatively trained feature, so intra-class similarity is naturally low, and variability caused by different speaker characteristics and contexts is large; the mean "indeed cannot fully capture" these variations; embeddings have already compressed intra-class differences, so the destructive power of the mean is correspondingly weakened. This cross-comparison is one of the most convincing evidence chains in the paper: **the precision loss of the mean route is inversely proportional to the discriminativeness of the features; the "rawer" the feature, the more dangerous the mean.**

**Finding 3: Altered Fréchet Mean significantly recovers performance, validating Technical Innovation 2.** Under HFCC, the test F-score rebounds from 42.44% to 49.54% (+7.10 percentage points), and under embeddings, it rebounds from 59.26% to 67.73% (+8.47 percentage points), with runtime unchanged (6.2 s / 4.2 s, exactly the same as the standard mean). The alternating structure of "one template encoding two replaceable templates" buys back most of the variability wiped out by the mean at zero overhead.

**Finding 4: multi-sample DTW catches up to or even surpasses per-sample querying.** Under embedding features, the validation F-score is 71.07% vs. 69.18% (1.89 percentage points higher), and the test F-score is 69.43% vs. 69.48% (0.05 percentage points lower, essentially持平); under HFCC features, validation is 57.93% vs. 60.18% (2.25 percentage points lower), and test is 58.08% vs. 58.82% (0.74 percentage points lower). Notably, under embedding validation, both precision and recall **increased simultaneously** (82.48% vs. 80.29%, 62.43% vs. 60.77%), indicating that the performance gain is not an illusion created by threshold shifting, but that the recombined templates produced by path switching really matched pronunciations that per-sample matching could not. This is empirical evidence of the lower bound property plus combinatorial gain.

**Finding 5: Runtime falls within the promised interval.** Under embedding features: Fréchet mean 4.2 seconds (theoretical optimum), multi-sample parallel version 6.5 seconds (1.5 times slower), multi-sample serial version 10.6 seconds, per-sample 18.9 seconds. Under HFCC features: 6.2 seconds, 12.4 seconds, 20.9 seconds, 29.5 seconds. Parallelizing the tensor-to-matrix step alone reduced HFCC from 20.9 seconds to 12.4 seconds and embeddings from 10.6 seconds to 6.5 seconds, with F-score/precision/recall completely unchanged (pure implementation optimization). Relative to per-sample querying, the parallel version accelerated by approximately 2.4 times and 2.9 times, respectively.

**Shot Number Extension Analysis in Fig. 3**: The runtime of per-sample querying **grows linearly** with the number of shots per class (2 to 10), leading the paper to judge this route as infeasible under large shot numbers; the slope of the multi-sample DTW curve is much smaller, because "performing DTW on relatively long test samples—this is the most computationally expensive step—needs to be executed only once per class"; the light-colored curve of the parallel version further approaches the almost horizontal line of the Fréchet mean, and the paper's summary is "obtaining a runtime very close to that of querying with Fréchet mean, which can be assumed optimal." There is another easily overlooked detail in Fig. 3: the paper points out that "higher recall means more DTW paths need to be examined, which also increases runtime"—systems with high recall incur greater overhead in candidate path enumeration, which is a subtle coupling between runtime and performance.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

The paper does not have an independently named ablation table, but Table I and Fig. 3 jointly constitute a de facto ablation framework, with clean control of variables:

- **Aggregation Strategy Ablation** (Table I, feature fixed): Comparing per-sample/standard mean/altered mean/multi-sample four aggregations under the same feature, isolating the variable of "how variability enters matching." The conclusion is that the impact of the aggregation method is far greater than any factor other than feature selection: under HFCC, the gap in test F-score between the best and worst aggregation is 15.64 percentage points (58.08% vs. 42.44%), and under embeddings, it is 10.17 percentage points (69.43% vs. 59.26%).
- **Parallelization Ablation** (Table I): Serial and parallel versions only change the implementation of tensor-to-matrix, with all performance metrics identical digit-by-digit, and runtime decreased by 8.5 seconds and 4.1 seconds under the two features, respectively. This validates the assertion in the complexity analysis—that this step is "extremely easy to parallelize."
- **Feature × Aggregation Interaction Effect Ablation** (Table I): The damage of the mean strategy to HFCC (−16.38 percentage points) is significantly greater than to embeddings (−10.22 percentage points), quantitatively confirming the mechanism inference that "discriminative features are naturally resistant to mean compression." This is the most informative cross-item in the ablation: it tells practitioners that if your features are training-free hand-crafted features, absolutely do not use mean compression for templates; if you have discriminative embeddings, the cost of the mean is barely acceptable.
- **Shot Number Ablation** (Fig. 3): Only the runtime dimension is ablated; the curve of performance varying with shot number is **not reported by the paper** (Table I is fixed at 5-shot). This is an obvious experimental gap—multi-sample DTW should theoretically be monotonically non-decreasing with K under the lower bound property, but the gain rate, and whether combinatorial explosion brings increased false alarms at larger K, are not supported by data.
- **Other Uncovered Ablations**: Direct comparison with clustered multi-mean schemes [23][24] is not reported by the paper (although introduced as a key related work in the introduction); the impact of different step sets, different local distances, and different reference template lengths is not reported; variance or significance tests over multiple runs are not reported.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed multi-sample DTW**: By constructing "K cost matrices → cost tensor → element-wise minimum → single cost matrix," it allows DTW paths to switch between samples, shifting the K-factor of multi-template matching from serial DP to parallelizable computation, while maintaining (lower-bound guaranteed no worse than) per-sample querying performance, it compresses runtime to a level close to single-mean querying. This is a third route outside of "lossy compression of the mean" and "linear overhead of per-sample."
2. **Proposed a two-step procedure for altered Fréchet mean**: The second DBA combined with sub-sequence DTW and non-standard step sets allows a single template to encode two replaceable templates in an alternating structure, significantly outperforming the standard Fréchet mean at zero runtime increment (+7.10 for HFCC test F, +8.47 for embeddings in Table I), and it can be used independently of multi-sample DTW.
3. **Systematic empirical validation**: On the few-shot open-set KWS benchmark KWS-DailyTalk, using training-free HFCC and discriminative embeddings as two features, covering a complete comparison of performance and runtime (Table I, Fig. 3), it proves the performance-efficiency trade-off positioning of the method, and leaves a transferable engineering conclusion: "the damage of aggregation strategy is inversely proportional to feature discriminativeness."
4. **Precise complexity characterization**: Clearly gives the computational complexity of each step, distinguishes the difference between "nominal complexity unchanged but constant greatly reduced" and "complexity truly dropped," and honestly marks Fréchet mean as the theoretical lower bound for runtime.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Nominal complexity has not truly changed**: The total complexity is still O(N·M·C·K), with the K factor remaining in the computation and storage of the cost tensor. The improvement in runtime relies on the engineering premise that "this part is parallelizable"; in environments where parallelization is impossible (e.g., single-threaded low-end MCUs), the advantage of the serial version of multi-sample DTW over per-sample will shrink significantly (in Table I, the HFCC serial version is 20.9s vs. 29.5s, only about 29% faster). Meanwhile, the memory occupancy of K cost matrices grows linearly with K, and specific memory numbers are not reported by the paper.
- **False alarm risk of chimera paths is not theoretically constrained**: Element-wise minimum allows paths to switch arbitrarily between different samples (pronunciations of different speakers, different contexts). Theoretically, there exists a "chimera template" such as "beginning of Sample A spliced with end of Sample B," which does not exist in real speech, and may cause false alarms in extreme cases. In the experiment, precision was not damaged (embedding validation precision actually increased from 80.29% to 82.48%), but this is only an empirical result on a single dataset, without stress testing in adversarial scenarios.
- **Non-differentiable, cannot enter the training loop**: The hard minimum and exact DP maintain the precision of matching but sacrifice differentiability—multi-sample DTW cannot be used as a loss function to train feature extraction networks like soft-DTW. Features and matching strategies are therefore split into two stages, unable to be jointly optimized end-to-end.
- **Dependence on heuristic reference templates**: The Fréchet mean in Step 1 is itself a heuristic approximation of an NP-complete problem. The choice of fixing the template length to the "average length of query samples" may be suboptimal for classes with large length variance; although the paper points out that "the Fréchet mean algorithm can theoretically be chosen arbitrarily," it does not systematically compare the sensitivity of different initializations.
- **Threshold dependence at deployment**: The decision threshold is optimized on the validation set, belonging to per-class hyperparameters; the risk of threshold mismatch under domain shift (new speakers, new environments) is the same as for all template matching systems, and the paper does not discuss the cost of recalibration.

### Shortcomings of Experimental Design

- **Single dataset, single language, single keyword domain**: All conclusions come from KWS-DailyTalk (English conversational speech, 15 financial/travel words), without cross-dataset generalization validation. The authors themselves list "evaluating on other KWS datasets such as Speech Commands" as future work in the conclusion. The 10-minute-level test set and the appearance frequency of approximately 12 times per class are also on the small side. A difference of 0.05 percentage points in test F-score (69.43% vs. 69.48%) is not statistically significant at this scale, and the paper does not report any significance tests.
- **Missing performance-shot number curve**: Fig. 3 only ablates the伸缩 of runtime with shot number; the paper does not report the curve of performance gain with shot number. For few-shot methods, the performance climbing shape from K=1 to K=10 is one of the core selling points. This gap leaves half the evidence missing for the argument of "variability capture."
- **Lack of empirical confrontation with clustered multi-means**: The introduction takes the clustered mean route of References [23][24] as the main comparison object (both are attempts to "reduce the number of query samples"), but this baseline is not implemented in the experiments. It is impossible to judge whether the combination of clustered mean + multi-sample DTW would be stronger.
- **Comparison scope limited to the DTW family**: There is no comparison with modern non-DTW schemes for few-shot KWS (metric learning classifiers, prototype network-like methods). "Internally optimal within the template matching route" does not equal "optimal for the task." The strong performer of embedding features (test F-score 69.48%) itself comes from the discriminative model of Reference [11], and the gain of multi-sample DTW is superimposed on top of it.
- **Lack of transparency in parallel implementation details**: The number of threads used, the parallel framework, and whether SIMD is utilized in the parallel version are not reported by the paper, reducing the reproducibility of the numbers 12.4s/6.5s; performance on GPU is not reported (only mentioned as possible with stronger hardware in future work).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Cross-dataset and cross-task validation** (indicated by the authors themselves): Re-validate on other KWS datasets such as Speech Commands, and generalize to other applications of DTW (bioacoustic event detection, multi-pitch estimation, etc.)—the method itself does not depend on speech characteristics and is applicable to any "multi-template against long sequence" scenario.
- **Soft-minimization to obtain differentiability**: Replace the element-wise minimum with a temperature-based soft-min (drawing on soft-DTW), making the tensor aggregation a differentiable operator. In principle, this allows end-to-end training of the embedding network to "adapt" to the multi-sample aggregation target, and even learn the aggregation weights for each sample (attention-style weighted minimum).
- **Stacking with orthogonal acceleration techniques**: Sakoe-Chiba/Itakura band constraints, path pruning, and GPU diagonal parallelization techniques can be directly stacked with multi-sample DTW to further compress the constant of that one DP per class.
- **Hierarchical structure for large-K scenarios**: When the number of samples per class reaches dozens or hundreds, clustering can be performed first, multi-sample DTW is used within clusters, and the optimum is taken between clusters, forming a hierarchical envelope to alleviate the linear memory and computational pressure of the cost tensor.
- **Control mechanism for chimera false alarms**: Introduce switching costs (applying penalties when the path switches samples) or limit the number of switches per path, constraining the generation of unnatural templates while preserving recombination gains, providing theoretical guarantees for precision.
- **More thorough variability modeling**: The alternating structure of the altered Fréchet mean currently encodes only two replaceable templates. Generalizing to "one template encoding N replaceable templates" (finer step sets or higher-dimensional template structures) is a natural direction; corresponding theoretical analysis (trade-off between effective capacity and false alarm rate) is completely untouched by the paper and is a space for subsequent research.

Overall evaluation: This is a work with precise problem definition, simple method construction, and experimental design strictly aligned with its claims. Its value does not lie in brushing up a benchmark number, but in liberating "multi-template matching" from the binary choice of "mean or per-sample," using the clean construction of a lower envelope at the cost level to capture both ends simultaneously; the quantitative conclusion that "the damage of aggregation strategy is inversely proportional to feature discriminativeness" (Table I) has guiding significance for engineering practice that may even exceed KWS itself.
