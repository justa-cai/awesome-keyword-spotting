# Open vocabulary keyword spotting through transfer learning from speech synthesis

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Kesavaraj V, Anil Kumar Vuppala (IIIT Hyderabad, LTRC Speech Processing Lab)
- **Date**: April 2024 (arXiv:2404.03914v2, April 18, 2024)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03914
- **Keywords**: open-vocabulary keyword spotting, transfer learning, text-to-speech, Tacotron 2, intermediate representation, cross-attention, audio-text matching

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) refers to the detection of specific keywords within a continuous audio stream, serving as an entry capability for voice interaction on edge devices. With the popularization of personalized voice assistants, the demand for user-defined wake-up words continues to grow, which is known as custom keyword detection or open-vocabulary keyword spotting. It differs fundamentally from closed-set detection: closed-set detection only recognizes a fixed vocabulary predetermined during training, and the model can essentially "memorize the vocabulary" at the output layer; open-vocabulary detection handles arbitrary keywords that the model has never seen during training, raising the difficulty level significantly—the model cannot rely on memorizing scores for a limited number of classes but must truly judge "whether this text and this audio refer to the same thing."

From a user product perspective, there are two entry points for registering a new keyword: recording an audio clip (audio registration) or inputting text (text registration). Text registration is more user-friendly (typing is more convenient than recording) and is naturally immune to speaker differences and environmental noise on the registration side, making the text registration route the mainstream approach in recent years. However, text registration introduces a core pain point, which the paper repeatedly emphasizes as **modality heterogeneity (audio-text mismatch)**: the text encoder starts from literal character sequences, and the learned representations contain no concept of "sound"; the audio encoder starts from acoustic features, and the learned representations are full of variables such as speaker timbre, channel, and noise that do not exist on the text side. Forcing these two distributions to align in a shared latent space is equivalent to building a bridge between two originally non-parallel tracks—no matter how well the bridge is built, the misalignment between the two tracks themselves remains.

The most direct symptom of this misalignment appears in the discrimination of **near-homophones**. The paper gives the example of "madame" and "modem": the two words differ by only a few vowels, with highly similar pronunciations, but differ by only one or two letters at the character level. Pure literal embeddings and pure acoustic embeddings both struggle to draw clear discriminative boundaries between such word pairs, because neither representation possesses knowledge of "how this word is actually pronounced and how it differs from that word." In terms of data, the strong baseline CMCD has an error rate as high as 32.9% on the LibriPhrase Hard (LPH) dataset, which consists of near-homophone negative samples (Table I), indicating that near-homophone discrimination is the biggest weakness of the end-to-end audio-text matching route.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper divides existing methods into three routes in the introduction and points out their shortcomings one by one. The numbers in Table I make this clear:

- **Query-by-Example (QbyE) route**: During registration, an audio clip of the target word is recorded, and during testing, the input query is matched for similarity with the registered audio. Representative works include CTC-based DONUT [7] and multi-head attention-based methods [6]. The shortcoming is that performance relies heavily on the similarity between the registered speech and the test speech: speaker timbre differences between users and environmental background noise will cause matching drift. Table I exposes this weakness thoroughly—the attention-based QbyE method [6] achieves an EER of 14.75% and AUC of 92.09% on Google Commands V1 (G), which is the best in the field, because G's keywords are high-frequency common words (the paper cites "on" and "off" as examples) and appear in the training set, benefiting the similarity scoring mechanism from "familiarity"; however, once switched to the unfamiliar Qualcomm dataset (Q), it immediately collapses to an EER of 49.13% and AUC of 50.13%, approaching random levels. This is precisely the failure mode least acceptable in open-vocabulary tasks.
- **ASR-based text registration route** [9]: Detects phoneme patterns in the input audio stream and compares them with the registered keyword representation. The shortcoming, explicitly pointed out by the paper, is the cascading dependency: the effectiveness of this method relies heavily on the accuracy of the acoustic model itself; if ASR fails, KWS fails, and errors amplify along the link.
- **End-to-end shared latent space route** (CMCD [8], PhonMatchNet [10], Matching Latent Encoding [11]): Projects speech and text representations into a shared latent space, using monotonic matching loss plus keyword classification loss, phoneme-level detection loss, and dynamic sequence partitioning for alignment. These methods are currently the strongest route (CMCD is the strongest baseline on Q and LPE in Table I), but their common shortcoming is the modality heterogeneity mentioned above: text representations lack acoustic knowledge and struggle to distinguish pairs of words with similar pronunciations, with an EER of 32.9% on LPH (Table I) being the cost.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

The chain of questions the paper aims to answer has three layers. The first layer is **how to make text representations carry acoustic knowledge**: not by relying on the audio and text encoders to compromise with each other under the loss function during training, but by directly bringing in pre-existing "text-to-sound" mapping knowledge from elsewhere, so that the text side already "knows" how to pronounce the word before training begins. The second layer is **which layer to transfer**: The TTS model has seven candidate intermediate representations internally (character embedding, convolution block, Bi-LSTM, attention, Prenet, Postnet, target Mel-spectrogram), each carrying different amounts and types of acoustic knowledge. Which layer is most useful for the KWS task requires systematic ablation, which is also one of the core contributions claimed by the paper. The third layer is **stability after transfer**: The defining scenario for open-vocabulary tasks is unfamiliar words, so robustness to words unseen during training (OOV) and keywords of different lengths (1 to 4 words) must be verified; otherwise, the benefits of acoustic priors might just be fitting the training vocabulary.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall architecture (Fig. 1) consists of four sub-modules: a text encoder, an audio encoder, a pattern extractor, and a pattern discriminator. The task is organized as binary classification: given a pair of {text, audio}, the model outputs whether "the two are the same keyword."

**Text Encoder**: A pre-trained Tacotron 2 [13] model receives the character sequence of the keyword, extracts an intermediate representation from a specific layer (the paper finally selects E3, i.e., the output of the encoder Bi-LSTM block, Table II), feeds it into a 64-dimensional Bi-GRU layer, and then into a fully connected layer with 128 units, outputting the text embedding $TE \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}$, where $d$ is the embedding dimension and $m$ is the text length (number of characters). The design motivation is straightforward: Tacotron 2 is a sequence-to-sequence synthesizer from characters to speech, and its encoder must encode "how this text is pronounced" into the intermediate representation to predict audio—this is exactly the acoustic prior lacking on the text side of KWS. The paper does not explain why Tacotron 2 was chosen over other TTS models; a reasonable inference is that NVIDIA released pre-trained complete weights on LJSpeech [19], and its encoder structure (character-level, clear hierarchy) facilitates the extraction of intermediate representations layer by layer.

**Audio Encoder**: The input is 80-dimensional Mel-filterbank coefficients, extracted every 10ms with a window length of 25ms. To process this feature, the encoder first uses two layers of 2D convolutions (kernel size 3 for both), with 32 filters in the first layer and 64 filters in the second layer; Batch Normalization is applied after each convolution to stabilize training; the first layer convolution uses a stride of 2, effectively reducing the number of frames to be processed subsequently by skipping adjacent frames, which the paper explicitly states is for computational efficiency considerations. After convolution, two Bi-GRU layers of 64 dimensions each are connected, and the output of the last Bi-GRU layer enters a fully connected layer to generate a 128-dimensional audio embedding, denoted as $AE \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $n$ is the number of audio frames. A detail easily overlooked is that both fully connected layers output the same dimension $d=128$. This is not a coincidence—the Query and Key of cross-attention must have the same dimension to perform inner product; unifying the embedding dimension on both sides is a prerequisite for the subsequent alignment module to mesh directly, belonging to the design of "divide and conquer but aligned interfaces."

**Pattern Extractor**: Based on cross-attention mechanism [14] (i.e., scaled dot-product attention of Transformer), it captures temporal correlations between audio embeddings and text embeddings. The role assignment is the key design: **audio embeddings serve as both Key and Value, while text embeddings serve as Query**, outputting a context vector carrying audio-text consistency information. **Why this assignment and not the reverse**: Audio is a long and variable-length sequence ($n$ frames), while text is a short sequence ($m$ characters); using text to query audio is equivalent to letting each character position "find its own sound" in the entire audio segment, naturally forming a soft alignment from text to audio, with the output length following the text side and decoupled from the audio length. This directly avoids the trouble of [11] (Matching Latent Encoding) needing "dynamic sequence partitioning" to align the lengths of audio and text sequences, and also avoids the need for explicit monotonic matching loss like [8] (CMCD) to constrain the alignment direction—the alignment structure is implicitly determined by the choice of Query/Value.

**Pattern Discriminator**: A single 128-dimensional Bi-GRU layer reads the context vector sequence, takes the output of the **last frame** into a fully connected layer with sigmoid activation, and gives the probability of whether the audio and text share the same keyword. Taking the last frame instead of pooling the entire sequence implies the assumption that the bidirectional GRU will accumulate and converge the decision evidence into the last step after reading the entire context vector sequence, consistent with the information convergence method of the bidirectional structure.

Another engineering aspect worth emphasizing: the entire framework **never actually synthesizes speech**. The paper explicitly states that this strategy "does not explicitly generate audio from text, but only utilizes the knowledge transfer of TTS intermediate representations." This means that registering a new keyword only requires passing its character sequence through the front end of TTS to obtain the E3 representation; the text embedding can be calculated once and cached; during inference, only the audio encoder and matching head are running, and the TTS front end is not on the critical path. However, the paper does not report deployment metrics such as parameter count, inference latency, or real-time rate; this efficiency advantage can only remain at the level of architectural deduction (see Limitations section).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Cross-Attention**: The pattern extractor is standard scaled dot-product attention, $Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / \sqrt{d_k}) \cdot V$, where $Q = TE \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}$, $K = V = AE \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. For the $i$-th character position on the text side, the attention weight $\alpha_{ij} = softmax_j(TE_i \cdot AE_j / \sqrt{d_k})$ measures the correlation strength between that character and the $j$-th frame of audio, and the context vector $c_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij} \cdot AE_j$ can be understood as "the sum of acoustic evidence collected by this character in the entire audio segment." The context vectors $\{c_i\}$ of the entire sequence are then handed over to the discriminator for sequence-level decision.

**Training Objective**: Binary cross-entropy loss. Each training sample consists of three entities: audio, text, and a binary target (1 for positive pair, 0 for negative pair). The optimizer uses Adam (default parameters), fixed learning rate 1e-4, batch size 128; weights in the audio and text encoders are initialized with Xavier, activation function uses Leaky ReLU, and 0.2 dropout is added after each layer of each encoder to prevent overfitting; the optimal model is selected based on validation set performance; training uses 4 NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPUs. The paper does not report the number of training epochs or training duration.

**Why use pairwise binary classification instead of fixed-class softmax**: This is the structural source of open-vocabulary capability. The output layer of fixed-class softmax is bound to the training vocabulary, and new words cannot be added without retraining; pairwise binary classification rewrites "recognition" as "verification," so any new keyword can participate in matching as long as it can be converted into a text embedding, and the vocabulary boundary disappears from the model structure and shifts to the data side. The cost is that such verification frameworks are usually evaluated on pre-segmented phrase pairs, and sliding window detection and localization on continuous audio streams are not discussed by the paper (no streaming evaluation is reported).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: TTS Intermediate Representations as Acoustically Aware Text Representations

This is the core idea of the entire paper and the main contribution claimed by the paper. Traditionally, in audio-text matching, the text encoder starts from random initialization, having seen only a limited number of words in the KWS training set, with blank knowledge of the character-to-pronunciation mapping when generalizing to new words. In contrast, the TTS model is pre-trained on LJSpeech, and its entire job is "guessing sound from text": Tacotron 2's encoder must map character sequences into representations sufficient to predict Mel-spectrograms, and this representation naturally encodes knowledge such as grapheme-to-phoneme conversion rules, spelling context, and syllable structure—"how this passage is read." Directly bringing these intermediate representations as input features for the text encoder is equivalent to filling the modality gap of the discrimination task with the free by-product of the generation task.

The transfer is very light: no audio synthesis, no new pre-training objectives introduced, only the input feature source of the text encoder is changed. The paper systematically lists seven candidate intermediate representations in Table II (Ta is the number of input characters of Tacotron 2, Tb is the number of output frames): E1 is the output of the character embedding block (Ta×512); E2 is the output of the convolution block (Ta×512); E3 is the output of the Bi-LSTM block (Ta×512); E4 is the output of the attention block (Tb×512); E5 is the output of the Prenet block (Tb×512); E6 is the output of the Postnet block (Tb×512); E7 is the target Mel-spectrogram (Tb×80). Note that the dimensional differences behind them reflect differences in sequence alignment methods: E1-E3 are aligned by character count, representing linguistic units; E4-E7 are aligned by synthesis frame count, already representing acoustic frames.

This idea has direct predecessors: Voice Transformer Network [12] also transfers knowledge from pre-trained TTS, but for speech conversion tasks. This paper systematically introduces "using TTS intermediate representations as text representations" into open-vocabulary KWS and performs layer-by-layer ablation, which is novel in the KWS scenario.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Encoder-side Representations Significantly Outperform Decoder-side, E3 is Optimal

The layer-by-layer ablation in Table III (see Experimental Results section) gives a clear conclusion: E3 (output of Tacotron 2 encoder Bi-LSTM block) is consistently optimal across all four datasets, E2 and E4 on the encoder side form competitive alternatives, E5, E6, and E7 on the decoder side are significantly worse, and E6 (Postnet output) is the worst in the entire field.

**Why encoder-side wins and decoder-side loses**: The representations on the decoder side of Tacotron 2 (attention context, Prenet, Postnet, Mel-spectrogram) are frame-aligned to the synthesis target, deeply entangled with specific acoustic implementation details such as the timbre and prosody of the single speaker of LJSpeech—this information is noise for matching "audio from any speaker to text" and instead dilutes the linguistic content. In contrast, encoder-side representations are speaker-independent, encoding only the abstract mapping of "how the text is read," which is exactly what the text side needs to supplement. The internal progression along E1→E2→E3 also has an explanation: E1 is a context-free single-character embedding, unable to disambiguate homographic characters in different spelling contexts (e.g., the hard and soft readings of the English letter 'c'); E2's convolution block brings local window context, capable of capturing spelling patterns of double-letter combinations like 'sh' and 'ch'; E3's bidirectional LSTM brings full-sequence context, resolving all long-range grapheme-phoneme dependencies, and is the "pronunciation-ready" representation consumed directly by the decoder attention—the content of acoustic knowledge peaks on the encoder side. The paper's own summary is: capturing information at the encoder stage before Mel-spectrogram generation is crucial for accurate keyword detection.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Audio-Text Consistency Discrimination via Text-Query Cross-Attention

The third innovation is the matching structure itself: using cross-attention for soft alignment (replacing CMCD's monotonic matching loss, [11]'s dynamic sequence partitioning), and using the last frame of Bi-GRU plus sigmoid for sequence-level decision. The significance of this design lies in turning "alignment" from an explicit constraint into an intrinsic property of the attention structure, making the framework concise and robust to variable lengths—this echoes the cross-word-length performance consistency shown in Table IV.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

The essential differences with the three existing routes are as follows. **For QbyE (audio registration)**: The registration signal is changed from audio to text, naturally immune to speaker and noise mismatch on the registration side, at the cost of the text side needing to supplement acoustic knowledge, which this paper does via TTS transfer. **For ASR text registration**: No cascaded ASR is performed; audio-text consistency is learned end-to-end directly, with no error propagation chain. **For end-to-end shared space methods like CMCD**: The "text entry" of the shared space is changed from a randomly initialized character encoder to an acoustically aware TTS encoder representation; only one entry is changed at the component level, but Table I shows that the EER on LPH drops from 32.9% to 24.68%—in the most difficult near-homophone scenario, the injection of acoustic priors brings a qualitative leap in benefits. The essence of the difference is the source of knowledge: CMCDs expect KWS training data to teach the text encoder "how to pronounce" itself, while this paper outsources this knowledge to a TTS fully trained on large-scale single-speaker corpora.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

- **Libriphrase** [8]: Main training and evaluation data, consisting of short phrases of 1 to 4 words generated from LibriSpeech [15]. The training set is generated using the train-clean-100 and train-clean-360 subsets, and the evaluation set is generated using the train-others-500 subset. The number of episodes for each word length in the evaluation set are 4391 (1 word), 2605 (2 words), 467 (3 words), 56 (4 words); each episode contains 3 positive pairs and 3 negative pairs; negative samples are divided into easy and hard categories according to Levenshtein distance [16], constituting LibriPhrase-Easy (LPE) and LibriPhrase-Hard (LPH) respectively. Using text edit distance to create "hard negative samples" (e.g., madame and modem differ by only one or two letters literally) is an approximate proxy for pronunciation distance—literally close words are usually pronounced similarly, but not strictly equivalent (homophones with different spellings have large literal distances but are completely homophonic), which is an implicit bias in the evaluation design.
- **Google Speech Commands V1 (G)** [17]: 1881 speakers, 30 small keywords, used for cross-database generalization testing without any fine-tuning.
- **Qualcomm Keyword Speech (Q)** [18]: 50 speakers, 4 keywords, 4270 sentences in total, also used for generalization testing.
- **Tacotron 2**: NVIDIA pre-trained model [19], trained on LJSpeech [20]. The paper does not report the duration scale of LJSpeech, nor does it explicitly state whether the TTS weights are frozen or fine-tuned during KWS training—inferred from the expression "pre-trained model + intermediate representation extraction" as a frozen feature extractor usage.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper uses three metrics. **EER (Equal Error Rate)**: The error rate when the false acceptance rate and false rejection rate are equal, independent of the decision threshold; the reason for selection is that a single threshold-independent number is convenient for fair comparison across methods in verification-based binary classification tasks. **AUC (Area Under ROC Curve)**: Overall discriminative power traversing all thresholds; the reason for selection is that the working threshold for open-vocabulary KWS actual deployment needs to be tuned according to the product, and AUC measures the foundation of "working well regardless of threshold tuning." **F1 Score**: Harmonic mean of precision and recall; the reason for selection is to provide practical performance at a single-threshold working point, supplementing this perspective in Table III and Table V. The three metrics cover both threshold-independent (EER, AUC) and threshold-dependent (F1) ends, and the metric selection for verification tasks is complete.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Table I gives the EER and AUC of five methods on four datasets (format: G/Q/LPE/LPH): CTC [7] EER is 31.65/18.23/14.67/35.22, AUC is 66.36/89.69/92.29/69.58; Attention [6] EER is 14.75/49.13/28.74/41.95, AUC is 92.09/50.13/78.74/62.65; Triplet [3] EER is 35.6/38.72/32.75/44.36, AUC is 71.48/66.44/63.53/54.88; CMCD [8] EER is 27.25/12.15/8.42/32.9, AUC is 81.06/94.51/96.7/73.58; The proposed method's EER is 22.3/10.82/5.61/24.68, AUC is 85.16/95.65/98.49/86.14.

Three key readings:

First, **the proposed method is first overall on Q, LPE, and LPH, and second on G** (EER 22.3, losing to the attention QbyE's 14.75). The paper explains the disadvantage on G: G's keywords are high-frequency common words and appear in the training data, and QbyE's similarity scoring mechanism benefits from the "familiarity" dividend; once the keywords are unfamiliar (Q and LibriPhrase), QbyE collapses while the proposed method remains stable. Considering that the defining scenario for open-vocabulary tasks is precisely "unseen words," the concession on G combined with the lead on Q/LPH actually supports the method's positioning.

Second, **the gap on LPH is the most convincing**. CMCD's EER 32.9% vs. the proposed method's 24.68%, an absolute improvement of 8.22 percentage points; AUC from 73.58% to 86.14%, an absolute improvement of 12.56 percentage points. It is worth pointing out specifically: the abstract and Section 4 text swap the metric attribution of these two numbers (the text states "AUC improved by 8.22%, EER improved by 12.56%"); according to the original numbers in Table I, 8.22 corresponds to the EER difference and 12.56 corresponds to the AUC difference; this note takes the table arithmetic as the standard. Being able to pull apart a gap of about ten percentage points on near-homophone pairs (madame vs. modem) directly verifies the core hypothesis—the grapheme-phoneme knowledge transferred from TTS allows the text embedding to separate near-homophones in the pronunciation space, making the discriminative boundary clearer.

Third, **cross-database generalization (G and Q tested directly without any fine-tuning)**. The paper claims an "AUC consistent improvement of about 3%, EER improvement of 2.62%" relative to CMCD. Verifying with Table I: AUC improved by 4.10 on G and 1.14 on Q, mean 2.62; EER decreased by 4.95 on G and 1.33 on Q, mean 3.14—the text swapped the two metric names again (the mean of 2.62 is AUC, the one of about 3 is EER). The generalization benefits themselves are real and consistent in direction, but when citing the paper, one must return to the table to verify independently.

Another baseline phenomenon worth recording: Triplet [3] is comprehensively the weakest on Q, LPE, and LPH (the paper also points this out), indicating that pure metric learning has insufficient representation quality for such fine-grained near-homophone discrimination; the CTC scheme is very poor on G (EER 31.65, AUC 66.36) but acceptable on Q (18.23, 89.69), and the performance variance within the QbyE route is extremely large, further confirming that registered audio quality is the Achilles' heel of this route.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

**(1) Layer-by-layer ablation of Tacotron 2 intermediate representations (Table III, EER/AUC/F1 three metrics across four databases)**. Core numbers (EER according to G/Q/LPE/LPH): E1 is 31.44/19.32/9.26/32.13; E2 is 26.43/14.52/7.11/28.47; E3 is 22.3/10.82/5.61/24.68; E4 is 24.6/15.27/8.43/31.2; E5 is 27.51/19.98/12.02/34.35; E6 is 34.61/33.14/20.9/39.19; E7 is 25.42/23.79/11.68/34.7. AUC and F1 show completely consistent ranking, E3's AUC is 85.16/95.65/98.49/86.14, F1 is 78.56/87.74/94.5/72.2, exactly the same as the Proposed row in Table I—confirming that the final system is the E3 configuration. The chain of discoveries is very clear: first, E3 is the best in the entire field, indicating that it captures both acoustic and linguistic information of the registered keywords (the paper's original words); second, E2 and E4 are competitive alternatives, with stable AUC performance (the paper calls them better alternatives to E3); third, E6 (Postnet output) is the worst in the entire field, even worse than the most naive E1 pure character embedding (G database EER 34.61 vs. 31.44), indicating that the deep decoder-side representation has almost only speaker-related acoustic details left and has lost linguistic abstraction; fourth, encoder-side (E2, E3) is significantly better than decoder-side (E5, E6, E7), from which the paper concludes that "capturing information at the encoder stage before Mel-spectrogram generation is crucial." A minor flaw: E7 is labeled as "target Mel-spectrogram," but during inference, the Mel-spectrogram can only come from model prediction (previous frame prediction or final predicted spectrum); the paper does not clarify how E7 is valued during inference, which needs to be defined independently during reproduction.

**(2) Word length robustness (Table IV)**. EER for word lengths 1 to 4 are 5.41/5.9/7.59/8.5, AUC are 98.07/97.83/97.04/97.12, F1 are 94.46/94.24/92.28/90.85. Short words (1 or 2 words) have better performance, lower EER, and higher F1; EER increases with word length, which the paper explains as the higher difficulty of recognizing longer keywords (a reasonable mechanistic explanation is: the longer the keyword, the more alignment ambiguities and the larger the space for false positives from partial matching). However, the decline is gentle—the EER difference between 1 word and 4 words is only about 3 percentage points—which the paper describes as "consistent performance across word lengths." A dose of cold water is needed: the evaluation set for 4-word length has only 56 episodes (Experimental Setup section), the sample size is too small, and the confidence of the 8.5% EER number is limited; the evidence for word length conclusions is weak at the long-word end.

**(3) OOV robustness (Table V)**. CMCD's EER/AUC/F1 is 23.48/84.08/76.2, the proposed method is 18.14/90.44/83.45. Absolute improvements: F1 improved by 7.25 percentage points, AUC improved by 6.36 percentage points, EER decreased by 5.34 percentage points according to table calculation (the paper text writes 5.53, which does not match table arithmetic, suspected to be a digit transposition typo). This is the most direct evidence for the "open-vocabulary" positioning in the entire paper: for keywords never seen during training, the text side can still correctly encode and match relying on TTS's grapheme-phoneme knowledge, performance does not collapse, and all three metrics are comprehensively better than the strongest baseline. The paper does not report the specific construction method of the OOV evaluation set (how the vocabulary is divided, what the scale is), which is a gap in reproduction.

**(4) Convergence speed (Fig. 2)**. The network using E3 representation has a faster training loss decline than CMCD. Mechanistically, this is completely explainable: the text encoder starts from "acoustically aware" initialization, and the cross-attention module does not need to learn the mapping from text to sound from scratch; the cross-modal alignment problem is partially pre-solved by TTS pre-training, allowing meaningful attention patterns to form early in training. This has practical value for engineering practice—smaller training overhead for the same target accuracy.

## Main Contributions

1. Proposed and verified an open-vocabulary KWS framework using "pre-trained TTS intermediate representations as acoustically aware text representations." The core value of this idea lies in cross-task knowledge arbitrage: using the by-product of training a generation task (speech synthesis) to fill the modality gap of a discrimination task (audio-text matching); this paradigm is not limited to KWS and can be transferred to other cross-modal matching scenarios.
2. Completed systematic ablation of seven intermediate layer representations of Tacotron 2 (E1 to E7, Table II and Table III) on KWS, giving the layer selection conclusion that "encoder-side is better than decoder-side, E3 (Bi-LSTM block output) is optimal and converges faster." This is a general engineering experience applicable whenever any sequence-to-sequence model is reused for feature transfer: the transfer point should be selected before the modality conversion occurs and after context modeling is completed.
3. On the most difficult LibriPhrase Hard (near-homophone negative samples), achieved an absolute EER reduction of 8.22 percentage points and an absolute AUC improvement of 12.56 percentage points relative to the strongest baseline CMCD (Table I), and consistent improvement in zero-shot generalization on two external databases G and Q, verifying the targeted nature of acoustic prior injection for the core difficulty of near-homophone discrimination.
4. Word length ablation (Table IV) and OOV experiments (Table V) prove the framework's robustness to keyword length changes and unseen words during training, and Fig. 2 proves that transfer brings faster training convergence, supporting the practicality of the method.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Single-layer transfer**: Only a single layer representation (E3) is extracted from Tacotron 2, and multi-layer information is not fused. The paper admits this in the conclusion and lists it as future work—each of the seven layers has its own focus (E2 leans towards local spelling, E3 towards full sequence, E4 towards alignment), and taking only one layer inevitably discards information.
- **Representation bound to specific TTS**: The quality of text representation is constrained by the specific model NVIDIA Tacotron 2, which is a character-level input, English, autoregressive TTS pre-trained on LJSpeech single-speaker style. Whether the grapheme-phoneme knowledge of character-level English TTS can cover languages with irregular spelling (French, Indian languages, etc.) has not been verified; although single-speaker pre-training is proven superior to decoder-side by ablation, whether its timbre bias will become a bottleneck under the need for greater speaker diversity is not discussed by the paper.
- **Only autoregressive TTS tested**: Whether intermediate representations of non-autoregressive TTS like FastSpeech, phoneme-input TTS, or multi-speaker TTS are better is not compared horizontally, and the architecture-independence of the conclusions is questionable.
- **Deployment metrics are completely blank**: The paper does not report parameter count, inference latency, real-time rate, or memory usage. KWS is an edge scenario task, and the acoustic-aware text encoder hangs behind the 512-dimensional encoder front end of Tacotron 2; there is no data support for whether this overhead can be borne by edge-side budgets. The efficiency advantage can currently only be inferred from two architectural properties: text embeddings can be calculated once and cached during the registration period; audio is not synthesized during inference, and the TTS front end is not on the critical path of audio processing.
- **Morphological limitations of pairwise classification**: The entire framework is a phrase-level {text, audio} binary classification, evaluated based on pre-segmented episodes; sliding window detection, keyword start/end localization, and confidence calibration on continuous audio streams are not discussed, and these are precisely the necessary steps from "verifier" to "detector" (no streaming or continuous audio experiments are reported by the paper).

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **No noise and channel robustness experiments**: The core weakness of the QbyE route is environmental noise and registration mismatch. This paper claims that text registration is naturally immune to this weakness, but no SNR scan or noise-adding experiments were designed to prove it, reducing persuasiveness.
- **No statistical significance testing**: All comparisons are single point estimates, with no variance reports for multiple runs; the evaluation set for 4-word length has only 56 episodes, and the statistical power of the long-word end conclusions in Table IV is very low.
- **Source of baseline numbers not specified**: Whether the baseline results in Table I are reproduced by themselves or cited from the original paper (the constructor of Libriphrase is the CMCD author team) is not reported by the paper, and the fairness of the comparison cannot be verified.
- **Inconsistency between text numbers and tables**: The LPH improvement numbers in the abstract and text swap the labels of AUC and EER (8.22 belongs to EER, 12.56 belongs to AUC, Table I); the two metric names in the G/Q generalization section are also swapped (the mean of 2.62 is AUC, the one of about 3 is EER); the EER improvement for OOV is written as 5.53 while the table calculation is 5.34. None of the three changes the direction of the conclusion, but they indicate that the paper's number proofreading is rough, and one must return to the table to verify when citing.
- **Proxy bias of near-homophone negative samples**: LPH divides negative sample difficulty using text Levenshtein distance; literal distance does not equal pronunciation distance (homophones with different spellings have large literal distances but are completely homophonic), and there is a systematic misalignment between the "hard negative sample" set and the true near-homophone confusion set, constraining the strength of conclusions on LPH.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Multi-layer fusion transfer** (direction proposed by the paper): Not relying on a single layer, exploring strategies to effectively utilize all intermediate layers of TTS, such as layer-weighted aggregation, hierarchical distillation, or learnable layer selection gating.
- **Model and multi-language ablation**: Repeat the layer-by-layer ablation of Table III on phoneme-input TTS, non-autoregressive TTS, multi-speaker and multi-language TTS, to test the architecture-independence and language-independence of the conclusion "encoder-side is better than decoder-side," while opening the door to non-English open-vocabulary KWS.
- **Streaming transformation**: Transform the pattern discriminator from a sequence-level last-frame decision to a frame-level monotonic decision,配合 sliding window to achieve detection and keyword localization on continuous audio streams, and report latency and real-time rate, completing the last leg of the journey towards real products.
- **Edge-side evaluation**: Supplement data on parameter count, latency, memory, and power consumption; the audio encoder (two layers of convolution plus two layers of 64-dimensional Bi-GRU, already lightweight in structure) can be further interchanged and compared with small KWS backbones like cFSMN, DS-CNN, to test the feasibility of the complete system under microcontroller-level budgets.
- **Robustness experiment makeup**: Add SNR scans, reverberation, and far-field conditions, directly contrasting with the registration mismatch weakness of QbyE, upgrading "text registration is more stable" from architectural deduction to experimental conclusion.
