## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) systems recognize preset commands and serve as the entry point for human-computer voice interaction. They are almost always deployed on edge terminals such as smart speakers, headphones, and wearable devices, operating 24/7 in a "always-on listening" mode. The paper points out three hard constraints for such deployments in the Introduction: endurance, CPU resources, and portability (Introduction, first paragraph). This means that KWS is less of an "accuracy problem" and more of an energy efficiency problem where "every milliwatt must be split in half"—the model must remain lightweight and low-power while maintaining high accuracy.

Current mainstream KWS systems are implemented using Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), which indeed achieve excellent accuracy. However, the computational core of ANNs is the Multiply-and-Accumulate (MAC) operation, which is burdensome for long-term operation on resource-constrained edge devices. The paper chooses Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) as the solution: as the third generation of neural networks, SNNs work in an asynchronous event-driven manner, transmitting information via binary spikes on the time axis. Computation is triggered only when spikes arrive, making information transmission naturally sparse. More critically, a key hardware basis is that Accumulate (AC) operations are far more energy-efficient than MAC operations—the paper cites the consensus measurement in neuromorphic computing (Horowitz, ISSCC 2014, Reference [31]), stating that the energy consumption ratio of a single floating-point addition to a single floating-point multiplication is approximately 1/71 (the text layer displays "AC/MAC = 71", but based on the dimensions of Equation (8), it should be 1/71, meaning the energy cost of one MAC is about 71 times that of one AC). Therefore, when deployed to hardware, SNNs dominated by AC consume significantly less energy than DNNs dependent on MAC.

However, the paper immediately points out a contradictory status quo: many deep SNN-based KWS schemes still hang manual feature extraction frontends like FFT (Reference [10]) or MFCC (Reference [11]) in front of the network. FFT and MFCC are themselves computationally intensive operations that require large-scale convolution-level computational resources on raw speech. This directly conflicts with the original intention of "using SNNs to save energy"—the electricity saved by the network is eaten back by the frontend.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

Focusing on the three keywords "end-to-end, low energy consumption, and lightweight," the paper breaks down the problems of existing work into four layers, each with a clear failure mechanism:

**Layer 1: Manual frontends are inescapable.** As described in the previous section, the computational overhead of FFT/MFCC frontends violates the original intention of using SNNs. To bypass this, one must perform end-to-end feature extraction directly from raw speech waveforms, i.e., using convolution to ingest the waveform directly (the continuous convolution form in Equation (1), where $f(t)$ is the raw speech sequence and $g(t)$ is the convolution kernel).

**Layer 2: The dilemma of end-to-end convolution.** On the path of direct convolution, the parameter count of standard Conv1d (Reference [18]) grows with the receptive field, leading to parameter redundancy in the convolution kernel $g(t)$. Atrous convolution D-Conv1d (Reference [19]) was proposed to solve this, but the paper uses Figure 1 to illustrate its two failure modes: First, D-Conv1d with stride = 1 (Fig.1(a), one branch of WaveSense) has significant overlap between adjacent windows, resulting in highly redundant hidden and output features (gray blocks in the figure), and it cannot compress the length of long speech sequences—if the sequence is not compressed, the computational load of subsequent layers cannot be reduced. Second, D-Conv1d with stride ≠ 1 (Fig.1(b)) has a receptive field that grows exponentially with the dilation rate $d$, causing sampling holes and loss of local information (white blocks in the figure). The paper concludes from this: at the time, no convolution method existed that could simultaneously solve the problems of "parameter redundancy" and "loss of local features."

**Layer 3: Pitfalls in neuron selection for existing end-to-end SNN-KWS.** Yang et al.'s end-to-end deep residual SNN (Reference [14], Interspeech 2022) achieves high recognition accuracy, but it adopts the IF (integrate-and-fire) neuron model, which lacks a membrane potential decay mechanism. The consequence is frequent spike firing—each extra spike triggers a whole row of synaptic calculations downstream, and the event-driven advantage of SNNs is directly eaten away by the frequent firing (Reference [15]). In other words, "choosing SNN" does not equal "saving electricity." If the neuron dynamics model is chosen incorrectly, sparsity is lost.

**Layer 4: Global-local fusion experience from ANNs cannot be directly transferred.** The idea of global-local feature extraction has had successful cases in ANNs (Branchformer, Reference [23]), but its success relies on complex attention mechanisms, not simply adding two convolution branches. The paper specifically demonstrates the outcome of "direct addition" in ANNs in Fig.3: element-wise addition of the outputs of two convolutions is a linear mixing process, where significant features (orange blocks in the figure) are submerged and become indistinguishable after summation, i.e., "feature disappearance." Furthermore, existing SNN models are generally not lightweight enough: ST-Attention-SNN (Reference [21]) has 2170K parameters, SRNN+ALIF (Reference [27]) has 222.1K, and Speech2Spikes (Reference [28]) has 410K (Table 1), which is still far from the storage and computational budget of edge devices.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the challenge this paper aims to answer can be written in one sentence: How to perform end-to-end feature extraction from raw speech waveforms within a pure spiking neural network framework, while achieving (1) layer-by-layer compression of long speech sequence lengths to save computation without losing local details (solving the dilemma in Fig.1); (2) preventing feature disappearance in the fusion of global and local convolution branches (solving the addition submersion problem in Fig.3); (3) neuron dynamics with inherent membrane potential decay to suppress firing rates (solving the frequent firing problem of IF); and (4) compressing the classification head parameter count to below 100K while maintaining over 90% accuracy (solving the edge lightweight problem). All four points point to the same goal: simultaneous dominance in accuracy, model size, and energy consumption.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire model (Fig.2) consists of two parts: the front end is NConv = 4 GLSC (Global-Local Spiking Convolution) blocks in series, responsible for end-to-end feature extraction; the back end is NCla = 2 Bottleneck-PLIF blocks, responsible for effective classification. The motivation for choosing this shallow "four plus two" configuration is straightforward: edge deployment requires controlling the parameter count to the tens of K range, and deep stacking conflicts with this budget; while the feature extraction segment undertakes sequence length compression at each layer, four layers are sufficient to compress the long waveform sequence layer by layer.

The general idea of the architecture design is to "transfer effective structures verified in the ANN era (dual-branch convolution, Bottleneck residual) into the spiking paradigm, and let the spiking neuron itself become the key component to solve the transplantation obstacles." In ANNs, directly fusing dual-branch convolutions leads to feature disappearance. The paper does not add attention or gating to the fusion branches (which would bring parameter and computational burdens), but instead discovers that the membrane potential dynamics of spiking neurons can naturally repair this problem—this is the most core design insight of the entire paper, elaborated below.

For training, the entire model is trained directly using STBP (Spatio-Temporal Backpropagation, Reference [25]), avoiding the "train ANN first then convert to SNN" conversion route. The reason is that the conversion method usually requires additional time-step redundancy to compensate for conversion accuracy loss, while direct training can jointly optimize membrane potential dynamics and weights end-to-end with a small number of time steps (TimeSteps = 8).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**LIF Neuron Baseline (Equations (2)-(4))**: The membrane potential of neuron $i$ at layer $n$ at time $t+1$ is

$$U_i^{t+1,n} = \tau U_i^{t,n} + \sum_{j=1}^{l(n-1)} w_{ij}^n O_j^{t+1,n-1}$$

where $\tau$ is the constant leakage factor, and the second term is the synaptic input from the previous layer (weighted sum of spikes). When the membrane potential exceeds the threshold $V_{th}$, a spike is fired and hard reset:

$$O_i^{t+1,n} = H(U_i^{t+1,n} - V_{th}), \quad U_i^{t+1,n} \leftarrow U_i^{t+1,n}(1 - O_i^{t+1,n})$$

$H$ is the Heaviside step function. Note that "hard reset" means the membrane potential is directly cleared to zero after firing, without retaining any remainder.

**GLSC Module (Equation (5))**:

$$outputs = H\big(b_n(g_1(t) * f(t)) + b_n(g_2(t) * f(t))\big)$$

$g_1$ is the Conv1d kernel (responsible for local features), and $g_2$ is the D-Conv1d kernel (responsible for global features). Both branches share the same input $f(t)$, pass through batch normalization $bn$ respectively, are added together, and then pass through the spiking neuron firing function $H$. That is, the fusion of the two branches occurs at the membrane potential level, and threshold decision is made only after fusion.

**PLIF Neuron (Equation (6))**:

$$U_i^{t+1,n} = U_i^{t,n} - k(a)\Big(U_i^{t,n} - \sum_j w_{ij}^n O_j^{t+1,n-1}\Big)$$

Compared to Equation (2), the constant $\tau$ is replaced by the learnable $k(a)$. Rearranging gives $U^{t+1} = (1-k(a))U^t + k(a) \cdot I^{t+1}$: $k(a)$ weights both membrane potential decay and synaptic input, with an effective time constant of $1-k(a)$, which is optimized via gradients during training.

**Bottleneck-PLIF Module (Equation (7))**:

$$Outputs = H\big[f_1\big(H\big(f_3(H(f_1(Input))) + f_1(Input)\big)\big)\big]$$

$f_n$ denotes $n \times n$ convolution plus batch normalization. $f_1$ is a $1 \times 1$ convolution for channel fusion and dimensionality reduction, $f_3$ is a $3 \times 3$ convolution for further computation, and $H$ is the PLIF firing function. Structurally, this is the spiking version of the ResNet (Reference [17]) Bottleneck block: $1 \times 1$ dimensionality reduction, $3 \times 3$ computation, residual direct connection ($f_1(Input)$), and spiking neurons placed between each stage.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: GLSC – Global-Local Spiking Convolution Module

GLSC aims to solve the dilemma in Fig.1 simultaneously. The design logic is dual-branch division of labor: the D-Conv1d branch ($g_2$) is paired with stride ≠ 1, using an exponentially growing receptive field to capture global dependencies and compress sequence length layer by layer; the Conv1d branch ($g_1$) uses small kernels and dense sampling to guard local details. Thus, the white blocks in Fig.1(b) representing "atrous convolution losing local information" are filled by the dense coverage of the Conv1d branch; while the gray blocks in Fig.1(a) representing "stride=1 feature redundancy" are eliminated by the length compression of stride ≠ 1. The comparison diagrams in Fig.1(c)(d) show that this combination achieves a balance between global and local features on long speech sequences, and maintains consistent attention to local features even when stride ≠ 1.

The real insight lies in "why adding two branches in SNN does not cause feature disappearance." The paper provides a mechanistic explanation in Section 3.1 (accompanied by the left-right comparison in Fig.3): In the ANN version (GLC-ANN, blue line), the two continuous value outputs are directly added, and the significant details represented by the orange blocks are submerged by the dynamic range after summation. In GLSC, the output of the spiking neuron at time $t$ depends not only on the current summation but also carries the membrane potential $U^t$ from time $t-1$ (Fig.3 right, $U^{t+1}$ represents the contribution of $U^t$ after decay to the membrane potential). This brings two effects: First, membrane potential accumulation across time steps gives features a "second accumulation axis in the time dimension," allowing components diluted by addition at the current moment to continue accumulating in subsequent time steps, significantly alleviating feature disappearance caused by summation. Second, the neuron only fires when the sum plus residual membrane potential exceeds the threshold $V_{th}$ (green blocks in Fig.3), and irrelevant features do not even qualify for accumulation. Therefore, the output of GLSC is naturally more sparse—threshold gating performs free hard feature selection for the network. The paper explicitly promises "to verify this through ablation experiments" (see the GLC-ANN control in the experimental section).

It is worth emphasizing the distinction of this technical route from Branchformer: Branchformer also performs global-local operations, but it relies on attention mechanisms to ensure fusion quality; GLSC does not use attention, directly adding the two convolution branches, and delegates the task of "preventing feature disappearance" to the membrane potential dynamics of spiking neurons. The cost is smaller, and the mechanism is more朴素 (simple), which is also why it can hold within a tens of K parameter budget.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Bottleneck-PLIF Classification Module

The second module aims to solve the problem of "higher accuracy with fewer parameters," composed of two parts.

**PLIF part solves neuron dynamics.** Compared to traditional LIF, the paper points out two enhancements of PLIF (Reference [15]): First, the learnable $k(a)$ replaces the constant decay hyperparameter $\tau$ in Equation (2)—the constant $\tau$ requires uniform network-wide setting and manual grid search, while $k(a)$ is automatically optimized with training gradients, effectively upgrading the "time scale" from a hyperparameter to a parameter. Second, $k(a)$ acts on the input side simultaneously (the input term in Equation (6) is weighted by $k(a)$). A deeper benefit is illustrated by Fig.4: different neurons can have different $\tau$, presenting different membrane potential leakage rates under the same input (Fig.4 right), thereby producing diverse output spikes (Fig.4 left). This is particularly valuable in speech tasks—speech events are naturally heterogeneous in time scale; transient consonants are millisecond-level events, while the resonance structure of long vowels lasts for hundreds of milliseconds. A unified $\tau$ is equivalent to prescribing a single "memory duration" for the entire network, whereas heterogeneous time constants act like a set of multi-scale adaptive time filters. This is also a direct response to the "IF without decay leads to frequent firing" problem mentioned above: learnable decay suppresses the firing rate, ensuring energy efficiency.

**Bottleneck part solves parameter efficiency.** The paper borrows the motivation of ResNet's Bottleneck structure (Section 3.2): it can efficiently integrate feature information with fewer parameters and reduce dimensionality through channel fusion. In Equation (7), the $1 \times 1$ convolution of $f_1$ is responsible for "fusing channels without damaging input features," reducing feature dimensionality without destroying the original structure; the $3 \times 3$ convolution of $f_3$ is responsible for "further computing the previous spiking features," continuing to process the signals output by GLSC with a lighter model size; the residual direct connection ensures that information does not collapse during dimensionality reduction. Embedding PLIF into every gap of the Bottleneck yields a lightweight spiking classifier. Ablation experiments (Fig.6(c)) prove that the parameter robustness of this combination is significantly superior to the control classifier.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Comparing with three related routes clarifies the position of this paper. Compared to the "manual features + SNN" route: GLSC extracts features end-to-end from raw waveforms, cutting off the FFT/MFCC frontend, and implementing "energy saving" down to the input end. Compared to WaveSense (Reference [13], atrous convolution stride=1 streaming model): WaveSense cannot compress long speech sequences and has obvious feature redundancy; GLSC compresses length layer by layer using stride ≠ 1 and preserves local features with dual branches. Compared to end-to-end residual SNN (Reference [14]): the latter uses non-decaying IF neurons that fire frequently; GLSC/Bottleneck-PLIF uses PLIF with learnable decay throughout, suppressing the firing rate to an average of 8.3% for the whole network (Fig.5). Compared to ANN global-local structures like Branchformer: it does not rely on attention, but completes fusion through membrane potential accumulation and threshold gating. Compared to high-precision SNNs like ST-Attention-SNN and SRNN+ALIF: accuracy is slightly lower (see experiments), but the model is an order of magnitude smaller.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Experiments were conducted on the V1 and V2 versions of the Google Speech Commands (GSC, Reference [16]) dataset. V1 contains 30 short commands and 1881 speakers; V2 contains 35 short commands and 2618 speakers. To ensure fair comparison with previous SNN models (References [14][24]), the experiments adopted two classification task settings: 12-class and 35-class. The 12-class task includes 10 commands ("yes", "no", "up", "down", "left", "right", "on", "off", "stop", "go") plus a silence class and an unknown class; the unknown class covers the remaining 20 (25) commands in the 30 (35) command set; the silence class is generated by splicing noise files from the dataset, accounting for approximately 10% of the total data. For data splitting, GSC-V1 is divided into 56,588 training, 7,743 validation, and 7,835 test samples; GSC-V2 is divided into 92,843 training, 11,003 validation, and 12,005 test samples. The entire model is trained directly using the STBP method (Section 4.1).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper uses three metrics, corresponding to the triple constraints of edge deployment. The first is classification accuracy (12-class and 35-class tasks), corresponding to the baseline requirement of "high accuracy"; the second is model size (parameter count in K), corresponding to the storage and computational budget of edge devices—the paper repeatedly emphasizes that model size directly determines deployment difficulty; the third is the energy efficiency ratio, estimated according to the standard formula in the neuromorphic computing field (Equation (8)):

$$Energy\ rate = \frac{AC}{MAC} \times SpikingRate \times TimeSteps$$

where AC/MAC is the single-operation energy consumption ratio of SNN floating-point addition to ANN floating-point multiplication, SpikingRate is the average spike firing rate, and TimeSteps is the simulation time window. The rationale for choosing this combination of metrics: for always-on listening KWS, energy consumption and parameter count are equal citizens to accuracy; and Equation (8) directly links energy consumption to firing rate, thereby making "whether the neuron design suppresses firing" a quantifiable and supervised quantity—this is precisely the reason the paper chose decay-capable PLIF and emphasized sparse feature extraction.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The main results are in Table 1, grouped by three tasks.

**GSC-V1 12-class task**: The proposed SNN-KWS achieves 93.0% accuracy with 70.1K parameters; compared to NLIF full SNN (Reference [26]) with 220K parameters and 87.9%, the proposed parameter count is about one-third of its, with accuracy 5.1 percentage points higher; compared to end-to-end residual SNN (Reference [14]) with 86.5K parameters and 92.2%, the proposed method uses 16.4K fewer parameters (saving about 19%) while surpassing accuracy by 0.8 percentage points. This group best reflects "comprehensive dominance over opponents of similar magnitude."

**GSC-V2 12-class task**: The proposed method has 70.1K parameters and 94.4% accuracy. Compared to three tiers of opponents: ST-Attention-SNN (Reference [21]) with 2170K parameters and 95.1%—the proposed accuracy is 0.7 percentage points lower, but the model is about 31 times smaller; SpikGRU (Reference [29]) with 111K parameters and 94.9%—the proposed accuracy is 0.5 percentage points lower, with parameters reduced by about 37%; SLAYER-RF-CNN (Reference [24]) with 280K parameters and 91.4%—the proposed accuracy is 3.0 percentage points higher and the model is 4 times smaller. It can be seen that in the 12-class task, the proposed method did not achieve the highest accuracy in the SNN series, but is in a Pareto position of "slightly yielding in accuracy, leading by orders of magnitude in size."

**GSC-V2 35-class task**: The proposed method has 80.2K parameters and 92.9% accuracy. Compared to SRNN+ALIF (Reference [27]) with 222.1K parameters and 92.5%—the proposed accuracy is 0.4 percentage points higher, with parameters reduced by about 64%; compared to Speech2Spikes (Reference [28]) with 410K parameters and 89.5%—the proposed accuracy is 3.4 percentage points higher, with parameters reduced by about one-fifth (model size about 5 times smaller); compared to LSTMs-SNN (Reference [22]) with 91.5% (model size N/A)—the proposed is 1.4 percentage points higher; compared to WaveSense (Reference [13]) with 79.5% (model size N/A)—the proposed is 13.4 percentage points higher. In the 35-class task, the proposed method actually achieved the highest accuracy among the comparison groups, while maintaining the smallest parameter count.

The paper's own summary of Table 1 is very honest: accuracy is slightly lower than ST-Attention-SNN and SRNN+ALIF (in the 12-class task, the former is higher; in the 35-class task, the latter is actually surpassed, mainly referring to the 12-class task), but the model size is significantly smaller, reaching a "dual competitive" position in accuracy and parameter efficiency among SNN series models in both tasks. Additionally, note that the 35-class model (80.2K) is 10.1K larger than the 12-class model (70.1K); the paper does not report the specific source of this difference (reasonably inferred to be the increase in output classes and corresponding channel adjustments, but not stated in the original text).

**Energy Efficiency Comparison** (Section 4.3, Equation (8)): Fig.5 reports that the average spike firing rate of the entire network on GSC-V1 with TimeSteps = 8 is approximately 8.3% (average per module). Substituting into Equation (8): Energy rate ≈ (1/71) × 0.083 × 8 ≈ 0.0094, meaning the theoretical energy consumption is about one-hundredth of the same-structure ANN. Based on this, the paper conservatively claims "more than 10 times energy saving compared to the same-structure ANN"—even by conservative standards, it exceeds the tenfold line. The meaning of "same-structure ANN control" here is: replacing the spiking neurons in GLSC and Bottleneck-PLIF with their corresponding ANN versions with continuous activation functions (i.e., the generalization of the GLC-ANN setting in ablation), ensuring structural fairness. The energy calculation is a theoretical estimate rather than chip measurement; the paper explicitly lists "real implementation on neuromorphic chips" as future work in the conclusion.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Ablation experiments focus on the two modules respectively (Section 4.4, Fig.6), with three core findings.

**Finding 1: GLSC consistently outperforms single convolution methods.** Comparing GLSC with single convolution methods (black and green lines in Fig.6(a)(b)) under the same parameter count, GLSC (red line) consistently dominates in accuracy and convergence. This verifies that the dual-branch design is not parameter waste: under the same parameter budget, the division of labor between "global branch + local branch" is more effective than a single branch.

**Finding 2: Spiking neurons are the key to solving feature disappearance.** The blue line GLC-ANN in Fig.6 is the version where the spiking neurons in the GLSC module are replaced by ANN continuous activation functions. The comparison between the red line (GLSC) and the blue line (GLC-ANN) directly proves the mechanistic claim in Section 3.1 of the paper—spiking neurons and their membrane potential residuals play a key role in preventing feature disappearance. In other words, the structure of adding global-local dual branches is not enough on its own; it must be paired with "cross-time-step membrane potential accumulation + threshold gating" to rescue significant features diluted by addition. This is the cleanest set of ablations in the entire paper's causal chain.

**Finding 3: Bottleneck-PLIF's advantage amplifies as parameters tighten.** Fig.6(c) shows that as the parameter count decreases, the performance of all classifiers declines, but the impact of parameter reduction on Bottleneck-PLIF is the smallest—achieving 93% accuracy even when parameters are below 100K. This indicates that the channel dimensionality reduction plus residual design of the Bottleneck structure has stronger information preservation capabilities in the small parameter domain than conventional classification heads, and also explains why the main model can compress total parameters to the 70-80K range without accuracy dropping out of the 93% tier.

It should be pointed out that the ablation section only provides curve graphs (Fig.6(a)(b)(c)), without providing point-by-point numerical tables or variance across multiple random seeds; specific quantitative differences need to be read from the graphs, which is a weak point in the experimental presentation.

## Main Contributions

The paper states three contributions (end of Introduction), which can be expanded in conjunction with experiments:

1. **GLSC Module**: Designed a spiking convolution module that balances global and local features and is more energy-efficient, capable of compressing long speech sequence lengths layer by layer while avoiding the dilemma of feature redundancy and local information loss in single convolution methods shown in Fig.1, achieving sparser end-to-end feature extraction (ablation verifies its superiority over single convolution controls, and spiking neurons are proven to be the key to preventing feature disappearance).
2. **Bottleneck-PLIF Module**: Combines the ResNet Bottleneck structure with PLIF neurons to construct a lighter spiking classifier—learnable time constants provide heterogeneous time scales (Fig.4), channel fusion dimensionality reduction provides parameter efficiency, and accuracy remains at 93% even when parameters are below 100K (Fig.6(c)).
3. **End-to-End SNN-KWS Overall Model**: The complete model of 4 GLSC blocks plus 2 Bottleneck-PLIF blocks achieves 93.0%/94.4%/92.9% accuracy with 70.1K/80.2K parameters on the 12-class and 35-class tasks of GSC-V1/V2 (Table 1), achieving dual competitiveness in accuracy and parameter efficiency among SNN series models, and estimating more than 10 times energy saving relative to the same-structure ANN according to Equation (8).

In one sentence: It proves that the route of "structure transplantation + neuron dynamics as the bottom line"—transplanting ANN's dual-branch convolution and Bottleneck into the spiking paradigm, and letting membrane potential accumulation and threshold gating repair the feature fusion that would fail in the ANN version—can produce end-to-end spiking KWS with accuracy that does not lag behind within a tens of K parameter budget.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**Energy consumption conclusion is a theoretical estimate, not a measurement.** Equation (8) only counts the factors of "AC replacing MAC" and firing rate, ignoring the timing overhead of spike routing, sparse readout, membrane potential updates themselves, and the equivalent computational repetition brought by time-step unfolding (T=8)—the same convolution must be calculated once for each of the 8 time steps. This amplification effect is counted in Equation (8) in the form of the TimeSteps factor, but whether real neuromorphic hardware can cash in sparsity into proportional energy savings depends on the quality of the chip's event-driven implementation. The paper's conclusion also acknowledges this: "real implementation on neuromorphic chips" is listed as future work.

**Width overhead of GLSC dual branches.** Dual-branch parallelism means the convolution width in the feature extraction segment is twice that of a single branch. The paper responds to this with an ablation design of "comparison under the same parameter count," but the impact of the parallel computational structure on memory access patterns during inference is not reported. Additionally, specific configurations of kernel size, channel number, stride, etc., for the two convolution branches within GLSC are not given in the main text (only illustrated in the Fig.2 structure diagram); reproduction requires finding them in the code, and the paper does not report these hyperparameters.

**Missing details on time steps and input encoding.** The model fixes TimeSteps = 8, but how the raw waveform is segmented and mapped to 8 time steps, and the sensitivity of accuracy and energy consumption to the number of time steps, are not reported by the paper. T is a multiplicative factor in the energy efficiency formula and a key degree of freedom for accuracy; the absence of this dimension makes the "energy-accuracy" trade-off curve incomplete.

**Simplification at the neuron model level.** The learnable time constant of PLIF provides heterogeneous dynamics as described by the paper (Fig.4), but the granularity of heterogeneity (one k per layer or one k per neuron) and the distribution of learned k values are not reported. The firing rate is only given as an overall average of 8.3% (Fig.5); the differences in layer-by-layer firing rates and their impact on energy distribution are not elaborated.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

**Comparison scope limited to within the SNN series.** All baselines in Table 1 are SNN or pulse-hybrid models; there is no comparison with ANN small models (such as DS-CNN, MatchboxNet, etc., mainstream KWS architectures in the industry) or quantized ANNs, and the paper does not report any ANN baseline numbers on the same dataset. This means the main claim of "10 times energy saving relative to ANN" is only supported at the level of the energy formula, lacking the complete puzzle piece of "how much accuracy is lost relative to ANN"—readers cannot evaluate from the paper how much accuracy cost is paid for exchanging efficiency for SNN.

**Ablation lacks numerical tables and statistical significance.** All three groups of ablations in Fig.6 are presented as curves, without point-by-point values, multi-seed variance, or confidence intervals. Especially for key conclusions like "still reaching 93% when parameters are below 100K," there is only one graph reading for support.

**Single task setting.** Evaluation is only performed on the 12-class and 35-class classification tasks of GSC, without covering key scenarios in real KWS deployment: custom wake words (detection of minority classes), far-field reverberation, robustness to additive noise, and streaming low-latency inference (RTT/latency is not reported by the paper). The silence class is generated by splicing noise (accounting for about 10%), and the distribution difference from real silence collection is also not discussed.

**Missing explanation for model size differences.** The paper does not report where the 10.1K difference between the 35-class model (80.2K) and the 12-class model (70.1K) comes from, nor whether it implies that channel numbers are adjusted according to the number of classes.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The direction given by the paper itself is to perform real implementation on neuromorphic chips to verify theoretical energy advantages. Following the mechanistic claims of this paper, there are several natural extensions: First, refine the granularity of learnable time constants (neuron-level, or even input-adaptive), further approaching the speech processing intuition of "multi-scale time filter banks"; Second, complete the accuracy-energy Pareto curve for time step T, exploring variable time-step inference adaptive to input—the spiking paradigm naturally supports "no spike, no computation" early stopping, which is a degree of freedom ANNs do not have; Third, perform knowledge distillation with ANN teachers to make up for the accuracy gap with ANN-series SOTA (there is still a gap of about 0.5-0.7 percentage points in the 12-class task in Table 1); Fourth, generalize the GLSC mechanism of "membrane potential accumulation preventing feature disappearance" to other spiking structures requiring multi-branch fusion (multi-scale, multi-branch backbones), testing the universality of this insight; Fifth, re-evaluate the effectiveness of the architecture in streaming, custom wake word, and far-field scenarios, completing the evidence chain from a deployment perspective.
