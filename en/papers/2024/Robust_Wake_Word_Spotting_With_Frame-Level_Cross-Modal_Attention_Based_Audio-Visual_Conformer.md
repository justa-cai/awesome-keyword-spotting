## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Wake Word Spotting (WWS, also known as Keyword Spotting, KWS) is the task of detecting specific keywords or key phrases within a continuous audio stream. It serves as the core entry component for voice-activated devices such as smart speakers, mobile phones, and wearable devices. In recent years, deep neural networks (CNNs, Transformers, etc.) have achieved excellent results in near-field, clean speech environments. However, the paper points out the vulnerability of such systems right from the beginning: performance degrades significantly when shifted to far-field scenarios. The degradation in far-field home environments stems from three叠加 (superimposed) factors: first, the decrease in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and increased reverberation due to the increased distance between the speaker and the device; second, speech overlap caused by background sound sources such as home televisions and conversations; and third, the extremely short duration of wake words themselves (in this scenario, only about 2.56 seconds for the four syllables of "Xiao T, Xiao T"). The density of discriminative information available for the model is limited, and any syllable submerged in noise may lead to missed detections or false alarms.

To address far-field degradation, there has been considerable work on the single-modal audio route: domain-aware training, expanding training data with generated samples, and Multi-View Minimum Variance Distortionless Response (MVDR) beamforming. However, the ceiling for all these methods is the same—they only utilize the acoustic channel, which is itself a polluted information source. Beamforming can alleviate but not eliminate reverberation and overlapping speech, which determines that pure audio solutions have a principled upper limit in complex far-field acoustic scenarios.

Visual lip-motion information provides an orthogonal complementary channel: lip movements are completely unaffected by acoustic noise. No matter how noisy the TV is or how much speech overlaps, the opening and closing trajectory of the speaker's lips remains clearly distinguishable. This is the common motivation for introducing the visual modality in fields such as Audio-Visual Speech Recognition (AVSR), Audio-Visual Speaker Separation, and Audio-Visual Speaker Verification. It has also given rise to the niche direction of Audio-Visual Wake Word Spotting (AVWWS) following the MISP Challenge 2021.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper reviews the technical routes of AVWWS work after MISP 2021 and points out three specific deficiencies.

The first category is the "separate-then-fuse" two-stage fusion paradigm. The approach of most existing systems (Cheng et al., Zhang et al., Wang et al.) is to first train two robust single-modal models (one audio model, one visual model) independently, and then fuse the results of these two models to produce the final output. The problem with this paradigm is that the two single-modal models optimize their respective objectives during training. Their parameters are often frozen during the fusion stage, and the fusion module can only passively accept two mutually ill-adapted representations. The synergistic relationship of "who is more trustworthy at what time" between the two modalities has never been jointly optimized. Although Xu et al.'s system trained an end-to-end audio-visual Transformer, the final result still relied on single-modal model voting, discounting the benefits of end-to-end training.

The second category is overly coarse fusion operators. Whether in the Audio-Visual Speech Recognition field (Ma et al., Burchi et al., Hong et al.) or the AVWWS field, multimodal fusion generally adopts simple addition or concatenation: either concatenating the two feature streams along the feature dimension before the encoder (early fusion), or concatenating/adding them after their respective encoders (late fusion). In formula terms, this is $X_{fused} = [X_{spec} \| X_{lip}]W_{fc}$ ($W_{fc} \in \mathbb{R}^{2D \times D}$) or $X_{fused} = X_{spec} + X_{lip}$. These operations treat every dimension and every time frame of the features equally, with the implicit assumption that "the contribution of both modalities is uniform across all frames." However, the reality is that noise is bursty, lip occlusion is sporadic, and within the same wake word utterance, some frames are reliable in audio while others can only be saved by lip shapes. Static linear combinations like addition and concatenation cannot express this frame-level inter-modal correlation, and the modeling of interactions between modalities has remained relatively blank in previous work.

The third category is insufficient utilization of training data. Multimodal labeled data is scarce (the positive samples in the training set of this paper are only 5K+), and most existing work trains multimodal models from scratch, failing to fully leverage the strong initialization that single-modal data or single-modal pre-training might provide.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, this paper aims to solve three progressively layered problems simultaneously: First, how to model the inter-modal correlation between audio and lip motion at a fine-grained (frame-level) scale, rather than stopping at coarse-grained addition or concatenation of segment-level features—lip motion and speech are highly synchronized on the scale of tens of milliseconds, which is the physical basis for frame-level modeling. Second, how to enable the joint end-to-end optimization of network parameters for both modalities under a single objective, breaking away from the old paradigm of "freezing single-modal models and then fusing" or "multi-model voting." Third, under the condition of limited multimodal positive samples, how to leverage single-modal pre-training to give the multimodal model a stronger starting point. The combined effect of these three is verified by a hard metric: refreshing the best WWS score on the Eval set of the far-field MISP dataset at that time.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system (Fig. 2(a) in the paper) consists of six modules connected in series: audio frontend, visual frontend, Transformer or Conformer encoder with the FLCMA module, convolutional fusion module, attention pooling layer, and fully connected classifier. The core idea in the design is "division of labor": the Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) in the standard Conformer block is responsible for global correlation within the single modality, the convolution module (CONV) is responsible for local correlation within the single modality, and the newly inserted FLCMA module is dedicated to inter-modal interaction on every frame. That is, single-modal temporal modeling and cross-modal interaction are split into two orthogonal attention components, placed into the same encoder block, and stacked N layers to work jointly.

The design of the two frontends revolves around "time alignment." The audio path uses 80-dimensional FBank features (25 ms window length, 10 ms shift), taking 256 frames per sample. After being compressed into temporal features by a subsampling module consisting of two layers of 2D convolution, the time dimension is downsampled to one-quarter of the original (from 256 frames to 64 frames), and then projected to the encoder dimension via a linear layer. The motivation for downsampling is straightforward: the audio frame rate is approximately 100 Hz, while video is typically only 25 frames per second. Without prior alignment, frame-level cross-modal attention cannot be performed—64 video frames correspond to 2.56 seconds, which aligns strictly in physical time with 256 audio frames (10 ms × 256 = 2.56 seconds). The visual path uses ResNet-18 with 3D convolutions (ResNet3D-18) to process lip-region videos, with an input shape of (64, 112, 112, 3). The 3D convolution retains the time dimension while extracting spatial features, followed by global average pooling along the spatial dimension to compress it into (T, D) temporal features, which are then projected to the same encoder dimension as audio via a linear layer. After alignment, the features from both paths are in the same time coordinate system within the encoder, allowing FLCMA to have a "same frame" to discuss.

After the encoder is the convolutional fusion module (Fig. 3 in the paper): the dual-modal feature maps are treated as "images" with the channel dimension being the number of modalities, and compressed step-by-step using a series of 2D convolutions with kernel sizes of 5×5 and channel numbers of 4, 2, and 1, respectively, finally outputting fused features of shape (B, 1, T, D). The design motivation (inspired by MFCCA) is a key sentence in the paper: reducing "corruption" caused by directly fusing multimodal features. Addition or concatenation indiscriminately mixes the two streams of information, where clean lip motion features might be dragged down by noisy audio features; whereas the learnable convolution stack allows the network to decide how to mix information from the two channels in each spatiotemporal neighborhood. This is followed by an attention pooling layer (borrowing a common component from the speaker verification field), which estimates importance weights frame-by-frame to aggregate a more robust sentence-level classification vector, which is finally passed through a fully connected layer with a sigmoid output to produce the wake word probability, trained with BCE loss.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The formal starting point of the method is to extend a new modality dimension for each single-modal feature: let audio features be $X_{spec} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times D}$ and visual features be $X_{lip} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times D}$. Concatenating them along the new modality dimension yields the multimodal feature $X_{av} = [X_{spec}, X_{lip}] \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times T \times D}$, where $M=2$. This step is the hub of the entire design: it transforms "two streams of features" into "two modality slices of a single tensor," allowing subsequent attention and convolution operations to explicitly operate on this modality axis, so that modality identity information is not erased by the concatenation operation.

The FLCMA module (Eq. 1 in the paper) defines head-wise cross-modal attention on this tensor:

$$Q_i^{cm} = X_{av}W_i^{cm,q} + (b_i^{cm,q})^T \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times M \times D/h}$$
$$K_i^{cm} = X_{av}W_i^{cm,k} + (b_i^{cm,k})^T \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times M \times D/h}$$
$$V_i^{cm} = X_{av}W_i^{cm,v} + (b_i^{cm,v})^T \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times M \times D/h}$$
$$O_i^{cm} = \mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{Q_i^{cm}(K_i^{cm})^T}{\sqrt{D/h}}\right)V_i^{cm} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times M \times D/h}$$
$$O^{cm} = [O_0^{cm}, O_1^{cm}, \ldots] \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times M \times D}$$

where $h$ is the number of attention heads, with each head receiving $D/h$ dimensions (in this paper, $D=256$, $h=4$, so 64 dimensions per head). The outputs of each head are concatenated back to $D$ dimensions. Translated into action: on each time frame, the audio embedding and visual embedding perform a scaled dot-product attention with each other—the audio frame "queries" supplementary information in the lip motion of this frame, and the visual frame "queries" disambiguation information in the acoustic signal of the same frame. The scope of attention is deliberately restricted to the modality dimension within the frame (which is also the origin of the name "frame-level"), while long-range dependencies across time are handled by the MHSA in the same block. The two components perform their respective duties. The value of this module is bidirectional, as succinctly stated in the paper: providing complementary lip motion information for noisy acoustic information (lip shapes cannot be knocked out by noise), while reducing phonetic confusion of lip motion based on the speech signal (many phonemes have similar mouth shapes, i.e., the viseme problem, which is disambiguated by querying back from the audio).

The training objective uses weighted binary cross-entropy, with a weight ratio of negative to positive samples of 1:5, directly responding to the severe class imbalance in the training set of 47K+ negative samples against 5K+ positive samples. Regarding evaluation metrics (Eq. 2, Eq. 3 in the paper): $FRR = N_{FR}/N_{wake}$, $FAR = N_{FA}/N_{non\text{-}wake}$. The final WWS score is defined as the sum of the two, $Score_{WWS} = FRR + FAR$, meaning that missed detection rate and false alarm rate are equally important, and the smaller the sum, the better.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Frame-Level Cross-Modal Attention Module (FLCMA)

The inspiration for FLCMA comes from Channel-Level Cross-Channel Attention (CLCCA) in the field of multi-channel speech processing: Wang et al., Horiguchi et al. used it to model frame-level correlation in multi-channel speech signals, improving speaker separation and recognition; Yu et al.'s MFCCA further performed multi-frame cross-channel attention in multi-speaker meeting ASR. The insight of this paper is to generalize "channel" to "modality"—just as it is necessary to judge frame-by-frame which channel is trustworthy among multi-microphone channels, it is equally necessary to judge frame-by-frame which modality is trustworthy between audio and visual modalities. The mathematical structure is isomorphic, with the information carrier changing from acoustic channels to lip motion video.

Why frame-level granularity is necessary can be understood from two aspects. From an information theory perspective, lip motion and speech are two projections of the same articulatory action, highly synchronized at the frame level. Averaging or concatenating segment-level features before fusion is equivalent to diluting the frame-level correspondence first and then expecting the network to find it back, resulting in a resolution that is inevitably too coarse. From an experimental perspective, the attention rollout visualization in Fig. 4 of the paper shows that the model's high attention weights are concentrated around 0.25s, 1.0s, 1.5s, and 2.3s—exactly corresponding to the identifiable feature positions of the four syllables of "Xiao T, Xiao T." This indicates that discriminative information is inherently sparsely distributed across a few critical moments, and the frame-level mechanism allows the model to precisely allocate computation and trust to these moments. Fig. 5 shows the attention map inside the FLCMA module: the same modality receives different attention weights on different frames. The model customizes the trust allocation between modalities frame-by-frame, pulling information from the other modality to reinforce when one modality lacks confidence. This is an adaptive behavior that simple addition/concatenation cannot achieve structurally.

There is another often overlooked benefit in the structure: with the explicit axis of the modality dimension, a single encoder can process both modalities simultaneously (parameters are shared across modalities), replacing the two separate single-modal encoders in late fusion schemes. The paper explicitly points this out in the ablation analysis—FLCMA replaces two split single-modal encoders with a single multimodal encoder and achieves better AUC (Eval set +0.097/+0.125, Table 1), indicating that sharing parameters across modalities does not interfere with each other but instead forms better representations due to joint training.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: End-to-End Joint Training with Convolutional Fusion

The second innovation is to carry "end-to-end" to the end. Unlike the previous paradigm of "two frozen single-modal models + fusion," the audio frontend, visual frontend, multimodal encoder, fusion module, pooling layer, and classifier in this paper are all jointly trained from scratch under a single BCE loss (for the pre-trained version, it is single-modal pre-training followed by joint fine-tuning). There is no freezing stage, nor is there a fallback logic of single-modal model voting. The significance of joint optimization lies in the fact that the fusion behavior back-propagates to feature extraction—the encoder knows that its output will eventually interact with the other modality at the frame level, so it learns to extract representations that are "convenient for cross-modal alignment," which is impossible in two-stage training in terms of the gradient path.

Paired with end-to-end is the convolutional fusion module (Fig. 3 in the paper). The (B, M, T, D) dual-modal features output by the encoder are not directly added or concatenated. Instead, they are treated as 2D feature maps with the modality dimension as the channel, and compressed step-by-step into a single fused feature through three groups of 5×5 convolutions (channels 4→2→1). Compared to static addition/concatenation, this set of convolutions is a learnable fuser: the 5×5 kernel simultaneously covers the local neighborhood of 5 time frames and 5 feature dimensions. Information from the two modalities is mixed with position weighting on the channel dimension. Which regions trust audio more and which trust lip motion more is learned from the data.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Single-Modal Pre-training Transfer Strategy

The third innovation is a pre-training strategy targeting the scarcity of multimodal positive samples, divided into three steps: Step 1, train a single-modal model (frontend + single-modal encoder + pooling layer + classifier) using the same training configuration as the multimodal model; Step 2, transfer the parameters of the two single-modal models to the corresponding positions in the FLCMA multimodal model for initialization; Step 3, fine-tune the entire multimodal model with a learning rate of 0.0001 (0.001 for training from scratch). The logic is consistent with the pre-training-finetuning paradigm in speech recognition (such as HuBERT-style self-supervised pre-training) and transfer learning: when multimodal labeled data is limited, the general acoustic/visual representations learned on single-modal tasks are valuable priors. Direct initialization allows the multimodal model to start from a high point, rather than groping from scratch on limited multimodal data.

This strategy has an counter-intuitive experimental finding (detailed in the experimental section): the single-modal model used for pre-training was not the best single-modal system at that time, but it still significantly improved the performance of the multimodal end-to-end system after transfer. This indicates that the value of pre-training lies not in "how strong the teacher is," but in "how stable the starting point is."

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

The comparison with representative work after MISP 2021 is as follows. Compared to the official baseline and LSTM schemes, this paper changes the architecture to an attention-based system. Compared to the "two robust single-modal models + frozen fusion" paradigm of Cheng et al., Zhang et al., and Wang et al., the biggest difference in this paper is the end-to-end joint optimization of the entire link, and the fusion granularity sinks from coarse sentence-level fusion to frame-level cross-modal attention. Compared to the end-to-end Transformer of Xu et al., this paper removes the single-modal voting step, giving the multimodal model exclusive decision-making power. Compared to the addition/concatenation fusion commonly used in the Audio-Visual Speech Recognition field, this paper writes the fusion as an explicit cross-modal attention operator plus a learnable convolutional fuser. Compared to CLCCA/MFCCA in the multi-channel field, this paper is a migration application of "cross-modal" rather than "cross-channel," extending channel correlation modeling to modality correlation modeling.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The experiments were conducted on the AVWWS dataset from the first MISP Challenge 2021, with the goal of detecting the wake word "Xiao T, Xiao T" spoken in far-field home scenarios. The publicly released database totals approximately 125 hours, divided into a training set and a development set (Dev): the training set contains 47K+ negative samples and 5K+ positive samples (positive-to-negative ratio of approximately 1:9), and the development set contains 2K+ negative samples and 600+ positive samples. There is also an evaluation set (Eval) with 8K+ samples, containing only far-field data with undisclosed labels. To ensure fair comparison with other teams, the authors obtained the evaluation set labels from the MISP organizing committee. The multimodal attributes of the data are very complete: audio includes three specifications of single-channel near-field, 2-channel mid-field, and 6-channel far-field; video includes single-person high-definition mid-field video and multi-person far-field video. This composition highlights the difficulty of the task—the evaluation set consists entirely of far-field data, meaning the model must be tested on the modality combination with the worst acoustic conditions.

The engineering details of preprocessing and augmentation are dense. On the audio side: 80-dimensional FBank, 25ms/10ms, 256 frames per sample. Augmentation methods include negative sample segment slicing, speed perturbation, slight cropping, and SpecAugment. Additionally, room impulse responses were generated using pyroomacoustics to artificially simulate mid-field and far-field audio from near-field audio, overlaid with officially provided noise (SNR randomly between -15 and 15 dB), and MVDR beam-enhanced audio was also added to the training data—the addition of far-field simulated data and beamforming data directly aligns with the far-field distribution of the evaluation set. On the visual side: the RetinaFace face detector and ArcFace face recognizer were used to lock the reference face and facial keypoints of the target speaker in multi-person far-field videos. The lip region was cropped according to the keypoints, uniformly scaled to 112×112, 3-channel RGB, 64 frames per sample, with pixel values normalized to [0,1]. Augmentation includes speed perturbation, frame-by-frame rotation, horizontal flipping, frame-level cropping, color jittering, grayscale conversion, and random histogram equalization.

Model training configuration: 6 blocks in the encoder (N=6), 4 attention heads per block (h=4), hidden dimension 256 (D=256), feed-forward layer 1024 dimensions; batch size 48; Adam optimizer, learning rate 0.001, warmup for the first 10,000 steps; weighted BCE (negative:positive = 1:5); multimodal fine-tuning after pre-training uses a learning rate of 0.0001.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric system follows the norms of the challenge organizing committee: FRR (proportion of wake words not detected) and FAR (proportion of non-wake words falsely reported as positive) are defined by Eq. 2, and the WWS score is defined as the sum of the two (Eq. 3). The choice of summation rather than reporting a single error rate separately is because wake word systems are always listening; missed detections harm interaction usability, while false alarms harm user trust. Neither can be neglected, and the sum score forces the system to strike a balance between the two types of errors. The authors additionally supplemented AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve) on this basis. The reason is stated honestly in the ablation analysis: the WWS score depends on the choice of a hard threshold, and the threshold can easily affect the score, whereas AUC is a threshold-free classification capability metric and can serve as another reference. It is worth noting the presentation technique in Table 1: the AUC of all systems is squeezed into the 99.x% range. The authors use 99.0 as the zero-point baseline for display (e.g., 0.636 represents 99.636%), otherwise the differences are invisible in the four-digit decimals.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

A comprehensive comparison with historical best audio-visual systems is shown in Table 3. The official baseline (Zhou et al.) had a WWS as high as 25.1% on the Eval set; Xu et al.'s end-to-end Transformer system reduced it to 9.1%; Cheng et al. was 7.1%; the MISP 2021 first-place system was 5.8%; Zhang et al. (VE-KWS) was 5.74% on the Eval set (3.62% on the Dev set); and Wang et al. was 5.59%. The FLCMA-Conformer with pre-training in this paper finally achieved 4.57% on the Eval set and 3.86% on the Dev set, refreshing the best audio-visual results at that time. Particularly noteworthy is the cross-relationship with Zhang et al.: the latter was better on the Dev set (3.62% vs. 3.86% for this paper), but this paper significantly surpassed them on the Eval set (4.57% vs. 5.74%)—the authors used this to argue that this model did not overfit on the Dev set and has better generalization. Since the Eval set consists entirely of far-field data, this reversal is particularly significant: it indicates that the benefits brought by frame-level cross-modal interaction are exactly realized in scenarios with the worst acoustic conditions, which is precisely the original intention of introducing the visual modality.

The comparison with single-modal systems (Table 2) is also informative. Pure visual systems are generally much weaker than pure audio systems: the visual LSTM (official baseline) had a WWS as high as 58.4% on the Eval set, and the best visual system (V-SimAM) still required 19.13%; while the best audio-side system (A-ResNet3D, Zhang et al.) reached 9.78% on the Eval set. This set of numbers answers a basic question: visual systems cannot stand alone; their value can only be realized in cross-modal complementarity—the multimodal system's score of 4.57% is significantly lower than the best scores of the two single-modal systems, verifying the positioning of "complementarity" rather than "substitution." At the same time, this paper's own single-modal models (V-Conformer Eval 21.30%, A-Conformer Eval 11.33%) did not achieve the single-modal optimum, slightly inferior to the single-modal systems of Zhang et al. and Wang et al., but as shown in the ablation studies below, this does not prevent them from playing a valuable role as pre-training starting points.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The ablation experiments (Table 1) revolve around three variables: fusion paradigm (early fusion E / late fusion L / FLCMA), backbone (Transformer / Conformer), and whether pre-training is used, yielding four clear conclusions.

First, late fusion is better than early fusion. When trained end-to-end from scratch, AV-Transformer(L) achieved a WWS of 6.67% on the Eval set compared to AV-Transformer(E)'s 8.95%, and AV-Conformer(L) achieved 6.70% compared to (E)'s 7.77%. The authors' explanation is that fusing the deep multimodal latent features output by the encoder is superior to fusing the shallow features output by the frontend—shallow acoustic features and shallow visual features are still in their respective original representation spaces, and forced mixing will only interfere with each other; deep features have already been semantically specialized, so fusion has a common language. This conclusion also explains why FLCMA is placed inside the encoder block rather than at the frontend.

Second, the FLCMA module brings consistent improvements. Inserting FLCMA on the basis of early fusion end-to-end training, the Eval set WWS for AV-Transformer dropped from 8.95% to 7.06% (AUC rose from 99.083% to 99.518%), and for AV-Conformer from 7.77% to 5.50% (AUC rose from 99.231% to 99.541%). The direction is consistent across both backbones and both datasets, indicating that the benefit comes from the module itself rather than a coincidence of the backbone. A more critical comparison is the confrontation with late fusion: the FLCMA version replaces the two sets of single-modal encoders in late fusion with a single multimodal encoder, and the AUC is actually higher by +0.097 (Transformer) / +0.125 (Conformer), proving that frame-level cross-modal interaction has reclaimed modeling capability from the two split encoders. The authors also honestly reported an imperfection: the WWS score of FLCMA-Transformer (7.06%) is slightly inferior to AV-Transformer(L) (6.67%), but the AUC is better—and provided an explanation: the WWS score is easily swayed by threshold selection, while the classification capability reflected by AUC is more credible. This self-disclosure actually enhances the credibility of the entire set of experiments.

Third, the pre-training strategy presses down another level. After adding single-modal pre-training transfer, FLCMA-Transformer reached Dev 4.45% / Eval 5.47%, and FLCMA-Conformer reached Dev 3.86% / Eval 4.57%. Taking Conformer as an example, the drop from 5.50% to 4.57% is a further 17% relative reduction (as stated in the paper). Combined with the fact from Table 2 that the "single-modal starting point is not optimal," we can derive a judgment that is very valuable for engineering practice: the benefit of pre-training transfer does not depend on the absolute strength of the teacher model, but on the initialization liberating optimization from a random starting point.

The fourth point is hidden in the decomposition of FRR/FAR. Observing Table 1, it can be seen that when only FLCMA is added, the system tends to suppress missed detections (e.g., FLCMA-Transformer has an FRR as low as 1.76% on the Dev set, but FAR reaches 4.18%), while after adding pre-training, the two tend to balance (FRR 2.24%, FAR 2.21%)—FLCMA corrects the conservative tendency of "afraid to miss when it can't hear clearly" through cross-modal complementarity, and pre-training calibrates the decision boundary as a whole. The two components complement each other in error types (this is an analysis and induction based on the values in Table 1).

Visualization analysis (Fig. 4, Fig. 5) provides evidence for the mechanism: attention rollout shows that the model concentrates attention on four moments corresponding to the wake word syllables at 0.25/1.0/1.5/2.3 seconds; the attention map of the FLCMA module in the last Conformer block shows that the same modality receives different weights on different frames, with the model customizing trust allocation frame-by-frame, and pulling information from the other modality when one modality loses confidence.

## Main Contributions

The contributions of this paper can be compressed into three translatable judgments. First, the FLCMA module was proposed, advancing multimodal fusion from sentence-level addition/concatenation to frame-level cross-modal attention: by modeling inter-modal semantic correlation on every frame with synchronized lip motion and speech signals, making "who to trust in this frame" a learnable, frame-by-frame variable decision, rather than a static weight. Second, a complete end-to-end FLCMA audio-visual Transformer/Conformer system was constructed: a single multimodal encoder, convolutional fusion module, attention pooling, weighted BCE, and full-link joint training, breaking away from the old paradigms of "freezing single-modal + fusion" and "multi-model voting," and proving that this approach is more economical in parameter organization (single encoder replacing dual encoders) and performs better. Third, the effectiveness of transferring single-modal pre-training to multimodal models was verified, and empirical evidence was provided that "the pre-training starting point does not need to be the optimal single-modal system"; the combination of the three achieved a WWS score of 4.57% on the MISP far-field dataset Eval set, refreshing the best result at that time.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

There is a deployment-level hard constraint in the inference chain: the model structurally requires both audio and visual inputs to be present simultaneously. The frame-level cross-modal attention does not design a degradation path for modality missing scenarios such as "camera off, face out of frame, lip region occluded," and the paper does not report any robustness experiments under modality missing. However, real speech devices often operate in states where no video is available, which is a more realistic barrier to deployment than accuracy. Secondly, the engineering cost of visual preprocessing: the entire pipeline of RetinaFace detection, ArcFace recognition, keypoint localization, and lip region cropping is not lightweight on multi-person far-field videos. The paper does not report any complexity metrics such as parameter count, computational load, or inference latency (not reported in the paper), making it impossible to evaluate its feasibility in edge-side wake word scenarios (always-on listening, milliwatt-level power budget). Third, the system performs offline segment classification based on a fixed 2.56-second window, whereas wake word detection is essentially an always-on streaming task. The compatibility of FLCMA with streaming decoding (causal attention transformation, frame-by-frame output) is not covered in the paper. Fourth, looking at Table 3, the FLCMA-Transformer without pre-training (Eval 7.06%) still lags behind existing systems, indicating that the benefits of the method highly depend on the加持 (boost) of pre-training, and the absolute level of end-to-end training from scratch still has room for improvement.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

All experiments were conducted only on one dataset from MISP 2021, one wake word ("Xiao T, Xiao T"), and one language. The generalization ability across datasets, keywords, and languages was not verified—whether frame-level cross-modal attention is equally effective for longer wake words or languages with denser syllables remains unknown. Secondly, the authors themselves admitted that the WWS score is sensitive to thresholds, but the paper did not report how the threshold was selected or whether it was tuned on the Dev set and then fixed for the Eval. This creates a risk of misalignment in operational points when directly comparing WWS scores between systems (the AUC partially compensates for this). Third, in the comparison with Zhang et al. and Wang et al., the data augmentation pipelines of each system (simulated far-field, noise addition strategies) were not completely consistent. The benefits brought by end-to-end joint training and those brought by stronger data engineering were not fully decoupled. Fourth, all results are from single runs. The paper does not report the variance of multiple runs or significance tests; it is impossible to judge from the paper whether the performance difference between Dev and Eval (e.g., 3.86% vs. 4.57%) exceeds random fluctuation.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the technical logic of this paper itself, there are several natural extensions. The pre-training link can be upgraded to large-scale self-supervised audio-visual pre-training: HuBERT-style methods cited in the paper have already proven the value of unlabeled data. Replacing single-modal supervised pre-training with audio-visual self-supervised pre-training (or stacking both) is expected to further raise the starting point. The robustness link can introduce modality dropout training, randomly masking one modality during training to force the model to learn graceful degradation when a single modality is missing, directly responding to the aforementioned deployment constraints. The efficiency link includes: replacing the RetinaFace/ArcFace suite with a more lightweight lip region extraction pipeline, quantizing and compressing the model to meet the power budget for edge-side always-on listening, and transforming FLCMA into causal attention to support streaming frame-by-frame output. The mechanism link can perform deeper modality reliability analysis: the attention map in Fig. 5 implies that the model is already implicitly estimating "which modality is more trustworthy at this moment." Making this implicit estimate explicit (e.g., correlating it with SNR or lip occlusion degree) can not only enhance interpretability but also guide the design of adaptive weighting strategies. Finally, re-verifying on data from subsequent years of MISP and with more keywords is an inevitable step to push this frame-level cross-modal route from "optimal on one dataset" to "a general solution for a class of problems."
