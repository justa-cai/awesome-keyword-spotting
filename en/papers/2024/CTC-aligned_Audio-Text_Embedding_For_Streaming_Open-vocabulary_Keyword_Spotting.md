# CTC-aligned Audio-Text Embedding for Streaming Open-vocabulary Keyword Spotting

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Sichen Jin, Youngmoon Jung, Seungjin Lee, Jaeyoung Roh, Changwoo Han, Hoonyoung Cho (Samsung Research, South Korea)
- **Date**: June 12, 2024 (arXiv:2406.07923v1 [cs.SD])
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07923
- **Keywords**: streaming keyword spotting, open-vocabulary KWS, audio-text embedding, CTC alignment, text-based enrollment, metric learning, edge-side lightweight

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the primary user interface for voice assistants: devices must continuously listen for wake-word triggers to enable hands-free operation. The paper explicitly states the engineering constraints of this scenario in the introduction: as the frontline interface for voice assistants, the computational load of KWS must be extremely small, and it can only use the minimal amount of past information, as it must remain active around the clock. This statement forms the foundational argument of the entire paper: any new capability introduced for open-vocabulary KWS must be achieved without violating the three bottom lines of "always-on, low compute, and causal (not relying on future frames)."

The evolution from fixed-vocabulary to open-vocabulary systems is another main thread in this field. Fixed-vocabulary systems (such as classic HMM, CNN, and small DNN architectures) require training with large amounts of audio data for the same keywords; changing a wake-word necessitates retraining and redeployment. Open-vocabulary systems allow users to customize keywords through a single "enrollment" action, without retraining the model itself. The paper points out that open-vocabulary KWS is difficult because it requires the system to maintain sufficient flexibility and robustness for unseen keywords—words not seen during training must be recognized and judged on the fly during inference.

There are two routes for enrollment. The first is Query-by-Example (QbyE): the user provides several utterances of the keyword, and the system aligns frame-level acoustic embeddings using DTW or attention mechanisms to compare the similarity between the enrollment audio and the query audio. The second is text-based enrollment: the user directly inputs the text of the keyword, and the system maps the text and speech into the same latent space for matching. The paper explicitly aligns with text-based enrollment for two reasons: first, user experience; typing to enroll is much more natural than reading the keyword aloud to the device multiple times; second, QbyE relies solely on acoustic features, resulting in weak generalization across environmental changes and different speaker voices, whereas text as an anchor is naturally unaffected by speaker timbre.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper’s criticism of existing text-based enrollment routes focuses on the "non-streaming" aspect. Existing approaches that map text and speech to a shared latent space mainly fall into two categories: cross-attention-based methods (Reference [7], i.e., the original LibriPhrase paper, Interspeech 2022) and dynamic programming-based alignment methods (References [9][10], such as DSP, Dynamic Sequence Partitioning, Interspeech 2023). The paper’s evaluation is that while these methods perform well, they often require computation over global context, making them difficult to run in a streaming manner. Specifically, cross-attention requires computing the full attention matrix for the entire audio segment and the entire text token sequence; attention weights depend on each other at both ends of the sequence, so the output of any frame requires seeing the entire input. Similarly, dynamic programming-based sequence partitioning requires holding the entire latent encoded sequence to perform global segmentation. The common problem with these two types of methods conflicts directly with the constraints of always-on KWS listening—devices cannot cache and repeatedly process infinite-length global context just to keep the wake-word active.

Another baseline implicitly criticized by the paper is pure CTC-based streaming KWS (Reference [14]): CTC outputs phoneme hypotheses frame-by-frame, which is naturally suitable for streaming, but it only provides local frame-level evidence, lacking global acoustic-text consistency verification for the entire keyword, resulting in weak false alarm control. This point is supported by data in the paper’s own ablation study: a model trained using only CTC loss (147K parameters) achieved an EER of 9.11% (LPE) and 32.37% (LPH) on the LibriPhrase evaluation set (Table 1), which is the worst result among the four rows in the paper, indicating that "looking only at local phoneme evidence" is indeed insufficient.

Furthermore, the parameter count of non-streaming methods is also a pain point: DSP uses 3.7M parameters, and attention-based methods use approximately 420K (Table 1), neither of which is friendly for always-on edge-side loads.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, this paper aims to simultaneously satisfy three mutually constraining conditions: First, the enrollment method must be text-based, enjoying the user experience and speaker-independence of text enrollment; Second, the matching process must be completed under streaming conditions, meaning that when each audio frame arrives, all states required for the decision can be updated without looking back at historical frames or waiting for future frames; Third, the compute budget must be compressed to the level of small-footprint KWS. The paper’s answer is: let CTC be responsible for extracting information instantly from each audio frame (alignment), let embeddings be responsible for comparing global information of the entire keyword (matching), and couple them frame-by-frame via dynamic programming during inference. According to the authors, this is the first KWS attempt to dynamically align audio and keyword text during inference, thereby obtaining joint audio-text embeddings. In terms of numbers, the goal is to achieve performance comparable to non-streaming methods using an acoustic encoder with 155K parameters, a computational load of 6.91M FLOPs per time step, and an inference algorithm with decoding complexity O(U) (where U is the keyword length).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system, called the CTCAT (CTC-aligned Audio-Text) keyword detector, consists of three parts (Fig. 1): a text encoder, an acoustic encoder, and a CTC aligner.

The text encoder first splits the keyword text into character-level tokens (the basic units required for CTC training), converts them into 256-dimensional vectors via a trainable lookup table, and then passes them through two layers of bidirectional LSTM to obtain token-level text embeddings. Why is it safe to use bidirectional processing on the text side: the text sequence is extremely short (keywords are only a few characters), and encoding occurs at the enrollment moment, which is a one-time offline calculation, unconstrained by streaming requirements.

The acoustic encoder runs in streaming mode, encoding the input audio stream frame-by-frame, and outputs to two heads: the CTC projection block provides the token distribution P(y|x_t) for each frame, and the AE projection block provides the acoustic embedding for each frame. To keep the model small, the paper stacks MobileNet blocks (depthwise separable convolutions), totaling 12 convolutional blocks, with a depthwise kernel width of 12 and 96 pointwise convolution feature maps. The entire acoustic encoder has only 155K parameters and 6.91M FLOPs per time step (Section 4). Both projection blocks consist of a single fully connected layer plus batch normalization. Why decouple the CTC head and AE head into two projections rather than sharing one output: the two losses require representations in different directions—the CTC loss requires frame-level outputs to be discriminative token posterior distributions, while the multi-view loss requires frame-level embeddings to fall into a metric space comparable to text embeddings; decoupling allows the two supervisions to shape their respective heads independently while sharing the underlying acoustic features.

The CTC aligner is the heart of the paper: it maintains a decoding graph constructed for the current keyword, updating the cumulative score, transition time, and cumulative acoustic embedding for each state on the graph frame-by-frame using Viterbi-style dynamic programming, thereby holding all information of the "optimal alignment path ending at t" at each moment t.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

First, let's look at the mathematical framework of CTC. Let the original vocabulary be V, and after adding the blank symbol, V' = V ∪ {Blank}. The network outputs y_t^k at each moment t, interpreted as the probability of observing label k at time t. The probability of a path π ∈ V'^T of length T is the product of token probabilities (Equation 1). Define the many-to-one mapping B, which removes blanks and adjacent duplicate labels in the path; the probability of the target label sequence l is the sum of probabilities of all paths that can map to it (Equation 2), and the loss takes the negative log (Equation 3), calculated efficiently using the forward-backward algorithm.

Next, on the metric learning side. The paper adopts the Asymmetric Proxy loss (AsyP, referred to as multi-view loss in the paper) proposed by Jung et al., following their notation: a mini-batch contains N triplets (x_i, t_i, c_i), where x_i is the acoustic embedding of the i-th speech segment (anchor), t_i is the corresponding text embedding (acting as proxy), P_i and N_i are the index sets for positive and negative examples, respectively, and S(·,·) is cosine similarity. The loss is the sum of the anchor-positive term and the anchor-negative term (Equation 4): The AP term uses the Extended-LogSumExp function on a set of α(λ − S(t_i, a_j)) to pull the anchor and positive examples closer, and the AN term uses the Mean-Softplus function on β(S(a_i, t_k) − λ) to push the anchor and negative examples apart; α, β, and λ are hyperparameters controlling the embedding space boundaries and penalty strength. Note that here the text embedding participates in the loss as a proxy, which is the meaning of "asymmetric"—the roles of the two views are unequal.

Then comes the aligner. Construct a decoding graph for the keyword token sequence y = {y_u | 1 ≤ u ≤ U}: the state sequence {s_l | 1 ≤ l ≤ L} corresponds to "y1, blank, y2, blank, …, blank, yU", with a total of L = 2U − 1 states; odd-numbered states correspond to real tokens, and even-numbered states correspond to blanks. Each state stores three types of information: cumulative CTC score z_CTC^{l,t}, transition time t_{s_l}^t (the moment the non-blank token associated with this state was last visited), and cumulative acoustic embedding h_{s_l}^{l,t}.

Regarding initialization, the first state s_1 is independent of other states, and its score directly takes the probability of emitting y1 from the current frame, with the transition time recorded as the current frame (Equation 5). The selection of transition sources (Equation 6) is divided into two cases based on state parity: blank states inherit the highest score from the two upstream states {l, l−1}, while non-blank states choose from three states {l, l−1, l−2}. The reason why upstream scores can be compared directly without comparing transition probabilities one by one is that the transition probabilities into a certain state are all defined as the emission probability of the token of that state at the current moment; the differences between source states are fully reflected in their respective cumulative scores. Score updates are simple logarithmic probability accumulations (Equation 7): z_CTC^{l,t} = z_CTC^{I,t−1} + log P(s_l | x_t), where I is the source state selected in Equation 6, and the transition time and cumulative embedding are inherited from the source state.

Token-level acoustic embeddings corresponding to non-blank states are pooled according to Equation 8: on the optimal path, the embedding of token y_u equals the average of all frame-level embeddings within its transition time interval. The final score is a linear combination of two channels: first, calculate the average cosine similarity between acoustic embeddings and text embeddings for all tokens according to Equation 10 to obtain z_embed, then fuse z = z_CTC + λ·z_embed according to Equation 11, where λ is a hyperparameter tuned on the development set. The training objective (Equation 9) is the direct sum of CTC loss and multi-view loss: L = L_CTC + L_multi-view.

Regarding complexity, the time complexity of one decoding step is O(U), and the space complexity is O(U²). Since the keyword length is much smaller than the audio length, the paper argues that this algorithm is much more efficient than non-streaming algorithms. If the AE of all frames is stored completely during training, it consumes O(TU²) memory; therefore, during training, the storage of AE is skipped, and only the CTC score and transition time of the final state at each time step are saved; after alignment, the path corresponding to t_optimal, where the CTC score is highest among all frames, is selected for embedding pooling and loss calculation.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Streaming Decoding Graph for Keyword Segments

Standard CTC forward-backward alignment aligns the entire input sequence with the entire annotation sequence, where both the start and end points are concepts of "absorbing" the entire sequence. This decoding graph makes two key modifications. First, both the beginning and end of the graph are non-blank tokens (starting with y1 and ending with yU), because the system is only concerned with the small segment of audio where the keyword is pronounced, not aligning the entire input with the keyword—the keyword may appear anywhere in the stream, and the graph only needs to depict the structure of "y1 followed by any length of blanks (pronunciation pauses or continuations), then y2 appears... until yU finishes." Second, the "optimal path ending at the current frame" is recalculated at each moment, with all historical information compressed into the dynamic programming states; inference does not require revisiting the calculation results of any past time steps—this is the fundamental reason it can stream, and also the most essential difference between this paper and attention-based and dynamic programming-based methods: the global computation of those methods requires the entire sequence to be on hand at once, whereas the globality here is amortized into frame-by-frame state updates.

A supporting detail design is the semantic redefinition of blanks: blanks immediately following a non-blank token are considered continuations of that token's pronunciation, so the frame-level acoustic embeddings of these blank frames are accumulated into the cumulative embedding of the leading non-blank token, rather than discarded. Why handle it this way: a character often spans multiple frames in actual pronunciation (the paper's Table 2 shows that on LibriPhrase, each character corresponds to an average of 5.6 frame embeddings); if only the frame where the token first appears is taken, the embedding only reflects the initial moment of pronunciation, losing the acoustic information of the latter half; merging subsequent blank frames allows the token-level embedding to cover the complete pronunciation segment.

There is also a padding strategy during training: padding tokens are added to the beginning and end of the keyword token sequence to train the CTC aligner. The paper gives two reasons: experiments found that this allows the model to converge with lower CTC loss; the extra padding at the end ensures that each non-blank token can accumulate corresponding frame-level embeddings without missing frames (without tail padding, there are no blank frames after the last token for accumulation, and the pooling interval would be incomplete). Note that padding is only used in algorithms related to CTC alignment; when comparing acoustic embeddings with text embeddings, only non-blank keyword tokens are considered—the two purposes are strictly separated to avoid padding contaminating the metric space.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Alignment-Driven Hierarchical Embedding Aggregation

The pooling in Equation 8 can naturally be generalized to different granularities. When characters are the basic tokens, character-level embeddings are obtained; word-level embeddings continue to accumulate across characters on top of character accumulation until a space character is encountered; phrase-level embeddings pool all embeddings of the entire keyword into a single vector. The motivation for this design is to treat "the size of the aggregation window" as a control variable for "how much global context is included"—character-level looks at only about 5.6 frames of local context, word-level averages 31.7 frames, and phrase-level averages 49.2 frames (Table 2). The intuition is: the larger the aggregation window, the more context the embedding carries, and the more separated it is from embeddings of other keywords in the latent space. This intuition is fully verified by ablation experiments (see Experimental Results section), and is also the core argument the paper uses to explain performance gains: considering more global context helps embedding vectors separate better in the latent space.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: End-to-End Joint Training of Pre-training-Free Aligner

The entire model is trained end-to-end from scratch, without requiring a pre-trained aligner; CTC alignment and the joint embedding space are learned simultaneously (Equation 9). Why this is feasible and worth emphasizing: previous audio-text KWS workflows usually relied on external aligners or attention to produce paired units, and pairing quality was constrained by the upstream; here, the CTC loss shapes the token posterior of the acoustic encoder frame-by-frame (providing alignment capability), and the gradients of the multi-view loss backpropagate from the pooled token-level acoustic embeddings to frame-level embeddings (shaping the metric space), with the two supervisions cooperating on the shared acoustic encoder. During training, the full sequence is fed in at once (unlike streaming inference), but by only storing the final state scores and times, and taking t_optimal afterwards, the memory is compressed from O(TU²) to an acceptable range.

To verify that the two supervisions do not conflict, the paper provides visualization (Fig. 3): for the word-level embedding model, draw an audio-text embedding correlation graph for the positive example of the target keyword "said the king," with blue lines marking the CTC-aligned segments ⟨silence⟩, "said", "the", "king", ⟨silence⟩. The observation is that the correlation within the alignment path is higher, indicating that CTC scores and embedding scores can work coherently without interfering with each other—this is direct evidence of joint training at the representation level.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Dual-Score Fusion of Local and Global Evidence

During inference, each frame only needs to retrieve the acoustic embedding from the last state of the updated decoding graph, calculate the cosine similarity with the target keyword text embedding (Equation 10), and then linearly fuse it with the CTC cumulative score (Equation 11). Why retain the CTC score in addition to the embedding score: the CTC score is the logarithmic accumulation of frame-by-frame token evidence, reflecting the local sequence evidence of "whether each character is really pronounced in order"; embedding similarity reflects the acoustic-text consistency at the whole-word level. The two types of evidence are complementary—the EER of LPE drops from 9.11% to 6.06% when moving from pure CTC to adding phrase-level embeddings in Table 1, and the gain comes from fusion. The fusion coefficient λ is tuned on 100,000 samples randomly held out from the training set, with the final result using λ = 6 (Section 4).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to cross-attention methods (Reference [7]): attention requires global interaction between the entire audio segment and text, which is non-streaming; this paper uses online Viterbi to amortize alignment into O(U) state updates per frame, with history compressed entirely into DP states, without revisiting past frames. In terms of parameters, this paper uses 155K compared to approximately 420K. Compared to DSP (Reference [9]): DSP performs global latent encoding matching, uses 3.7M parameters, and requires a complete sequence; this paper surpasses it on the subset where it excels with approximately one-twenty-fourth of the parameters (Table 1). Compared to pure CTC streaming KWS (Reference [14]): those methods stop at frame-level phoneme hypotheses, while this paper adds embedding comparison across the entire keyword on top of alignment, making up for the global verification part. Compared to the QbyE route (DTW or attention aligning audio with audio): enrollment requires no audio, only text, with near-zero enrollment cost, and the matching anchor is the text embedding rather than the enroller's voice, with cross-speaker generalization handled by the text.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The evaluation uses the LibriPhrase dataset (proposed in Reference [7]), chosen for fair comparability with existing methods. The training set is generated from train-clean-100 and train-clean-360, with keyword phrase lengths of one to four words. To enhance robustness, clean speech is convolved with synthetic room impulse responses from OpenSLR, and MUSAN noise is added, with signal-to-noise ratios randomly selected between −3 and 25 dB (Section 4). The evaluation set is generated from train-others-500, with negative examples divided into simple (LPE) and hard (LPH) subsets based on edit distance (Levenshtein distance). The evaluation data contains 4391, 2605, 467, and 56 episodes, corresponding to the four lengths of one to four words for the anchor phrase; each episode contains 3 positive pairs and 3 negative pairs; the text of the anchor phrase is used for text enrollment, and the audio of the comparison samples is used for detection evaluation (Section 4). λ in Equation 11 is determined on 100,000 samples randomly held out from the training set, with the final result using λ = 6.

Training configuration: 80-channel filter bank features, window length 25 ms, frame shift 10 ms; the text encoder uses 28 tokens (English letters, spaces, apostrophes), plus two tokens for padding and blank for the aligner; the lookup table and BLSTM hidden layers are both 256-dimensional; multi-view loss hyperparameters α = 2, β = 50, λ = 0.1 (note that this λ is the embedding space boundary, not the same quantity as the fusion coefficient λ = 6 in Equation 11); the mini-batch contains 1024 keywords, with each keyword having two samples forming a positive pair, and samples from all other keywords serving as negatives; Adam optimizer with cosine annealing, trained for 100 epochs, taking two days on two A100s (Section 4). The acoustic encoder has 12 convolutional blocks, depthwise convolution kernel 12, pointwise convolution 96 channels, 155K parameters, and 6.91M FLOPs per time step. The specific dimension of the AE embedding is not reported in the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metrics are EER (Equal Error Rate, the error rate where the false alarm rate and miss rate are equal) and AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve), reported separately for the LPE and LPH subsets (Table 1). The rationale for selection: open-vocabulary KWS under this protocol is a verification-style binary decision task (comparing enrollment text with comparison audio, judging same class or different class); EER eliminates the influence of operating threshold selection, and AUC reflects average discriminative power across the full threshold range; both metrics do not depend on a specific operating point, making them suitable for threshold-independent horizontal comparisons between different methods, and they are also the standards established in the original LibriPhrase paper, so following them ensures comparability with baselines. Deployment-side metrics (recall at specific false wake-up rates, false wake-ups per hour, trigger delay) are not reported in the paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

The comparison objects are two non-streaming open-vocabulary systems: a method using cross-attention (Reference [7], approximately 420K parameters) and DSP using dynamic sequence partitioning (Reference [9], 3.7M parameters). The complete numbers in Table 1 (EER% / AUC%, two columns for LPE and LPH):

- Attention (approx. 420K parameters): EER 8.24 / 32.90, AUC 96.70 / 73.58;
- DSP (3.7M parameters): EER 7.36 / 23.36, AUC 97.83 / 84.21;
- This paper's pure CTC (147K parameters): EER 9.11 / 32.37, AUC 96.76 / 73.95;
- This paper with character-level embedding (155K parameters): EER 8.65 / 32.76, AUC 96.99 / 73.53;
- This paper with word-level embedding (155K parameters): EER 7.05 / 31.62, AUC 97.97 / 75.12;
- This paper with phrase-level embedding (155K parameters): EER 6.06 / 29.63, AUC 98.32 / 77.10.

Three core conclusions. First, on the simple negative subset LPE, the EER of 6.06% for this paper's phrase-level model is significantly better than the two non-streaming baselines (DSP 7.36%, Attention 8.24%), and the AUC of 98.32% is also the highest in the entire table—meaning that a system with less than one-twenty-fourth the parameters of DSP (155K vs. 3.7M), less than two-thirds the parameters of the attention method, and completely streaming, surpasses non-streaming methods on simple negatives. Second, on the hard negative subset LPH, the phrase-level model's EER of 29.63% still lags behind DSP's 23.36% (a difference of 6.27 percentage points), but is 3.27 percentage points better than Attention's 32.90%, which the paper describes as "competitive results." Why it cannot beat DSP on LPH: hard negatives are phrases with close edit distances and highly similar pronunciations; distinguishing them requires stronger global discriminative capability, and DSP's 3.7M parameter capacity and full-sequence latent encoding matching happen to excel in this interval, while this paper sacrifices model capacity and context length for streaming and lightweight design. Third, the cost of parameters and computation is extremely low: from pure CTC to adding the embedding head, parameters increase by only 8K (147K to 155K), bringing an improvement in LPE EER from 9.11% to 6.06%, with very high cost-performance ratio; inference is 6.91M FLOPs per time step, with decoding complexity O(U) (Section 4), both within the always-on edge-side budget. The paper does not report actual measured latency and memory usage on devices.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper trained four models from scratch separately: one using only CTC loss, and three variants adding character-level, word-level, and phrase-level joint embeddings respectively (Table 1). The patterns revealed by the ablation are more informative than the total scores:

Finding 1: Adding embedding scores on top of CTC scores is generally effective. The EER of pure CTC is 9.11% (LPE) and 32.37% (LPH); adding any level of embedding brings improvement on LPE, with phrase-level compressing LPE EER to 6.06% and raising AUC to 98.32%. This verifies the core hypothesis: global embedding evidence and local frame-level evidence are complementary.

Finding 2: Aggregation granularity is not finer the better; there is an intuitive local degradation. Character-level embedding improves on LPE (from 9.11% to 8.65%) but slightly worsens on LPH (from 32.37% to 32.76%), and AUC also drops slightly from 73.95% to 73.53%. Why: character-level embedding averages only 5.6 frames of acoustic information (Table 2), with too little context, resulting in limited discriminative power of the pooled vector, which may even inject noise on hard negatives; whereas word-level (average 31.7 frames) and phrase-level (average 49.2 frames) aggregation bring significant improvements. The conclusion drawn by the paper is: considering more global context helps embedding vectors separate better in the latent space. The aggregated frame counts in Table 2 and the performance ranking in Table 1 are completely in the same direction—5.6, 31.7, 49.2 frames correspond to LPH EER 32.76%, 31.62%, 29.63%—granularity is context amount, context amount is discriminative power, and this chain of evidence is the most solid finding in the paper's ablation section.

Finding 3: Visualization at the representation level corroborates the compatibility of joint training. Fig. 3 draws an audio-text embedding correlation graph for the word-level model and the target keyword "said the king," with the CTC-aligned segments (silence, three words, silence) showing higher internal correlation than inter-segment correlation, indicating that the alignment path and embedding space are consistent, and the two scoring systems of CTC scores and embedding scores do not undermine each other. This also explains from the side why end-to-end joint training (rather than training the aligner first and then the embeddings) is feasible.

The paper does not report ablation variance and significance (each configuration was trained only once), nor does it report sensitivity curves for different λ fusion coefficients.

## Main Contributions

First, methodological contribution: proposes the first streaming KWS scheme that dynamically aligns the audio stream with keyword text during inference, thereby obtaining joint audio-text embeddings online (claimed novelty by the authors). Before this, KWS with joint audio-text embeddings required global computation like attention or offline dynamic programming; this paper makes the "alignment" itself a streaming dynamic programming process, eliminating the biggest obstacle to the always-on deployment of open-vocabulary KWS.

Second, architectural contribution: provides a design paradigm of complementary CTC and embedding dual channels—CTC is responsible for instant information retrieval frame-by-frame (where to align), and embeddings are responsible for global information comparison of the entire keyword (how similar overall); the two projection heads share the same 155K parameter streaming acoustic encoder, trained end-to-end jointly, without requiring a pre-trained aligner.

Third, algorithmic contribution: a decoding graph algorithm with the current frame as the endpoint, with non-blank tokens at both ends, updating O(U) per time step and O(U²) space, with states carrying three types of information simultaneously: cumulative score, transition time, and cumulative embedding; combined with the memory strategy of storing only the final state during training and taking t_optimal afterwards, compressing training costs to a feasible level.

Fourth, empirical contribution: on LibriPhrase, with a budget of 155K parameters and 6.91M FLOPs per time step, surpasses non-streaming baselines on the LPE subset (EER 6.06% vs. DSP 7.36% and Attention 8.24%, Table 1), and establishes the evidence chain of "aggregation granularity is global context" through character, word, and phrase three-level ablation (Table 1, Table 2, Fig. 3).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, hard negatives remain a significant shortcoming. The EER gap with DSP on LPH is 6.27 percentage points (29.63% vs. 23.36%, Table 1), and the reverse degradation of character-level embeddings on LPH (from 32.37% to 32.76%) indicates that for negatives with highly similar pronunciations, neither frame-level nor embedding evidence is strong enough for the lightweight encoder. There is an unfilled gap between lightweight design (155K) and discriminative power for hard negatives.

Second, the dual-score fusion is an unnormalized linear combination. z_CTC is the accumulation of logarithmic probabilities, whose dimension grows unbounded with keyword length U and pronunciation duration; z_embed is a bounded mean of cosine similarities (approximately between −1 and 1). Adding them directly (Equation 11) means that for keywords of different lengths and pronunciations of different durations, the relative weights of the two evidence paths are naturally different; whether the coefficient λ = 6, determined on 100,000 held-out samples, can remain stable across keyword lengths and deployment scenarios is not analyzed by the paper, and the score distribution itself is not reported.

Third, the complete chain of streaming triggering is not formalized. The paper explains frame-by-frame updates and scoring, but the detection declaration strategy—how to determine triggering after z exceeds the threshold, how to perform endpointing, how to deduplicate in continuous streams (multiple consecutive frames exceeding the threshold for the same pronunciation should not trigger multiple wake-ups)—is not detailed, and the working threshold is not reported. For a system that markets "always-on streaming" as a selling point, this is a missing link from algorithm to product.

Fourth, the space complexity O(U²) grows quadratically with keyword length. Although completely manageable for one to four-word keywords, the storage method of cumulative embeddings in states raises doubts about scalability for longer custom phrases (or full-sentence commands).

Fifth, the vocabulary is closed to 28 English characters. There is no discussion on the robustness of character-level tokens to spelling variations, abbreviations, case sensitivity, and non-Latin alphabets, nor are alternative schemes like phonemes or subword units compared. The AE embedding dimension is not reported.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

First, the evaluation is entirely built on LibriPhrase and its simulated enhancements: both training and evaluation use read speech with synthetic reverberation and MUSAN noise (SNR −3 to 25 dB), with no testing on real far-field, real devices, or real user inputs (typos, colloquial spellings). Second, there is no comparison with any QbyE methods—the advantages of text enrollment over audio enrollment (speaker robustness, enrollment experience) remain at the level of motivational discussion, without experimental quantification. Third, there is no on-device measured data: RTF, actual delay (time from end of keyword pronunciation to trigger), and device memory usage are all not reported in the paper; only theoretical values for FLOPs and complexity are provided, making the chain of persuasion incomplete for a paper主打 lightweight streaming. Fourth, all results are from single training and single evaluation, with no variance and significance testing; it is impossible to judge whether differences of the order of 0.4 percentage points (e.g., character-level 32.76% vs. pure CTC 32.37% on LPH) exceed random fluctuations. Fifth, the parameter counts in Table 1 are labeled as "estimated values"; it is not fully clarified whether text encoder parameters are included during inference, or whether text encoding during enrollment only needs to be executed once; total training cost is disclosed (two A100s for two days), but inference-side details are missing.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the paper's own framework, the most direct upgrade is the token unit: switching from characters to phonemes or Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) subwords can cover multiple languages and spelling variations, and may also improve the insufficient context of character-level; the paper itself has proven that moving aggregation granularity upward yields huge benefits, and subword units are a natural extension of this curve. Second, replace linear fusion with learnable, calibratable fusion (learning weights, normalizing by keyword length, temperature scaling), and supplement trigger thresholds and endpointing strategies, completing the product chain from scoring to wake-up. Third, the acoustic encoder can absorb more efficient architectures from the KWS community (such as TC-ResNet, BC-ResNet) or use distillation from large models, attacking LPH hard negatives while maintaining streaming and lightweight design; the remaining 6 percentage point gap left by the paper should mainly be addressed here. Fourth, expand evaluation dimensions to real devices (measured latency, false wake-ups per hour), real far-field, and real user enrollment text, and supplement comparisons with QbyE and mixed text-audio enrollment—text and audio as two anchors are likely complementary. Fifth, in terms of training strategy, adversarial mining of hard negatives (actively constructing confusing phrases based on edit distance) and multi-granularity joint training (simultaneous supervision at character, word, and phrase levels) are both low-cost directions to try.
