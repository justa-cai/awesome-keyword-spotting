## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the always-on front-end function for all voice-activated devices: smart speakers, wireless earbuds, wearable devices, and IoT nodes must listen for wake words around the clock, waking up downstream high-power modules (ASR, communication, application processors) only upon keyword detection. This positioning imposes three hard constraints on KWS chips: first, the power budget is in the microwatt range—it is the component that consumes power continuously; second, the area must be small—it is usually just a supporting module in the system; third, accuracy cannot collapse—poor wake-up performance or false alarms directly ruin the user experience. The paper points out in the introduction that this field is a hotspot in the integrated circuit design community, with the core challenge being to reduce power consumption while maintaining KWS accuracy.

The paper presents two domain facts that quantify these pain points. First, in reported KWS chips, the weight memory (SRAM) alone accounts for 40% to 60% of the total chip power (Introduction, citations [1][2][6]), which directly limits the scaling of networks—models cannot grow larger because storage power becomes the bottleneck. Second, according to Horowitz’s classic data (citation [9]), reading 6.2 kB of data from off-chip DRAM consumes approximately 1 µJ of energy; in contrast, the entire chip in this paper spends only 36 nJ for one decision, a difference of nearly two orders of magnitude—therefore, weights must be placed on-chip, and the on-chip storage itself must be made energy-efficient. Meanwhile, advances in AI methods have allowed CNNs, RNNs, and attention networks to achieve high accuracy on KWS, but the complexity of these networks brings significant redundant computation and hardware costs at the edge (Abstract and Introduction). The tension between "accuracy dividends" and "power red lines" is the engineering constraint this paper aims to resolve.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper reviews gaps in existing work from three fronts.

The first front is the insufficient exploitation of sparsity. Existing KWS chips mainly reduce redundant computation by mining weight sparsity and activation sparsity in networks: a recurrent network KWS chip using in-memory computing architecture leveraged activation sparsity but achieved only up to 70% activation sparsity on a 7-class GSCD subset (Introduction, citation [3]); a switch-capacitor array KWS chip used CNNs for classification, mining bit-level weight sparsity, and achieved 90.9% accuracy on an 11-class GSCD subset (citation [6]); a KWS SoC running skip-RNN utilized 76% coarse-grained temporal sparsity by skipping entire audio frames, but only reported results on a 7-class GSCD subset (citation [8]). The paper’s key judgment is that no existing work has exploited "the change in neural network activation values" (temporal sparsity in the change of neural network activations) as an additional source of energy efficiency (Introduction). Why is this dimension worth exploring? Because adjacent audio frames are highly correlated: in a segment of audio, the feature vectors of adjacent frames, as well as the hidden states of recurrent networks, have most components that remain unchanged, with only a few neurons changing. Dense RNNs still perform full matrix-vector multiplications and full weight reads for all neurons every frame—the computational redundancy lies not in "what the values are," but in "whether the values have changed." Change-based sparsity is finer-grained than activation sparsity, weight-bit sparsity, or whole-frame skipping, and is orthogonal to the previous ones, theoretically allowing for叠加 (stacking).

The second front is the power and area of the Feature Extractor (FEx). Common approaches include serial FFT-type or time-domain bandpass filter-type features, or simply using a single deep network to perform both feature extraction and classification: a serial FFT chip achieved ultra-low power of 510 nW, but at the cost of 64 ms latency, and was only validated on a 2-class GSCD subset (citation [2]); a 23 µW chip used an analog ring oscillator time-domain FEx, but achieved only ~86% accuracy on the full 12-class GSCD (citation [1]); a fully synthesizable wired logic DNN processor used a 16-layer DNN to integrate feature extraction and classification, achieving only 88% accuracy for 10 keywords (citation [5]). Furthermore, most previous works rely on 16b high-resolution ADCs (citations [2][16][17]), and the power and area of the ADC itself become a system burden.

The third front is the structural problem mentioned earlier: standard SRAMs provided by foundries cannot reduce read/write power at conventional voltages, preventing network scaling.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

Translating the above gaps into three design challenges that must be solved. First, how to correctly implement the "event-driven" ΔRNN algorithm in digital circuits: sparse skipping means the input set for matrix-vector multiplication changes every frame; the hardware must ensure that skipped neurons use old values, updated neurons write back correct new values, and handle data handshakes between the feature extraction and network clock domains—once state consistency is compromised, sparse acceleration becomes an accuracy disaster. Second, how to make the feature extractor more energy-efficient than FFT-MFCC: IIR digital filters require fixed-point quantization; cutting bits too aggressively causes numerical stability issues (the paper explicitly points out the need for fixed-point analysis); a systematic solution must be found between power, area, and accuracy. Third, how to pull the SRAM, which accounts for 40%–60% of power, into the near-threshold region: at 0.6V, SRAM read static noise margin and access time degrade, requiring circuit techniques like level shifting and word-line boosting to ensure reliability, without these overheads eating up the saved energy. The three core modules of the paper—the ΔRNN accelerator, the IIR BPF feature extractor, and the 0.6V near-threshold SRAM—correspond one-to-one with these three challenges.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall architecture (Fig. 1) consists of three main blocks: a serial IIR bandpass filter (BPF) time-domain feature extractor, a ΔRNN accelerator, and a 24 kB near-threshold weight SRAM. Speech samples are quantized to 12b and downsampled to 8 kHz, then sent serially into the chip via the SPI interface (Section II-A). This involves a typical multi-clock-domain design: the SPI interface transmits only 1b per clock cycle, requiring a relatively fast clock; while on-chip filtering and network processing are more energy-efficient at slower clocks. Therefore, the chip uses two clock dividers driven by the FPGA master clock to downclock the IIR (CLKIIR) and RNN (CLKRNN) respectively. The 12b features output by the FEx are sent to the ΔRNN accelerator via an asynchronous FIFO for clock domain crossing. The 24 kB near-threshold SRAM stores all ΔRNN model weights entirely on-chip—the reason is the data cited in the introduction: reading 6.2 kB from off-chip DRAM costs 1 µJ, while the on-chip solution’s access overhead is lower by several orders of magnitude (Section II-A).

The design motivation can be summarized as "one algorithm lever plus two circuit levers": computational redundancy is eliminated using ΔRNN temporal sparsity (Abstract: eliminating unnecessary computations and memory accesses); the power and area of feature extraction are reduced using IIR structure optimization, mixed precision, and channel selection (5.7x power, 4.7x area reduction); storage power is reduced using near-threshold custom SRAM (6.6x read power reduction). The three levers are independent and their benefits are multiplicative, ultimately achieving 0.78 mm² and 36 nJ/decision for the entire chip in 65nm technology (Abstract). It is worth emphasizing that the paper explicitly incorporates the bio-inspired origin of ΔRNN into the methodology (Section II-B): cortical neurons have firing thresholds, their activations are discrete in time, and firing rates have an upper limit—ΔRNN搬 (transfers) this neuromorphic characteristic of "acting only when changes exceed a threshold" into artificial recurrent networks (citations [10]–[14], where the original Delta Networks work was proposed by Neil et al. in 2017, and the DeltaRNN FPGA accelerator was the team’s prior work in 2018). This paper is the first to implement this algorithm line as a KWS-specific integrated circuit.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The mathematical core of Δ encoding is first-order differencing plus threshold gating. Time differences are calculated for input activations and hidden states respectively: Δx = x_t − x_{t−1}, Δh = h_t − h_{t−1} (defined in the ΔEncoder of Fig. 3, Section II-B). Each neuron maintains its own state; only when the absolute value of its difference exceeds the set threshold ΔTH does the neuron update in the current frame; otherwise, the state retains the previous frame’s value—this is equivalent in signal processing terms to using a zero-order hold predictor for each neuron’s state: the predicted value is the previous frame’s value, and only when the prediction error exceeds ΔTH is an "event" emitted and the update executed, with state reconstruction satisfying x_t = x_{t−1} + Δx_t. The event rate is the temporal sparsity, which directly determines the number of skipped multiply-accumulate operations and weight reads, thus directly determining latency and energy. ΔTH is the only accuracy-energy knob: when ΔTH = 0, the network degenerates into a dense GRU (0% sparsity); when ΔTH is raised to 0.2, the measured temporal sparsity reaches 87% (Fig. 12), with accuracy dropping by less than 0.6 percentage points (Section III-A). The cleverness of this design lies in that it does not change the gating mathematics of the GRU itself (the standard structure of update gates and reset gates remains, which is background knowledge); instead, it changes "when calculations are needed"—applying sparsity to the dimension of state changes, with granularity down to individual neurons and individual frames.

Network structure (Fig. 2): 10 input channels enter the ΔGRU layer, with 64 hidden neurons, followed by a fully connected layer outputting 12 GSCD command classes (Silence, Unknown, Down, Go, Left, No, Off, On, Right, Stop, Up, Yes); the paper also reports accuracy for 11 classes excluding Unknown to align with the evaluation convention of reference [6]. Weights are stored at 8b, with two 8b weights packed into the 16b word of the SRAM (Fig. 3 caption), and the 24 kB capacity implies the entire model has approximately 20,000+ 8b weights, which is a typical small-footprint KWS scale—the specific parameter count and number of layers of the model are not reported in the paper.

The mathematical object on the feature extraction side is a fourth-order IIR bandpass filter, implemented as two second-order sections (SOS) cascaded (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). The denominator coefficients a and numerator coefficients b of each second-order section form a standard direct-form structure; the baseline complexity given by the paper is: a conventional implementation of the fourth-order structure requires 10 multipliers and 8 adders (Section II-C). The paper observes that coefficients a and b possess hardware-friendly properties such as symmetry and constant-value representations, so half of the multipliers can be directly replaced by shifters (Section II-C). Meanwhile, the fixed-point implementation of IIR cannot avoid numerical stability issues, so coefficient bit-width cannot be uniformly cut; a fixed-point analysis process with network accuracy as the acceptance criterion is required—this is the starting point for mixed-precision optimization.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: ΔRNN Temporal Sparsity Accelerator

The accelerator microarchitecture (Fig. 3) consists of four components working in synergy: the ΔEncoder calculates the input activation difference Δx and hidden state difference Δh signals, comparing them with ΔTH to filter out "critical neuron updates" exceeding the threshold; each non-zero delta state is broadcast to all ΔFIFOs; 8 multiply-accumulate (MAC) units perform matrix-vector multiplication; the State Assembler is responsible for correct state updates and coordination. Why is a ΔFIFO array needed? Because the arrival pattern of sparse events fluctuates irregularly with audio content, and the number of neurons to be updated in the current frame is dynamic. The role of the FIFO is to queue these "events to be computed" to supply the MAC array, allowing the 8 MACs to drain the queue when events are numerous and finish early when events are scarce—this explains why 87% sparsity yields a 2.4x latency reduction rather than constant latency (Section III-A: latency drops from 16.4 ms to 6.9 ms, Table II). Why use a broadcast structure? Because in matrix-vector multiplication, a change in one input component affects all output rows connected to it. Broadcasting non-zero deltas to respective FIFOs triggers the reading of corresponding weight rows and MAC operations on demand, fundamentally "eliminating unnecessary computations and memory accesses" (Abstract)—skipped neurons neither perform MACs nor trigger weight SRAM reads, so the energy reduction (3.4x) is greater than the latency reduction (2.4x), with the extra part being the saved storage access power. The State Assembler is the gatekeeper of correctness: it ensures that at the end of each frame, each neuron’s state is either the newly calculated value for that frame or the retained old value, ensuring that the recursive dependency of hidden states is not broken by sparse skipping. Measured results (Fig. 12, Table II): at ΔTH=0.2, 87% sparsity, 12-class accuracy 89.5% (drop of less than 0.6% compared to dense mode), energy per decision 36.11 nJ, latency 6.9 ms, total chip power 5.22 µW; the ΔTH=0 dense baseline is 121.2 nJ, 16.4 ms, 7.36 µW.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Serial IIR BPF Digital Feature Extractor

The FEx architecture (Fig. 4) includes four components: a fourth-order IIR BPF formed by cascading two second-order sections; a post-processing unit (implementing envelope detection, per-channel offset and scale adjustment, logarithmic compression, normalization); a register file storing intermediate results and filter coefficients; and a reconfigurable control module selecting the number of processing channels. The input GSCD speech, quantized to 12b and downsampled to 8 kHz, enters the FEx (Section II-C), outputting 12b features. Compared to the FFT-MFCC route, the time-domain IIR does not require hundreds of points of FFT buffering; data storage requires only 200 bytes (Table I), which is the structural source of its area advantage. The paper performs optimizations at three levels.

First is the low-arithmetic-complexity structure (Fig. 5). The baseline fourth-order IIR requires 10 multipliers and 8 adders; deep observation of coefficients a and b reveals hardware-friendly properties such as symmetry and constant-value representations. Utilizing these, half of the multipliers are replaced by shifts (Section II-C). Why is this feasible: multiplying by a constant that can be decomposed into a sum of powers of 2 is equivalent to several shift-add operations, and the coefficients of biquad filter banks naturally exist in this form. This step brings a 1.8x power and 1.8x area reduction (Fig. 7).

Second is reconfigurable channel selection. The FEx supports configurations from 1 to 16 channels. Simulation scans (Fig. 6) show that when the number of channels is reduced to 10 (covering Mel-scale center frequencies from 516 Hz to 4.22 kHz), 12-class GSCD accuracy remains maintained. In the hardware design, choosing 10 channels instead of 16 directly reduces FEx power by 30% (Section II-C). Why dare to cut: channels at the ends of the Mel frequency bands contribute very little to keyword discrimination, belonging to purely power-consuming redundant dimensions; while retaining the reconfigurability of up to 16 channels leaves flexibility for other applications requiring wider frequency ranges—this is a dual design of "task-specific customization + leaving room for expansion."

Third is mixed-precision selection for filter coefficients. Previous works used uniform bit-width for coefficients; the paper leverages the neural network’s resilience to feature extraction errors, using network accuracy itself as the acceptance criterion, and determining coefficients via grid search. The specific process (Section II-C): first, determine the integer bits of a and b respectively using maximum values; then, cut fractional bits from the 16b baseline and re-quantize network accuracy. The search found that a and b have different dynamic ranges across different filters, and the impact of bit-width on accuracy varies—this is evidence of "uniform bit-width waste." Ultimately, 12b/8b (b/a) mixed precision is sufficient to maintain target accuracy, bringing a 2.4x power and 2.6x area reduction (Fig. 7). The three steps combined reduce the entire digital FEx power by 5.7x and area by 4.7x (Fig. 7). Another systematic advantage: due to the time-domain IIR route, the chip only requires 12b quantized audio, whereas most previous works require 16b high-resolution ADCs ([2][16][17]), opening up space for selecting low-power, small-area ADCs (end of Section II-C). Frame shift and frame window are both 16 ms (Table I), finer than the 32 ms frame window in comparative works.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: 0.6V Near-Threshold Custom Weight SRAM

SRAM organization (Fig. 8): 24 kB capacity is divided into 12 banks of 2 kB each; it includes 4 storage BLKs, a 10b address register, a 16b input register, a 0.6V to 0.65V output level shifter (connecting to the ΔRNN accelerator), and a skew-tolerant column MUX. Why does the dual power supply of 0.6V/0.65V appear? The paper frankly states this is purely due to the unidirectional nature of the I/O level shifters used (citation [18]); if the output bypasses the I/O shifter, or if bidirectional level shifters are used (citation [19]), single-supply operation is possible—but this was not done in the chip design phase (Section II-D). There are three key points at the circuit level. First, deploy 6T level shifters aligned with the vertical pitch of the 8T bitcell (pitch-matched), boosting the word lines WL/RWL from 0.6V to 1.2V, gaining faster access time and larger static noise margin (Section II-D). Why boost the word line: near-threshold read operations are most likely to fail due to insufficient word line drive and insufficient bitline swing; boosting the word line voltage far above the memory array power supply is a classic method to ensure read stability. Second, on-chip integration of a voltage booster (VDDH): previous work [20] relied on off-chip above-VDD word line boosters; [21] used a booster but only drove the footer of the read buffer inside the 8T bitcell; the combination of "shifter + booster driving word lines" in this paper is different from both (Section II-D). Third, the bitcell uses high-VTH devices to suppress leakage, and the column MUX uses dynamic NOR logic (citation [22]) plus the paper’s proposed skew-tolerant precharge scheme (PCHCMX), ensuring that output data Q is always refreshed at the clock falling edge (Fig. 13’s SRAM measured waveforms verify this), making the integration of custom storage and synthesized logic easier (Section II-D). Result: the read power of this custom SRAM is 0.93 µW, 6.6 times lower than foundry-provided SRAM, accounting for only 18% of the total chip power (Section II-D); the cost is that the area is 2 times larger than push-rule foundry SRAM. Why accept this area cost: in always-on scenarios, leakage and read energy are continuous costs paid every second, while area is a one-time cost; exchanging 2x area for 6.6x read power reduction is a worthwhile trade-off in the system ledger.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Differences from four categories of related work can be laid out horizontally. First, sparsity granularity difference: in-memory computing KWS uses activation value sparsity (70% upper limit on 7-class tasks, [3]), skip-RNN uses coarse-grained temporal sparsity of frame-level skipping (76% on 7-class tasks, [8]), switch-capacitor CNN uses weight-bit sparsity ([6]); this paper uses neuron-level "state change" sparsity, with granularity between whole frames and individual bits, while maintaining nearly 90% accuracy on the 12-class full-label GSCD—advantageous in both task difficulty and sparsity granularity. Second, feature domain difference: opponents either follow FFT-MFCC ([2][4][16]), analog time-domain ([1][23]), or simply use deep network integration ([5]); this paper follows digital time-domain IIR, combining the synthesizability and portability of digital circuits with low buffering of time-domain processing (200 bytes storage vs. 256/512 bytes for FFT schemes, Table I), while avoiding 16b ADC overhead using 12b ADC. Third, storage route difference: most works directly use foundry SRAM (thus accepting 40%–60% power share), while this paper uses near-threshold custom combined with word-line boosting, compressing storage power share to 18%. Fourth, algorithm-hardware coupling difference: ΔTH is a runtime knob adjustable after training; Fig. 12 provides the entire selectable curve of accuracy-sparsity-energy-latency, unlike pruning, quantization, and other compression methods that are fixed at deployment time.

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

All evaluations are based on the Google Speech Command Dataset (GSCD): the chip is evaluated under a 12-class full-label setting (Silence, Unknown, Down, Go, Left, No, Off, On, Right, Stop, Up, Yes), and also reports 11-class accuracy excluding the Unknown class, following the convention of reference [6] (Fig. 2 caption, Table II). During testing, GSCD audio streams are downsampled to 8 kHz and sent into the chip; the paper states that the impact of downsampling on GSCD accuracy is negligible (Section III). The training/testing split of the dataset, sample counts, and training hyperparameters (epochs, data augmentation, whether quantization-aware training was used) are not reported—this is common practice for chip papers; algorithm details are traced back to the team’s prior work (FEx prior work is ISCAS 2023, citation [15]), which poses a barrier for readers wishing to reproduce.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper uses six dimensions of metrics: 11/12-class classification accuracy; energy per decision (nJ); computational latency (ms); total chip power (µW); temporal sparsity (%); and module-level power/area breakdown (Fig. 10). The rationale can be explained from the evaluation conventions of KWS chips: the hard currency for always-on chips is "energy per decision," which combines power and latency into a single comparable number (self-consistency verification for this paper: 5.22 µW × 6.9 ms ≈ 36 nJ, matching the 36.11 nJ in Table II; at ΔTH=0, 7.36 µW × 16.4 ms ≈ 121 nJ, also matching); latency is listed separately because it determines interaction response speed; temporal sparsity is listed separately to attribute energy gains to the Δ mechanism itself—without this intermediate variable, it is impossible to prove that energy reduction comes from temporal sparsity rather than other circuit modifications. Measurement conditions: ΔRNN operates at a 125 kHz clock (Fig. 12 caption, Table II; the clock column in the FEx row of Table I is 128k); the test board is the Xilinx Zynq 7007S SoC FPGA on the MiniZed development board, responsible for initializing the chip and feeding inputs (Fig. 9, Section III).

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Core measured results (Fig. 12, Table II): the design point is selected at ΔTH=0.2, where temporal sparsity is 87%, 12-class GSCD accuracy is 89.5% (drop of less than 0.6% relative to ΔTH=0 dense operation, Section III-A), computational latency is 6.9 ms (2.4x reduction), energy per decision is 36.11 nJ (3.4x reduction), and total chip power is 5.22 µW; the对照 (control) ΔTH=0 dense baseline is 121.2 nJ, 16.4 ms, 7.36 µW, accuracy 91.1% (11-class)/90.1% (12-class). Fig. 11 uses a 1-second "Yes" sample to demonstrate the physical intuition of the mechanism: relatively quiet frames bring about a 40% latency reduction (compared to active speech frames)—sparsity is adaptive to audio content, automatically saving power during silence, which is the behavioral characteristic of an event-driven architecture. The measured waveforms of the SRAM (Fig. 13) confirm that the skew-tolerant precharge scheme keeps Q data updating near the clock falling edge.

Feature Extractor Comparison (Table I): this paper’s 65nm 12b digital serial IIR FEx area is 0.084 mm², only 1.5 times larger than the 28nm 8b digital serial FFT-MFCC FEx (0.057 mm², Shan ISSCC'20 [2])—note that this is the older 65nm process against 28nm; it also provides 2x feature dimensions (measured 10-dim vs. 8-dim), with power only 1.22 µW; compared to the MFCC FEx at the same 65nm node (Giraldo JSSC'20 [4]), power is reduced by 5.9x, while the latter’s feature accuracy is only 8b, whereas this paper’s is 12b (Section III-B). Input accuracy and feature accuracy of 12b/12b are also the highest among the four schemes in Table I (opponents are 16b/8b, 10b/8b, 16b/8b respectively). Frequency band coverage is 100 Hz–7.9 kHz, frame window 16 ms (opponents are mostly 32 ms). Shan JSSC'23 [16]’s 0.17 µW is more power-efficient, but that is a configuration of 28nm, 8 kHz slow clock, 11-dim features, and frequency band only up to 4 kHz.

Total Chip Comparison (Table II, six chips): compared to Kim ISSCC'22 [23] (same 65nm, same GSCD): the opponent has 23 µW, 285.20 nJ/decision, and only 86.03% accuracy for 12 classes (10 keywords)—this paper achieves power reduced to 5.22 µW (~4.4x) and energy to 36.11 nJ (~7.9x) at the same process, with accuracy 3 to 4 percentage points higher, a complete victory in the same caliber. Compared to Frenkel ISSCC'22 [24] (28nm Spiking RNN processor ReckOn): 42 nJ/decision is at the same level as this paper, but it was only validated on the 2-class (single keyword) task of Spiking Heidelberg Digits, with power 79 µW, an order of magnitude higher. Compared to Seol ISSCC'23 [8] (28nm skip-RNN SoC): 23.68 nJ, 92.8% is better, but the task has only 7 classes (5 keywords), and its 76% sparsity is a coarse-grained scheme of frame-level skipping. Compared to Kosuge VLSI'23 [5] (40nm): 35-command task only 78.2%, 10-keyword 88.0%, area 7.63 mm² is about 10 times this paper’s (0.78 mm²), 183.4 nJ/decision. Compared to Tan ISSCC'24 [25] (28nm): 1.73 nJ/decision, 2 ms latency, 91.8% is the energy-efficiency benchmark in the table, but its advantage is built on the 28nm advanced node and 10-keyword task; this paper’s value proposition is achieving 36 nJ in a mature, cheap process like 65nm, under the more difficult task setting of 12-class full labels—process accessibility and task difficulty are contexts that must be included in this comparison.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

The paper’s three sets of scans constitute a complete ablation evidence chain. The first set is the FEx channel number scan (Fig. 6, simulation): the inflection point for 12-class accuracy dropping with channel number appears only after 10 channels, so 10 channels (516 Hz–4.22 kHz) is the most power-efficient configuration without accuracy loss, cutting 6 channels for 30% FEx power reduction—quantitative evidence of feature dimension redundancy. The second set is the FEx optimization step decomposition (Fig. 7): mixed-precision coefficients (12b/8b) bring 2.4x power and 2.6x area reduction; coefficient hardware-friendliness (half multipliers replaced by shifts) brings another 1.8x, 1.8x; the two steps multiplied yield a total of 5.7x power and 4.7x area reduction—the marginal contribution of each step is individually calibrated. The third set is the ΔTH scan (Fig. 12, measured): the four curves of accuracy, energy per decision, average temporal sparsity, and computational latency move in tandem with ΔTH, with 0.2 being the selected sweet spot; this curve shows that the gains from temporal sparsity are not free (accuracy cost is less than 0.6 percentage points), but the knob is continuously adjustable, allowing deployers to re-select operating points on the curve according to product requirements. Additionally, module-level breakdown (Fig. 10 and Section III text) can cross-verify absolute numbers elsewhere: power share is FEx 25%, ΔRNN 57%, SRAM 18% (FEx measured 1.22 µW ≈ 25% of 5.22 µW, SRAM read power 0.93 µW ≈ 18%, both self-consistent); area share is 11%, 41%, 48% (corresponding to the absolute areas of the three modules 0.084/0.319/0.381 mm², Abstract). It is worth pointing out that these two sentences in Section III of the main text are both written as "proportion of total power" in the original; judging by numerical cross-verification, the first sentence (11%/41%/48%) is actually an area breakdown, and the second sentence (25%/57%/18%) is the power breakdown—this is a minor typo in the original, but does not affect the conclusion.

## Main Contributions

First, this is the first work, as claimed by the paper, to implement ΔRNN fine-grained temporal sparsity as a KWS integrated circuit (Abstract: to the best of our knowledge, the first ΔRNN-enabled fine-grained temporal sparsity KWS IC), bringing the previously unmined energy-efficiency dimension of "activation change sparsity" into chip implementation. Second, system-level achievement: 65nm process, 0.78 mm², 5.22 µW, 36 nJ/decision, GSCD 11-class 90.5% / 12-class 89.5%, achieving 2.4x latency and 3.4x energy gains under 87% temporal sparsity (Abstract, Table II). Third, the digital IIR BPF feature extractor achieves 5.7x power and 4.7x area reduction through a triple optimization of mixed precision, low-cost computational structure, and channel selection, and exceeds opponents' 8b feature configurations with 12b precision (Section II-C, Table I). Fourth, the 0.6V near-threshold custom 24 kB SRAM uses a combination of word-line level shifting and on-chip booster, achieving 6.6x lower read power than foundry SRAM (0.93 µW, accounting for only 18% of the total chip), directly addressing the structural bottleneck of "storage eating 40%–60% power" (Section II-D). Fifth, methodological demonstration: three-layer co-design of algorithm (Δ encoding), architecture (FIFO broadcast + state assembly), and circuit (near-threshold storage), proving that bio-inspired sparse computation can be mapped to digital VLSI without sacrificing accuracy.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, process generation gap limits the absolute energy-efficiency ceiling: this paper’s 36 nJ/decision is achieved on 65nm, while Tan ISSCC'24 at 28nm achieves 1.73 nJ/decision (Table II); in absolute numbers, this paper is not SOTA; the paper’s value proposition must be understood in the context of "mature process + 12-class full-label task." Second, SRAM area cost: the custom near-threshold SRAM is 2 times larger than the foundry solution (Section II-D), requiring re-balancing in products with extreme area sensitivity; the dual power supply of 0.6V/0.65V is also a compromise caused by the nature of the I/O level shifter; the paper itself admits that single-supply schemes (Q bypassing the I/O shifter or using bidirectional shifters) were not implemented in this chip (Section II-D). Third, ΔTH is statically configured: it cannot change during operation, while sparsity itself is content-dependent (silence frames automatically save 40% latency, Fig. 11); worst-case design still has to calculate based on the load of active speech—the potential of the knob is not fully released by runtime strategies. Fourth, the accuracy-sparsity trade-off is not zero-cost: 87% sparsity costs nearly 0.6 percentage points of accuracy; the paper does not give specific values for the accuracy collapse point in higher ΔTH intervals (it only exists in the curve of Fig. 12). Fifth, system integrity gap: the chip input is a SPI digital audio stream; the microphone front-end, ADC, on-chip clock (clock derived from FPGA master clock division), and power management are not integrated or not reported in the paper (Seol’s power metric in Table II includes AFE, while this paper’s does not, so care must be taken in comparison); 6.9 ms latency is also not the lowest in the comparison array (Kosuge 1.2 ms, Tan 2 ms). Sixth, the FEx filter bank is a fixed design rather than a learnable front-end; the ceiling of feature representation capability is determined by the grid search at design time.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First, evaluation only covers clean conditions of a single dataset, GSCD: robustness experiments for far-field reverberation, noise addition, multi-speaker, and real-product wake words are not reported, while always-on devices operate precisely in noisy far-field environments. Second, missing training and model details: data augmentation strategies, training epochs, whether quantization-aware training was used, exact model parameter counts, and per-layer bit-width details are not reported (one can only infer ~20,000+ weights from 24 kB, 8b weights); reproduction requires tracing back to the team’s prior work. Third, fairness issue in cross-process comparison: Table II mixes three generations of processes (65/40/28nm); ranking energy numbers directly without process context is not rigorous, and the paper does not perform process normalization. Fourth, minor inconsistencies in documentation: the two sentences on power/area breakdown in Section III are both labeled as "total power" (judging by numerical cross-verification, the first sentence should be area); the clock 128k in Table I is inconsistent with the 125k expression in Table II. Fifth, the paper does not report chip-to-chip variation, PVT (Process, Voltage, Temperature) corner analysis, or yield data, which are exactly key verification items for engineering a custom near-threshold storage design.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

The most direct short-term path is the single-supply near-threshold SRAM pointed out by the paper itself (removing dependence on I/O level shifters), which can save another power domain overhead. The most imaginative medium-term direction is content-adaptive ΔTH: turning the static design point curve of Fig. 12 into a runtime strategy, dynamically adjusting the sparsity-accuracy knob using input energy or speech activity level, being aggressively sparse during silence and reverting to dense during speech—this shares the same origin as Seol et al.’s content-adaptive frame subsampling idea, but with granularity down to the neuron level. In terms of process, migrating this architecture to 28nm or more advanced nodes, combined with more compact storage, is expected to push 36 nJ below the nanojoule range, while maintaining the 12-class task setting. In terms of algorithms, Δ-ization can be extended to other edge-side loads such as attention and TCN (the team has laid the framework for activity-driven computing architectures in review works [13][14]), and the filter bank can be jointly trained with the network to become a learnable front-end. In terms of productization, the integration of on-chip clock, power management, ADC, and microphone front-end, along with joint energy-efficiency reporting, is the necessary path from demonstration chips to deployable devices. As a reader, the most transferable judgment from this paper is: in always-on edge computing, "mining signal invariants in time, paying only for changes" is an energy-saving axis orthogonal to compression, pruning, and quantization, and can be stacked. Its cost is architectural complexity (state consistency, clock domain crossing, event scheduling), and this paper proves with a 0.78 mm² prototype that this exchange is worthwhile on 65nm.
