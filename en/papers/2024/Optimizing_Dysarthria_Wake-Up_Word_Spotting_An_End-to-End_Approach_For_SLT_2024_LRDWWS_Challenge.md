## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Speech has become a widely adopted human-computer interaction interface for various devices, and Wake-Up Word Spotting (WWS) is the first link in the speech interaction chain: subsequent command recognition is meaningless unless the device first "hears" the wake-up word. Deep learning has significantly improved the accuracy of wake-up word detection in noisy environments and under accent variations, but this progress has an implicit premise—the user's pronunciation is close to a "standard, intelligible" speech distribution.

Dysarthria falls exactly outside this premise. It is a motor speech disorder caused by neurological diseases damaging the control of speech muscles, commonly seen in patients with Parkinson's disease, cerebral palsy, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Typical manifestations include inaccurate pronunciation, irregular speech rate, disrupted speech rhythm, and reduced volume and clarity (paper citation [5]). Commercial speech recognition technologies are primarily designed for users with standard pronunciation and lack sensitivity to non-standard, unclear speech, leading to significant degradation in recognition performance (paper citation [6]). For these users, the failure of wake-up word detection is not merely an experience issue, but a problem of closing the entire speech interaction channel—and it is precisely the mobility-impaired dysarthric patients who most need hands-free speech interaction. Therefore, Dysarthric Speech Recognition (DSR) has long attracted researchers' attention; its value is not only a technical metric but also directly relates to the quality of life of this group.

However, this field faces a cruel data reality: the cost of collecting dysarthric speech is much higher than that of normal speech (patients are difficult to recruit for recording, recruitment is difficult, and annotation requires professional knowledge). The scarcity of training data exacerbates the challenges of DSR (paper citation [16]). More regretfully, as pointed out in the paper, research and data on "wake-up word" recognition for dysarthria are even scarcer than for general dysarthric speech recognition—this is understandable: wake-up word datasets require each user to record words one by one, and dysarthric wake-up word data can almost only be collected in a speaker-specific manner, leaving public corpora blank for a long time.

To fill this gap, the IEEE SLT 2024 workshop launched the Low Resource Dysarthric Wake Word Recognition Challenge (LRDWWS, paper citation [21]). The task setting is speaker-dependent wake-up word detection: using a small amount of wake-up word audio from a single speaker to customize a wake-up word system for that person. The challenge also released the first Mandarin dysarthric speech corpus. This paper presents the first-place solution for the challenge, named PD-DWS (Pretrain-based Dual-filter Dysarthria Wake-up word Spotting).

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper reviews three existing routes for dysarthric speech recognition in the introduction and points out their deficiencies one by one:

- **Phonological Repair Route**: Repairing dysarthric speech into normal speech at the input end before sending it to the recognizer. Yang et al. (citation [11]) used CycleGAN to convert dysarthric speech signals in the spectral domain and synthesize new speech to improve language intelligibility; Daniel et al. (citation [12]) used Variational Autoencoders (VAE) to reconstruct blurred speech signals to improve recognition accuracy. The fatal weakness of this route is that the effect of repair methods decreases as the severity of dysarthria increases. The reason is that generative repair relies on a learnable mapping between the source and target domains, while the pronunciation variations in severe dysarthria are large and unstable, making it difficult for the mapping itself to converge.
- **Personalized Model Route**: Training personalized models using the terminal user's speaker samples, which can effectively improve accuracy even in moderate-to-severe environments (citations [13][14][15], including Google's Parrotron series works, where personalized models exceeded human listeners in phrase recognition). However, this route is constrained by data scarcity—the amount of samples an individual can provide is too small.
- **Data Augmentation and Self-Supervised Route**: Using synthetic data for augmentation (citations [17][18]) or using Wav2Vec self-supervised representations as features (citation [20]). These efforts have brought improvements, but the paper explicitly states that DSR still faces major challenges, and these works target general dysarthric speech recognition, not touching upon the special structure of wake-up word detection.

Specifically for this challenge, the official baseline's performance intuitively exposes the difficulty of the problem: the official baseline achieved a total score of 0.130306 on the final evaluation set test-B-eval, with a False Rejection Rate (FRR) as high as 0.101667 (Table 3, official baseline row). An FRR exceeding 0.1 means that for every ten clear wake-up attempts by the user, the system misses one—and for dysarthric patients who already struggle with pronunciation, a failed wake-up attempt is a double blow. On the development set test-A-eval, the baseline total score was even higher at 0.3112 (Table 2), with an FRR of 0.2725, approaching one failure for every three wake-up attempts.

In summary, the deficiencies of existing methods can be summarized into four points: First, small models trained from scratch (the baseline is a 4-layer DS-TCN with hidden dimension 256, see Section 3.2 configuration) have a model capacity that severely mismatches the high-variation space of dysarthria given the low-resource data of only 17 hours; Second, a single wake-up word detection head only outputs word-level probabilities, lacking any linguistic-level verification, making acoustic negative samples (fillers) similar to the wake-up word prone to false triggers; Third, threshold decision-making has only one level, with no layered means to balance the seesaw between False Alarm Rate (FAR) and FRR; Fourth, general ASR large models have never seen dysarthric speech, resulting in high word error rates in transcription, making them unable to serve directly as verifiers.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

First, how to enable the model to robustly model highly variable dysarthric speech under the ultra-low resource condition of 18,630 recordings and 17 hours—this is essentially not a problem of "training a model," but rather "how to transfer general speech priors to this distorted domain." Second, how to suppress the False Alarm Rate (FAR) without sacrificing the False Rejection Rate (FRR)—the evaluation metric is the sum of the two, and the two naturally trade off against each other; optimizing at a single point inevitably leads to neglecting the other. Third, how to adapt an open-source ASR large model (Paraformer) designed for normal speech to the dysarthric domain, so that its output can be used to verify wake-up word results. Fourth, the registration data for speaker-dependent tasks is extremely thin (each speaker has only 5 recordings per wake-up word, see the dataset section); how to extract the value of this small amount of data without overfitting.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The PD-DWS system consists of two main components plus a data engine (Fig. 1(a)): the audio modeling part is a fine-tuned 2branch-d2v2 encoder, the dual-filter part consists of a Threshold Filter and an ASR Filter connected in series, and there is also an independent VITS Text-to-Speech engine for synthesizing dysarthric speech for Paraformer fine-tuning. The entire inference chain is: audio input goes into 2branch-d2v2, its WWS branch outputs probability scores for ten wake-up words, and after sorting and threshold selection, a preliminary wake-up word result is obtained; simultaneously, the ASR branch obtains the first transcription result (ASR result1) via beam search decoding, and a fine-tuned Paraformer outside the link outputs the second transcription result (ASR result2); the ASR Filter comprehensively uses the two transcription results to make a secondary decision on the wake-up word result, outputting the final result.

Every layer of design in this architecture has a clear "why":

- **Why use pre-trained data2vec2 for audio modeling backbone**: Training from scratch with 17 hours of data cannot possibly cover the variation space of dysarthria, while self-supervised pre-trained models have learned representations that are somewhat invariant to pronunciation distortions from massive unlabeled speech. The first contribution point of the paper is "verifying the audio modeling capability of different encoders, with experiments showing that pre-trained models can achieve good results in low-resource scenarios"—this judgment is directly supported by the data in Table 2: the total score of pre-trained d2v2 (0.0343) is less than one-ninth of the from-scratch baseline (0.3112).
- **Why do multi-task (joint fine-tuning of ASR+WWS)**: The supervision signal for wake-up word detection is word-level binary/multi-classification, which is sparse; while the CTC supervision of ASR provides character-level, frame-by-frame alignment pressure, forcing the encoder to map blurred dysarthric acoustic frames to the correct character sequence. This is equivalent to using the ASR task as structured regularization; the paper's original expression is "leveraging ASR to assist WWS modeling." Table 6 ablation validates this motivation: after removing the CTC loss (1branch-d2v2), the total score degraded from 0.0343 to 0.0396.
- **Why do dual filters**: The probability score of the WWS branch manages recall (it is sensitive to the acoustic presence of the wake-up word), while the ASR transcription manages content verification (it can judge "whether the word said is this word"). The two-stage series connection allows FAR to decrease without harming FRR—Table 5 shows that after adding the ASR Filter, FAR dropped from 0.004434 to 0.003322, while FRR remained stable at 0.007500.
- **Why synthesize dysarthric speech with TTS**: The upper limit of the ASR Filter depends on transcription quality, and dysarthria is exactly the most vulnerable domain for general ASR, with only 9.4 hours of real dysarthric data. Using style-controllable TTS to generate unlimited "simulated dysarthric" audio is a supplement to domain adaptation in the data dimension.

The training process follows the three-step data flow of the challenge official (official repository footnote 1): Step 1, train a speaker-independent control (SIC) KWS model from scratch using control group (non-dysarthric, control) data; Step 2, fine-tune the SIC using uncontrol group (dysarthric, uncontrol) data to obtain a speaker-independent dysarthric (SID) model; Step 3, the paper fine-tunes the SID using the entire enrollment set, rather than fine-tuning separately for each speaker. The choice of Step 3 is worth pondering: why merge all enrollment data? Each speaker has only 5 recordings per wake-up word, and separate fine-tuning would almost certainly lead to overfitting; merged fine-tuning allows the variation patterns of different dysarthric speakers to be shared with each other, relying on model capacity and data augmentation to generalize to individuals, which is a rational trade-off under low-resource conditions.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Multi-Task Training Objective (Equation 1)**: The final training loss of the 2branch-d2v2 is the weighted sum of the losses of the two branches:

$$L = 0.5 \cdot L_{CTC} + 1.0 \cdot L_{WWS}$$

The WWS branch uses the challenge official's max pooling loss (citation [26], Hou et al. ICASSP 2020's negative sample mining framework): taking the maximum of the frame-level wake-up word probabilities for the entire audio segment as the segment-level score to calculate the classification loss. Why use max pooling instead of average pooling: the wake-up word occupies only a small segment of the audio, and averaging would dilute the high confidence of frames with the wake-up word, while taking the maximum allows any segment with high confidence to pass, naturally fitting the task form of "detection" rather than "classification," and working in conjunction with negative sample mining to strengthen hard example constraints. The ASR branch uses the CTC loss (citation [27]), whose characteristic of not requiring frame-level alignment and obtaining sequences through blank and repetition folding exactly adapts to the reality that dysarthric speech rate is irregular and pause abnormalities make alignment information unreliable. The weight allocation of 0.5 and 1.0 for the two losses indicates that WWS is the main task and ASR is auxiliary regularization—if the CTC weight is too large, the optimization direction will be pulled towards transcription completeness at the expense of wake-up word discriminability.

**Evaluation Metrics (Equation 2)**: The challenge official metric is

$$Score = FRR + FAR = \frac{N_{FR}}{N_{wake}} + \frac{N_{FA}}{N_{non-wake}}$$

where $N_{wake}$ is the number of samples containing the wake-up word, $N_{non-wake}$ is the number of samples not containing the wake-up word, $N_{FR}$ is the number of samples with a wake-up word that were not recognized, and $N_{FA}$ is the number of samples without a wake-up word that were judged as positive. The reason for choosing this metric is clearly stated in the paper: it "mitigates the overly optimistic evaluation that may be brought about by highly imbalanced class distribution." In wake-up word tasks, non-wake-up samples are far more numerous than wake-up samples; a degenerate model that always outputs "non-wake-up" would score high in terms of accuracy; the sum of FRR+FAR forces the model to perform well at both ends, and any collapse at one end will directly enter the total score.

**Sorting Mechanism of the Threshold Filter**: The threshold filter processes the ten wake-up word probabilities output by the WWS branch. For each audio sample, the maximum of the ten probabilities is taken as the temporal score for that sample, and the corresponding wake-up word is taken as the temporal label; then all samples are sorted in descending order by temporal score, and a temporary threshold is established by checking the ordered scores—samples with scores below the threshold have their temporal labels changed to the filter label (filler), while those above retain the original label. Note the essence of this design: it is not a fixed probability threshold, but a "quota system"—truncating at the 60th rank. Why is this more stable: the absolute value of probabilities drifts with model and data distribution, while the ranking order of scores is relatively stable; the quota system threshold is naturally immune to drift where scores are generally too high or too low. Extensive experiments on test-A-eval show that taking the 60th highest score as the threshold yields the best performance.

**Length Matching of the ASR Filter (Algorithm 1)**: The algorithm input is the predicted word label $p$ after the second stage (after threshold filtering), the two ASR transcriptions ASR_result1 and ASR_result2, and the list of ten wake-up words. If $p$ is in the wake-up word list, and len(ASR_result1) == len(p) or len(ASR_result2) == len(p), then the prediction $p$ is retained; if the lengths of both transcriptions are not equal to len(p), the prediction is updated to -1 (judged as filler). The most counter-intuitive and interesting part here is: **it compares the length (number of characters) of the transcription results, not the transcription content itself**. Why not do literal matching? Because inaccurate pronunciation in dysarthria leads to a large number of homophone and near-homophone substitutions, and ASR transcription is prone to wrong characters; literal matching would mistakenly kill a large number of true wake-up words (FRR explodes); while the number of characters in a wake-up word is a coarse-grained but much more robust feature—the transcription of a two-character wake-up word is likely still two characters. This is an engineering compromise for content verification in the domain of high ASR word error rates: better to let go of "other words that happen to have the same number of characters" than to sacrifice recall. The data in the first row of Table 5 confirms this from the opposite side: the route of using Paraformer transcription alone for strict matching had an FRR as high as 0.019500, which is 2.6 times that of the complete system.

**Style-Conditional Generation of VITS**: The paper trains an end-to-end VITS (citation [30]) using two types of data: control group and uncontrol group, using control/uncontrol labels to distinguish different speech styles, and injecting the label as a style embedding into VITS's Text Encoder and Flow module (Fig. 2 shows the inference structure: text input to text encoder, via random duration predictor and projection, via Flow and decoder to output waveform). Using the uncontrol label during inference generates audio with a dysarthric style. Its feasibility is based on the fact that VITS, as a conditional generation model, allows discretizing "speech style" into controllable conditions; pairing the same text with two style labels can generate normal and dysarthric versions of audio in pairs, thereby expanding the wake-up word text list into training data of arbitrary scale in dysarthric style.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: 2branch-d2v2 Multi-Task Audio Modeling

The 2branch-d2v2 encoder (Fig. 1(b)) initializes with pre-trained data2vec2, and the output connects to two independent branch heads: the WWS linear head and the ASR linear head, applying max pooling loss and CTC loss respectively. This "one shared encoder plus two task heads" structure is a classic paradigm of multi-task learning, and its value lies in the fact that the acoustic distortion of dysarthric speech is a shared difficulty for both tasks; the shared encoder is forced to learn representations that are invariant to distortion and sensitive to linguistic content, and the gradients of the two heads mutually constrain the representation space.

The configuration of the pre-training stage is fully aligned with d2v2 large (paper footnote 3, fairseq implementation): prenet depth 8, main body depth 16, 16 attention heads, dimension 1024, with a total parameter count of approximately 300 million. Training used 8 A800 GPUs, gradient accumulation of 6 steps, for a total of 600,000 steps; the learning rate schedule was cosine scheduling, reaching a peak of 0.0004 after 10,000 steps. The pre-training corpus (Table 1) is very mixed and broad: LibriHeavy, GigaSpeech, WenetSpeech, AISHELL-1/2, ACAV100M, multiple OpenSLR sets (38/47/68/82/87/111/119/123/124/133), CommonVoice, and the LRDWWS training set itself—note that the challenge data was mixed in during the pre-training stage, allowing the self-supervised objective to "see" the distribution of dysarthric speech first. The total duration of these corpora is not reported by the paper.

The fine-tuning stage was conducted on 2 A800 GPUs, with a dynamic batch size (approximately 300 seconds of audio per batch), using the Adam optimizer. The learning rate adopted a refined dual-track system: the non-pre-trained part (two new branch heads) reached a peak of 0.001 after 450 steps, and the pre-trained part reached a peak of 0.00005 after 1600 steps—the learning rate of the pre-trained part is 20 times lower than that of the new heads. Why: newly initialized heads need fast convergence, while the 300-million-parameter pre-trained representation only allows slight perturbations; a large learning rate would cause catastrophic forgetting of the general speech knowledge learned by self-supervision, and this 20-fold difference is the embodiment of protective fine-tuning. The control, uncontrol, and enrollment datasets were iterated for approximately 35, 7, and 7 epochs respectively, with each fine-tuning continuing until convergence (official accuracy reached approximately 1.0). The vocabulary of the ASR branch was taken from the LRDWWS training set text, with 451 units in total including Chinese characters and English letters (including blank, unk, sos/eos).

Three types of dynamic augmentation were applied throughout the training: volume perturbation with 10% amplitude, MUSAN noise (citation [28]) superposition with 15% probability (signal-to-noise ratio 8 to 20 dB), and speed perturbation from 0.9 to 1.1 times. Why these three: volume corresponds to robustness against the dysarthric symptom of "volume reduction," adding noise corresponds to real home environments, and speed perturbation directly targets the core symptom of dysarthria "irregular speech rate"—widening the speed of training audio by 10% up and down is equivalent to teaching the model to tolerate rhythm distortion.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Dual-Filter Strategy

The dual-filter is the core design of this paper at the decision level, consisting of a threshold filter and an ASR filter connected in series.

The threshold filter is responsible for the first level, converting the probability output of the WWS branch into a preliminary decision using sorting and quota truncation (60th rank). The determination of the quota parameter relies entirely on ablation on test-A-eval (Table 4): scanning the threshold rank from 55 to 61, FAR monotonically increased from 0.0022 to 0.0056, and FRR monotonically decreased from 0.0675 to 0.0275; the total score was lowest at rank 60 (0.0322). Although rank 55 achieved the lowest FAR (0.0022), the FRR (0.0675) was 2.45 times that of rank 60—the tighter quota let go of fewer samples, reducing false alarms but drastically increasing missed alarms, resulting in a worse total score.

The ASR filter is responsible for the second level, using two ASR transcriptions for length consistency verification. The first comes from the beam search decoding of the model's own ASR branch (ASR result1), and the second comes from the externally fine-tuned open-source Paraformer large (ASR result2, model from FunASR). Why use two paths instead of one: the model-internal ASR branch shares the encoder with the WWS branch, so its errors are correlated with WWS errors (the same encoder sees wrong, both heads make mistakes); the external Paraformer is a completely independent model with uncorrelated error patterns; any one of the two paths matching in length is sufficient to retain, equivalent to a low-cost double-model vote, significantly reducing the probability of single-point false killing. The ablation in Table 5 proves the value of this level: after adding the ASR filter to 2branch-d2v2, the total score dropped from 0.011934 to 0.010822, FAR dropped from 0.004434 to 0.003322 (a relative decrease of about 25%), and FRR remained unchanged at 0.007500—this is the empirical source of the abstract's claim of "reducing false alarm rate while maintaining the same false rejection rate."

### Key Technical Innovation 3: VITS-Synthesized Dysarthric Speech Augmentation for Paraformer

The second transcription source of the ASR Filter is the open-source Paraformer large (citation [22], non-autoregressive parallel transcription ASR model). To adapt this model designed for normal speech to the dysarthric domain, the paper uses VITS-synthesized dysarthric audio for fine-tuning data augmentation, and the three-level progression of fine-tuning data is fully ablated in Table 7: bare Paraformer total score 0.1050; after adding LRDWWS training set fine-tuning, it dropped to 0.0646; adding synthetic data further dropped it to 0.0493 (all measured under test-A-eval, Table 5 same matching strategy). This step of synthetic data contributed the largest single-step improvement (total score relatively decreased by about 24%, FRR dropped from 0.0625 to 0.0475). Why synthetic data can further suppress after real data fine-tuning: the real dysarthric training set is only 9.4 hours and has only 21 speakers, so fine-tuning quickly overfits; TTS synthesis is not limited by recording volume, and can generate dysarthric style speech on any text combination, broadening the "domain" from the distribution of 21 people to a continuous distribution in the text space.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the official baseline: The baseline is based on the WEKWS toolchain (citation [37]), inputting 80-dimensional log-Mel filter bank features (25 ms window, 10 ms shift), the encoder is a 4-layer depth separable time convolutional network (DS-TCN, citation [38]), hidden dimension 256, Adam optimizer, iterating 80 epochs for each of the three datasets—a typical from-scratch training small-footprint KWS paradigm. The path of this paper is completely different: rewriting the problem from "training a small model" to "domain adaptation of a 300-million-parameter self-supervised pre-trained model," compensating for the data gap with multi-task fine-tuning and decision-end filtering. The result is that the development set total score was compressed from 0.3112 to 0.0343 (Table 2), and FRR was compressed from 0.2725 to 0.0300, an improvement of nearly an order of magnitude.

Compared to the Conformer strong baseline: The paper also implemented a 12-layer, 4-head, 256 hidden dimension, approximately 31-million-parameter Conformer encoder (global and local modeling via self-attention plus convolution modules, citations [23][24]) as a control, with a total score of 0.1008 (Table 2). Although far better than the official baseline, it is still significantly worse than the 0.0343 of 2branch-d2v2. This comparison isolates two factors: the architecture itself (Conformer is already a strong architecture) and pre-training plus multi-task (contribution of d2v2), indicating that the main source of the gap is not "switching to a better architecture" but "pre-training prior plus ASR auxiliary supervision." This is the hardest evidence for the paper's conclusion that "pre-trained models should be used in low-resource scenarios."

Compared to the speech repair route (cycle GAN, VAE): This paper does not do repair at the input end (fails in severe scenarios), but compensates for distortion simultaneously at the model end (pre-training plus multi-task) and the decision end (dual filtering), essentially replacing "making speech normal" with "making the model tolerant to distortion."

Compared to the pure ASR detection route: The first row of Table 5 gives the pure ASR scheme of "audio directly into Paraformer, transcription result strictly matched with wake-up word list"—FAR is extremely low (0.001601, lowest in the field) but FRR is as high as 0.019500, and the total score of 0.021101 is actually worse than the 0.011934 of 2branch-d2v2. This shows that the pure ASR route has sufficient precision but insufficient recall, while this paper uses the WWS branch to manage recall and ASR verification to manage precision, which is a structural utilization of their complementarity.

Compared to other teams in the challenge (Table 3): The second place total score was 0.009801 (FAR 0.004801, FRR 0.005000), the third place was 0.010533 (FAR 0.003033, FRR 0.007500). This paper ranked first with 0.008210, and the balance point of FAR (0.003210) and FRR (0.005000) was chosen better than the second place (FAR 50% higher) and the third place (FRR 50% higher)—winning on the balance of both ends rather than a single-point crush.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The main dataset LRDWWS (challenge released, Table 1 and Section 3.1) has a total of 18,630 recordings, 17 hours: control group (non-dysarthric) 10,125 recordings, 7.6 hours, from 25 speakers (13 female, 12 male); dysarthric group 8,505 recordings, 9.4 hours, from 21 speakers (12 female, 9 male). Each speaker contributes a fixed 405 recordings, of which 50 are wake-up word recordings (10 different wake-up words, 5 recordings per word), and the other 355 are non-wake-up word recordings, covering fixed command words, furniture descriptions, TV and audio control words, numbers, interactive sentences, and negative samples—the composition of these negative samples indicates that fillers and wake-up words are in the same semantic field, the task is not "finding words in arbitrary speech" but "finding wake-up words in easily confused commands," and the difficulty design is quite tricky.

The d2v2 used in the pre-training stage used large-scale external corpora (Table 1): LibriHeavy (approximately 50,000 hours of English), GigaSpeech (approximately 10,000 hours of English), WenetSpeech (over 10,000 hours of Mandarin), AISHELL-1/2, ACAV100M, ten OpenSLR corpora sets, CommonVoice, and the LRDWWS training set itself. All training stages except pre-training only use the LRDWWS training set (when fine-tuning 2branch-d2v2, the enrollment set is added separately; the TTS generator only uses the LRDWWS training set). Evaluation uses the challenge's test-A-eval (with ground truth labels, used for threshold exhaustive search and ablation) and test-B-eval (without labels, final ranking list). The total duration of external corpora is not reported by the paper.

### Definition and Reasons for Choosing Evaluation Metrics

The metric is the officially defined Score = FRR + FAR (Equation 2, source citation [39]). The reason for choosing it: the class distribution of wake-up word detection is highly imbalanced (non-wake-up samples are far more numerous than wake-up samples), and metrics such as accuracy will give overly optimistic estimates; FRR and FAR are normalized by their respective denominators ($N_{wake}$ and $N_{non-wake}$) and then summed, forcing the system to perform well at both missed wake-up and false alarm ends. FRR directly corresponds to user experience (shouting without waking up), and FAR corresponds to the consequences of false triggers (device mistakenly starting); the equal weight addition of the two means that the challenge organizers view the costs of the two types of errors as being of the same magnitude. All comparative experiments (Table 2 to Table 7) follow this metric, with arrows pointing down indicating lower is better.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Encoder Comparison (Table 2, test-A-eval, exhaustive threshold search)**: Official baseline total score 0.3112 (FAR 0.0387, FRR 0.2725); Conformer total score 0.1008 (FAR 0.0183, FRR 0.0825); 2branch-d2v2 total score 0.0343 (FAR 0.0043, FRR 0.0300). The relative decrease in total score of 2branch-d2v2 compared to the baseline is about 89%, and compared to Conformer about 66%; FRR was compressed from the baseline's 0.2725 all the way to 0.0300. This table establishes that all subsequent experiments use 2branch-d2v2. It should be noted that the performance in Table 2 is the "ideal threshold" performance where all possible thresholds are exhaustively searched on the labeled test-A to take the optimal value, which is different in caliber from the fixed rank threshold performance in Table 4 (0.0322 at rank 60), and they should not be mixed.

**Challenge Final List (Table 3, test-B-eval)**: This paper's system total score 0.008210 (FAR 0.003210, FRR 0.005000) first place; second place 0.009801 (FAR 0.004801, FRR 0.005000); third place 0.010533 (FAR 0.003033, FRR 0.007500); fourth place 0.099282; fifth place 0.112711; official baseline 0.130306 (FAR 0.028639, FRR 0.101667). The paper claims a "maximum absolute improvement of 93.69%" relative to the official baseline—calculated from the total score dropping from 0.130306 to 0.008210, this number is actually the relative decrease in total score ($(0.130306-0.008210)/0.130306 \approx 93.7\%$), and the paper's wording is absolute improvement, so the caliber needs to be noted when reading. Another detail that must be recorded: the paper explicitly notes in Section 4.2 that the performance on test-B was obtained "after merging the test-A-eval set into the training process"—utilizing development set data for final training within the allowed scope, which cannot be ignored when interpreting thevalue of the performance.

**Magnitude Sense on Test Sets**: From the official baseline's 0.13 magnitude to the first place's 0.008 magnitude, the progress span of the entire challenge is about 16 times; and the gap between the first place and the second place (0.008210 vs 0.009801, about 16%) is already a contest of millimeters, indicating that top solutions are approaching the reachable upper limit of this set of data and metrics.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper conducted four groups of ablations in total, each with clear conclusions:

**Threshold Rank Ablation (Table 4, test-A-eval, 2branch-d2v2)**: The total scores for ranks 55 to 61 are 0.0697, 0.0575, 0.0454, 0.0384, 0.0339, 0.0322, 0.0331 respectively; FAR are 0.0022, 0.0025, 0.0029, 0.0034, 0.0039, 0.0047, 0.0056 respectively; FRR are 0.0675, 0.0550, 0.0425, 0.0350, 0.0300, 0.0275, 0.0275 respectively. The pattern is very clean: for every level the rank is relaxed, FAR monotonically increases, FRR monotonically decreases, and the total score hits the bottom at rank 60 (0.0322) and rebounds at rank 61 (0.0331, at which point FRR has no room for improvement while FAR continues to deteriorate). Conclusion: the quota system threshold achieves the optimal trade-off between FAR and FRR at rank 60, and subsequent test-B experiments all take rank 60. Why FRR no longer drops after 61: true wake-up samples in the low-score segment have already been exhausted within rank 60, and relaxing further only lets in fillers.

**ASR Filter Ablation (Table 5, test-B-eval, rank 60)**: Pure Paraformer matching route total score 0.021101 (FAR 0.001601, FRR 0.019500); 2branch-d2v2 without ASR filter total score 0.011934 (FAR 0.004434, FRR 0.007500); with ASR filter total score 0.010822 (FAR 0.003322, FRR 0.007500). Three findings: First, the pure ASR route has the lowest FAR in the field but FRR collapses, verifying that strict content matching is not feasible under the high word error rate of dysarthric transcription; Second, the ASR filter relatively suppresses FAR by about 25% while FRR remains unchanged, achieving the design goal of "reducing false alarms without harming missed alarms"; Third, looking at the two rows together, the ASR filter is actually the optimal fusion point between the pure ASR route of "exchanging recall for precision" and the "high recall" WWS route.

**Dual-Branch Structure Ablation (Table 6, test-A-eval)**: 1branch-d2v2 without CTC loss total score 0.0396 (FAR 0.0046, FRR 0.0350), 2branch-d2v2 total score 0.0343 (FAR 0.0043, FRR 0.0300). Conclusion: CTC auxiliary supervision brings small but consistent improvements at both FAR and FRR ends, directly supporting the core hypothesis of "ASR modeling assisting KWS modeling"—character-level supervision on the shared encoder improves the quality of wake-up word representations. The magnitude of improvement is not large (total score relatively decreased by about 13%), indicating that the benefit of auxiliary supervision is progressive rather than revolutionary.

**Paraformer Fine-Tuning Data Ablation (Table 7, test-A-eval, same matching strategy as Table 5)**: Original Paraformer total score 0.1050 (FAR 0.0025, FRR 0.1025); after adding LRDWWS training set fine-tuning 0.0646 (FAR 0.0021, FRR 0.0625); after adding TTS synthetic data 0.0493 (FAR 0.0018, FRR 0.0475). Conclusion: Fine-tuning with real dysarthric data brings the first step of significant improvement (total score relatively decreased by 38%), and TTS synthetic data presses one more step (relatively decreased by 24% again), and FAR and FRR improve in the same direction in the three steps—synthesized dysarthric speech indeed allows general ASR to migrate to the dysarthric domain, verifying the effectiveness of style-embedded controllable generation in domain adaptation.

## Main Contributions

1. **Verification of Pre-training Paradigm for Low-Resource Dysarthric Wake-Up Word Detection**: Through controlled comparison of baseline, Conformer, and 2branch-d2v2 (Table 2), it is proved that in ultra-low resource scenarios of the order of 17 hours, self-supervised pre-trained models plus multi-task fine-tuning can achieve performance unattainable by from-scratch training (total score 0.3112 vs 0.0343), and provide fine-tuning details (dual-track scheduling with 20 times lower learning rate for pre-trained part) as reproducible engineering references.
2. **2branch-d2v2 Multi-Task Structure**: Training WWS branch (max pooling loss) and ASR branch (CTC loss) simultaneously on the shared d2v2 encoder, using the weighted loss of Equation 1 to turn ASR supervision into regularization for wake-up word modeling, ablation (Table 6) proves its effectiveness.
3. **Dual-Filter Decision Strategy**: Two-stage series connection of threshold filter (sorting quota truncation) and ASR filter (dual-path transcription length consistency verification, Algorithm 1), compressing FAR by about 25% more without losing FRR (Table 5), which is a key link for the system to win the final list.
4. **VITS Style-Controllable Dysarthric Data Synthesis**: Using control/uncontrol labels as style embeddings to train VITS, using synthesized dysarthric speech to enhance Paraformer's domain adaptation, ablation (Table 7) shows that synthetic data contributed the largest single-step improvement in fine-tuning benefits.
5. **Competition Performance**: Ranked first in the SLT 2024 LRDWWS challenge test-B-eval with a total score of 0.008210 (FAR 0.003210, FRR 0.005000).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **ASR Filter Only Verifies Length, Not Content**: The criterion of Algorithm 1 is to retain if the transcription length is equal to the number of characters in the wake-up word. Any transcription whose length happens to equal the wake-up word (e.g., a two-character filler transcribed into two other characters) will pass the verification. This is a reasonable compromise in the high word error rate domain, but the ceiling is obvious—it prevents errors like "the length of what ASR heard is not this word," but cannot prevent errors of "same length, different content." The theoretically better approach would be edit distance or phoneme-level fuzzy matching, but that would reintroduce FRR risk.
- **Quota System Threshold is Strongly Coupled with Evaluation Distribution**: The threshold of rank 60 implicitly contains the prior of test-A sample scale and wake-up word proportion. Once the prior of the deployment scenario changes (the proportion of negative samples is usually much higher than in the evaluation set), the operating point corresponding to rank 60 will drift, requiring recalibration. The rank threshold is robust to score distribution drift, but not robust to sample composition drift.
- **Authenticity of Synthesized Dysarthria is Unverified**: The paper does not report the MOS, intelligibility, or acoustic distance metrics of the synthesized speech compared to real dysarthric speech. The "uncontrol style embedding" learned might be superficial quality and rhythm distortion, and whether the "inaccurate articulation position, phoneme substitution" of real dysarthria is covered is not reported by the paper. The benefits in Table 7 prove that synthetic data is effective, but there is no evidence that it covers the variation spectrum of severe dysarthria.
- **Inference Overhead Completely Deviates from the Edge-Side Tradition of Wake-Up Words**: The industrial tradition of wake-up word detection is small footprint, low-power always-on (which is exactly the reason for the existence of toolchains like WEKWS), while this paper's scheme is a 300-million-parameter d2v2 plus dual-branch heads, plus the complete transcription of the external Paraformer large. The paper does not report the system's inference latency, real-time rate, or total parameter count. This scheme demonstrates "how accurate it can do," but does not answer "how to stay resident on the device"—distillation or quantization is likely required for landing.
- **Key Hyperparameters are All Empirical Values and Lack Sensitivity Analysis**: Multi-task weights 0.5 vs 1.0, quota threshold 60, augmentation parameters (10% volume, 15% noise probability, 0.9 to 1.1 speed) have no scanning experiments, and the paper does not report the robustness boundaries of these choices. What would happen if the 0.5 CTC weight was changed to 0.3 or 0.7 is unknown to the reader.
- **Risk of Training to Training Set Accuracy Saturation**: Fine-tuning "continues until official accuracy is approximately 1.0," pushing training accuracy to saturation on such a small dataset implies overfitting pressure, relying entirely on augmentation and early stopping conventions to cover.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **test-B Performance Mixed with test-A Data**: The paper itself notes that the final performance was obtained after merging test-A-eval into training. This conforms to the challenge rules, but makes the "zero development set contamination" value of the number 0.008210 unable to be evaluated, and is not completely in the same caliber as other teams if they do not use development set training.
- **Inconsistency Between Ablation and Final Performance Datasets**: Three of the four groups of ablations were done on test-A, only the ASR filter group was verified on test-B (Table 5); the extrapolation of the conclusions of threshold rank, dual-branch, and Paraformer data three-level ablation to test-B relies on the unverified assumption that the distributions of the two evaluation sets are similar.
- **Lack of Stratified Analysis by Dysarthria Severity**: The paper does not report the severity distribution of the 21 dysarthric speakers, nor the difference in FRR between mild and severe patients. For assistive applications, "whether severe patients can use it" is exactly a more critical issue than the average score.
- **Lack of Significance Testing**: Total score differences often appear in the ten-thousandth place (e.g., 0.0322 for rank 60 vs 0.0339 for rank 59), with single runs and no variance reported, it is unknown whether these small gaps are stable.
- **Lack of Direct Comparison with Academic SOTA**: The comparison objects are only challenge teams and the official baseline; academic routes such as Parrotron-style personalized ASR and cross-lingual self-supervised features (paper citation [20]) were not included in the experimental comparison, and can only be indirectly inferred from the magnitude of scores.
- **Shift in the Setting of Speaker-Dependent Tasks**: Step 3 fine-tuning merged all enrollment sets for training rather than separating by person (following the official process), meaning the final system is not strictly per-speaker customized, and the variance in performance between speakers is not reported by the paper.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

- **Upgrade ASR Verification from Length Matching to Phoneme-Level Fuzzy Matching**: Use edit distance threshold or phoneme posterior confusion degree for content verification, doing finer interpolation between maintaining recall and improving precision, replacing the current "equal number of characters" criterion which is too coarse.
- **Threshold Moves from Quota System to Probability Calibration**: Perform temperature calibration on the WWS branch output or speaker-adaptive threshold, so that the operating point no longer depends on the evaluation set sample composition prior, improving deployment generalization.
- **Hierarchical Style Control of Synthetic Data**: Introduce severity-grade embeddings (mild, moderate, severe) in VITS instead of a single uncontrol label, allowing synthetic data to cover the continuous spectrum from mild blurriness to severe distortion, and customizing the enhancement distribution according to the target user group.
- **Lightweight and Edge-Side Deployment**: Distill 2branch-d2v2 into a small model of DS-TCN level, and make ASR verification an offline or low-frequency triggered secondary confirmation, returning to the small-footprint tradition of wake-up word detection, which is likely the most direct next step for industrial landing (such as Xiaomi's speaker and mobile phone product lines).
- **Systematic Exploration of Multi-Task Structure**: The current CTC weight of 0.5 is a single-point empirical value; the weight curve can be scanned, and a third task such as phoneme branch or wake-up word contrastive learning can be tried, turning "ASR assisting KWS" into a tunable spectrum.
- **Expand Language and Task Scope**: Verify the transferability of this framework to dysarthric wake-up words in other languages such as English, and its extension to downstream tasks such as command word recognition and speaker verification; combined with the subsequent release of challenge corpora, make up for the public benchmark stratified by severity.

## Appendix: Terminology Quick Reference

- **Dysarthria**: A motor speech disorder caused by neurological diseases damaging the control of speech muscles, characterized by inaccurate pronunciation, irregular speech rate, disrupted rhythm, and reduced volume and clarity.
- **WWS / KWS**: Wake-Up Word Spotting / Keyword Spotting, detecting preset trigger words from a continuous audio stream.
- **data2vec2 (d2v2)**: An efficient self-supervised speech pre-training model proposed by Meta; this paper uses its large configuration (approximately 300 million parameters) as encoder initialization.
- **CTC Loss**: Connectionist Temporal Classification, sequence supervision without frame-level alignment, outputting sequences through blank and repetition folding.
- **Max Pooling Loss**: Official WWS training objective, taking the maximum of frame-level wake-up word probabilities as the segment-level score to calculate loss, from the negative sample mining framework.
- **Paraformer**: A non-autoregressive parallel transcription ASR model proposed by Alibaba; this paper uses it as an external transcriber (ASR result2).
- **VITS**: An end-to-end TTS combining conditional variational autoencoder and adversarial training; this paper injects control/uncontrol labels as style embeddings.
- **FAR / FRR**: False Alarm Rate (non-wake-up judged as positive divided by total non-wake-up) / False Rejection Rate (wake-up not recognized divided by total wake-up), the sum of the two is the challenge total score.
