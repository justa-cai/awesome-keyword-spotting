## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is a ubiquitous voice entry point for smart speakers, wearable devices, wireless earbuds, and IoT nodes. Unlike applications that can wake up intermittently, wake-word detection must listen to the audio streamaround the clock. Therefore, it runs on battery-powered ultra-low-power microcontrollers (MCUs) rather than application processors. The industry's mainstream paradigm is "train once, deploy everywhere": a DNN is trained on a large-scale, general-purpose keyword dataset on a server, quantized, and burned into the MCU, after which the model is permanently frozen. This paradigm exposes two rigid pain points in real-world products.

First, lack of personalization. If a user wants to add their own wake word or desires higher accuracy under their specific accent or home noise conditions, the only option is to send new data back to the server, manually annotate it, retrain, and re-burn the model. The cost, latency, and privacy implications of this entire chain are almost unacceptable for consumer-grade devices. Second, the acoustic conditions of the deployment environment are inconsistent with the training distribution. The recording experiments in the paper (HeySnips-REC, Fig. 4, playing back a public dataset via speakers in an office, including reverberation and non-stationary noise) provide direct evidence: the pre-trained DS-CNN-L model achieves only 60% accuracy on this data (Fig. 7), far below the 84.4% on clean public datasets (Table III).

Personalized KWS approaches attempt to bypass the server: a keyword-agnostic feature extractor encodes audio segments into embedding vectors. After deployment, the user only needs to record a few examples (usually fewer than 10), and the system constructs a prototype vector by taking the mean of the embeddings. Classification is performed at runtime based on the "distance from embedding to prototype," naturally supporting open-set recognition (anything unseen is classified as `unknown`). However, this approach has a structural ceiling: the feature extractor is frozen. Data from reference [13] shows that ResNet15 achieves only about 80% accuracy per class when there are only 10 samples per class on a 10-class problem—the metric space learned from general corpora is not optimal for specific users or environments. To break this upper limit, the feature extractor itself must be fine-tuned on-device, post-deployment, and in a label-free condition. This is the precise meaning of "self-learning" in the paper's title.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes related work into three families and points out their defects one by one; this梳理 (梳理/analysis) itself forms the foundation of the paper's argument.

**Personalized KWS / Few-Shot Family**: ProtoNet [12] uses prototype loss for few-shot classification; Jung et al. [9] train ResNet15 on 1000 classes from Librispeech using cosine prototype loss, reaching 96% in a 10-shot scenario; Kim et al. [8] add a dummy prototype to handle open-set recognition. There are two common defects: first, the feature extractor is frozen, capping accuracy at the level of general pre-training; second, evaluations are almost all based on datasets like GSC, which are pre-cut into fixed 1-second lengths. In reality, audio is a continuous signal, and the start/end positions of keywords are unknown. Fixed-length segmentation inevitably leads to misalignment in some cases. The team's own IEEE Micro work [13] serves as a direct baseline: it implements few-shot inference initialization on a multi-core MCU at approximately 20 mW, but it also only processes 1-second fixed-length speech, uses only 5 unknown classes for open-set testing, and completely lacks training capabilities.

**Self-Learning / Pseudo-Labeling Family**: In the ASR field, pseudo-label transcription is used for unsupervised domain adaptation [24]-[26]; a representative work in KWS is Noisy Student-Teacher [27]: a teacher model is trained on 2.5 million "Ok Google" samples, and the pseudo-labeled dataset it produces expands the supervised data to train a student model, reducing the error rate from 1.83% to 0.78% on an internal far-field clean set. The premise for this paradigm to work is a strong teacher trained on millions of samples; what this paper faces is a few-shot scenario—there is only a prototype classifier initialized with 3 user recordings, and pseudo-labels can only rely on distance rules, resulting in vastly different quality. Another transfer learning route: Mazumder et al. [28] add a linear classification head to an EfficientNet with 11 million parameters; TinyTrain [29] selectively retrains only the most important weights, achieving 3.6%-5% higher than naive fine-tuning; LifeLearner [30] repeats online training multiple times for continual learning. However, TinyTrain requires 30 annotated samples, while this paper has only 3; moreover, data augmentation can only perform in-distribution transformations on a few samples, failing to expand distribution diversity. The paper's core argument lies here: rather than augmenting 3 samples, it is better to harvest real data from the deployment environment in an unsupervised manner, where diversity and quantity are provided for free by the environment.

**On-Device Training Family**: TinyOL [32] trains only new layers, TinyTL [33] learns only bias terms, and [34] uses sparse update rules. The goal of all these works is to compress the activation storage for backpropagation into 256KB-level memory. However, these works assume that new data comes with true labels and do not solve the problem of where labels come from. In the KWS direction, [35] uses collected environmental noise to augment existing corpora for on-device domain adaptation, and [36] uses user recordings to improve accuracy for known classes; neither can learn "new" keywords. On the hardware level, the previous personalized KWS prototype [23] used a HiSilicon Hi3516EV200 development board (ARM Cortex-A7 @ 900MHz, 32MB memory), with a total power consumption of 100 mW, which is more than an order of magnitude higher than the budget targeted in this paper.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Condensing the above gaps into one sentence: Under the conditions of no labels, only 3 user recordings, variable-length audio, 1.5MB on-chip MCU memory, and milliwatt-level power budget, enable the deployed sensor node to generate its own training data and train itself. Broken down, there are four specific challenges:

1. **Where do pseudo-labels come from, and what if they are wrong?** The pre-trained prototype classifier itself is inaccurate (DS-CNN-S achieves only 62.5% on HeySnips, Table III). Using an inaccurate classifier to label data, and then using these labels to train the same model, is the classic error accumulation loop in self-learning, which is severely amplified under few-shot conditions.
2. **How to label variable-length audio?** In real audio streams, the start and end of keywords are unknown. A mechanism is needed to extract a single-value decision ("does this sentence look like a keyword?") from a sliding window sequence, and to filter out samples with uncertain confidence from the training set.
3. **Can training fit into the MCU?** Backpropagation requires caching intermediate activations for the entire mini-batch. The activations for a single forward pass of a 0.5M parameter convolutional network may far exceed the on-chip RAM. A hierarchical storage design must be implemented.
4. **Can the energy budget be balanced?** The labeling task is always-on, while the training task is triggered occasionally. The sum of both must fall within the battery budget—how many times the labeling energy exceeds the training energy, and how often training is triggered to be sustainable, determines whether the self-learning sensor is a real product or just a paper demonstration.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The framework (Fig. 1) is a closed loop of three stages:

1. **On-Device Calibration**: The user records K=3 positive examples and an equal number of negative examples. The system calculates the new keyword prototype $c_p$, dual thresholds $Th_L/Th_H$, and low-pass filter length $\alpha$. The choice of K=3 is practical: real users are unlikely to record many examples during device initialization.
2. **Labeling Task (Always-On)**: Continuously analyzes new environmental audio streams, assigns pseudo-labels based on distance rules, and stores pseudo-positive and pseudo-negative examples in memory separately.
3. **Incremental Training (Occasionally Triggered)**: Once enough pseudo-labeled samples are accumulated, the feature extractor is fine-tuned using triplet loss. After training, the **keyword prototypes are recalculated**, and the classifier is refreshed accordingly.

Note the final design point of the closed loop—the object of fine-tuning is the feature extractor itself, not a trainable classification head stacked on frozen features. This directly addresses the structural problem of "frozen feature extractors capping accuracy": pre-training learns a metric space for general keywords, and fine-tuning distorts this space towards the specific user and environment. The prototype is then recalculated in the new space, meaning both the feature manifold and the classification boundary move. Meanwhile, retaining the user's original 3 recordings in every mini-batch anchors the true values during the adaptation process, preventing the model from being led astray by pseudo-labels as a whole.

The hardware carrier (Fig. 2) consists of a Vesper VM3011 MEMS microphone + GreenWaves GAP9 processor + a 64MB external PSRAM (APMemory APS512XXN, peak bandwidth 400MBps, OctoSPI interface). The design philosophy is to partition computing resources according to three load types: "always-on," "burst," and "occasional":

- **Always-On Acquisition Path**: The microphone outputs digital signals via PDM modulation through I2S, consuming only 0.67 mW (1.8V supply, 768kHz I2S clock). The GAP9's always-on (AO) subsystem is managed by the RISC-V Fabric Controller (FC) core, which includes 1.5MB on-chip RAM, 2MB FLASH, and a programmable stream processor (SFU). The SFU is responsible for resampling audio to 16kHz and performing PDM-to-PCM conversion, outputting 32-bit fixed-point time-domain signals into a 4×8KB circular buffer (8KB exactly equals the memory usage for one TS=0.125s segment of audio). Offloading resampling and demodulation to the SFU instead of the CPU is because the labeling task spends most of its time waiting for data—the peripheral autonomous acquisition plus event-wakeup mode allows the FC core to sleep at 1MHz clock between events.
- **Computational Burst (Every 0.125s)**: The FC is woken up by an SFU event (triggered when one segment of the circular buffer is full), ramping up to 240MHz (0.65V), and powering up the Cluster subsystem—9 general-purpose RISC-V cores + an 8-bit convolution accelerator + 128KB TCDM tightly coupled data memory + DMA engine. MFCC is calculated in parallel by 9 cores using half-precision floating-point units (outputting a 47×10 feature map), and DNN inference runs through the convolution accelerator using 8-bit quantization. Immediately after calculation, the Cluster is turned off, and the FC drops back to 1MHz. The Cluster and AO subsystems belong to independent voltage-frequency domains, precisely to support this fine-grained power gating. The pseudocode for the entire process is in Fig. 3.
- **Training Path (Occasional)**: External RAM is powered off normally and only powered on during training tasks to store intermediate activations for backpropagation. The GAP9's memory interface can autonomously complete data transfer between external and internal memory without interrupting the CPU.

The quantization on the inference side uses the GAPflow toolchain's Post-Training Quantization (PTQ), 8-bit, per-channel asymmetric quantization range calibrated with only 4 random training samples. Consistent with previous work [13], it is observed that quantization does not cause accuracy loss—this means that the mixed-precision switching of "half-precision floating-point during training and 8-bit fixed-point during inference" in the self-learning closed loop incurs no accuracy cost.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Foundation: Metric Learning-based Personalized KWS** (Sec. III). The feature extractor $f(\cdot)$ maps a 1-second audio segment $x$ to an embedding $z=f(x)$. Pre-training uses triplet loss (Eq. 1):

$$loss = \frac{1}{N_{tr}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{tr}} \max(d(z_i^{p1}, z_i^{p2}) - d(z_i^{p1}, z_i^{n}) + m,\ 0)$$

where $z_{p1}, z_{p2}$ are embeddings of samples from the same class, $z_n$ is an embedding of a randomly selected different-class sample, distance $d$ is Euclidean distance, and margin $m=0.5$ (following [22]—the same team's open-set comparative study at Interspeech 2023 concluded that encoders trained with triplet loss have higher accuracy than ProtoNet, which is the basis for choosing triplet loss over prototype loss here). The pre-training corpus consists of the 500 classes with the most samples from MSWC, totaling 2.7M samples (approximately 5470 per class on average). After deployment, the prototype for each new class $j$ is the mean of the embeddings of K recordings (Eq. 2). At runtime, classification is performed (Eq. 3): the closest prototype is selected, and if the distance is less than $\gamma$, it is classified as that class; otherwise, it is `unknown`. $\gamma$ is a score threshold; lowering it forces the system to be more conservative (classifying more as `unknown`), directly controlling the False Alarm Rate (FAR).

**Streaming Labeling** (Sec. IV-A). A 1-second window slides with a step size $TS < 1s$. Every $TS$ seconds, a distance to the positive class prototype $dist(t)=d(f(x(t)),c_p)$ is produced (Eq. 4), which then passes through a sliding average low-pass filter of length $\alpha$ (Eq. 5) to obtain $dist_f$. The criterion is a dual-threshold dead zone:

- $\min_t dist_f(t) < Th_L \rightarrow$ Pseudo-positive (Eq. 6)
- $\min_t dist_f(t) > Th_H \rightarrow$ Pseudo-negative (Eq. 7)
- Between the two thresholds $\rightarrow$ No decision, sample discarded

The purpose of the $\min$ operator is to select the window with the best alignment from multiple sliding windows of variable-length speech; the purpose of the low-pass filter is to smooth inter-window jitter and suppress single-frame glitches. The purpose of the dual thresholds is to separate the confidence interval from the uncertain interval: the metric learning feature extractor inherently pushes different keywords far apart. Embeddings far from the prototype are highly unlikely to contain the target word, so treating them as negatives is safe; those very close to the prototype are treated as positives. The middle ground is the fuzzy region of the classification boundary; sampling into the training set would only inject label noise, so it is better to discard them.

**On-Device Threshold Calibration** (Sec. IV-B). The average filtered distances from K=3 positive and 3 negative examples to the prototype $c_p$ are calculated to obtain $dist_p$ and $dist_n$ (Eq. 8). The thresholds are calculated via linear interpolation (Eq. 9):

$$Th(\tau) = dist_p + \tau \cdot (dist_n - dist_p)$$

It is required that $\tau_L < \tau_H$; in the extreme case where $\tau_L=0$, the threshold degenerates to $dist_p$. The filter length $\alpha$ is selected from $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ to maximize the margin ($dist_n - dist_p$). The entire calibration requires no manual tuning, extra data, or server participation—the user completes initialization by recording 3 sentences. This is the premise for the self-learning closed loop to function in the field. The physical meaning of $\tau$ is the "relative position of the threshold on the average distance band between positive and negative examples": a small $\tau_L$ means the positive threshold is close to the positive mean (strict), a large $\tau_H$ means the negative threshold is close to the negative mean (loose), and the dead zone becomes wider accordingly.

**Incremental Training** (Sec. IV-C). Fixed number of epochs, no validation set; backpropagation directly returns the last set of weights (saving the memory and computation of a validation set); Adam optimizer, learning rate 0.001, loss is still triplet loss, and hyperparameters all follow the pre-training configuration [13]—deliberately not introducing new hyperparameters to reduce the risk of field failure. Each epoch randomly divides pseudo-positives into groups of $N_{pB}$; if a full group is not formed, training is not started (this explains why some speakers in the HeySnips-REC results did not trigger training); each group is randomly paired with $N_{nB}$ pseudo-negatives, plus K user original recordings, forming a mini-batch of $N_{pB}+N_{nB}+K$; triplets are constructed in all combinations among pseudo-positives, pseudo-negatives, and user samples, where the anchor and negative are taken from pseudo-labeled samples, and one positive is fixed as the user recording.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Training Construction Naturally Robust to Label Noise

This is the most critical design in the entire paper. Pseudo-labels will inevitably contain errors—Table III shows that on HeySnapdragon, the pseudo-positive error rate is as low as 48% (ResNet15) and as high as 84% (DS-CNN-S). A naive self-learning framework would enter an error accumulation dead loop here. The paper's solution is not to clean the labels, but to utilize the soft constraint structure of triplet loss: the loss only requires $d(z_{p1}, z_{p2}) + m < d(z_{p1}, z_n)$, it does not require $z_{p1}$ to truly be a positive example. Even if $z_{p1}$ is mislabeled (actually does not contain the keyword), the optimizer can still correct the position of this sample in the embedding space by pulling it closer to the user's true example $z_{p2}$ and pushing it away from the pseudo-negative $z_n$. The paper's original statement is "if $z_{p1}$ is mislabeled as positive, $d(z_{p1}, z_{p2}) > d(z_{p1}, z_n)$ may still not hold," meaning the optimization direction is generally still correct. Coupled with the K user true-value samples anchored in every mini-batch, the contaminated positive example set is closer to a noisy anchor pool rather than poison. The results in Table III support this analysis: on HeySnapdragon, ResNet15 pseudo-positive error rate is 48%, and accuracy still rises from 82.6% to 94.6% after fine-tuning; for the more extreme DS-CNN-S with an 84% error rate, accuracy still rises from 68.8% to 84.8%.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Dead-Zone Labeling and Tuning-Free Calibration for Variable-Length Streaming Audio

Addressing the issue of "variable-length real audio" that previous few-shot KWS works generally avoided, this paper uses a four-piece set of sliding window + min + low-pass + dual thresholds to label continuous streams, and all parameters ($\alpha, Th_L, Th_H$) are automatically exported on-device from 6 recordings (3 positive, 3 negative). Compared to Coucke et al. [2]'s streaming KWS which smooths posterior probabilities with a sliding window, the output of this paper is not a detection decision but **confidence filtering for training samples**—the dead-zone discard mechanism ensures that samples entering the training set carry a controllable noise rate. This is the key difference in transforming a "detector" into a "data harvester." Ablation studies (Sec. VI-C, Fig. 5) further pinpoint the sweet spot for $\tau$ as $\tau_L \in \{0.3, 0.4\}, \tau_H=0.9$: when $\tau_L=0.5$, too many false positives cause accuracy to drop; when $\tau_L \in \{0.1, 0.2\}$, there are too few pseudo-positives, leading to insufficient training set diversity.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Training Memory Hierarchy for MCU

Training uses 16-bit half-precision floating-point to store weights, activations, and gradients (PULPTrainLib has verified the efficiency of half-precision vectorized instructions on GAP9 [39]). Memory is divided into three layers: weights and gradients reside in on-chip RAM (DS-CNN-L requires 1.54MB, Table IV); newly collected data **stores only MFCC feature maps**, saving 34 times the memory compared to storing 16-bit raw audio (400 MFCC maps occupy only 0.36MB, Table IV); forward activations for each convolutional layer overflow to external RAM (DS-CNN-L requires 32.28MB, batch 73, Table IV), and external RAM is powered on only during training. After training, PTQ calibration with 4 samples is used to convert weights back to 8-bit, seamlessly connecting to the inference accelerator. Forward and backward computations reuse PULPTrainLib [37]—the first performance-driven on-device training library on multi-core RISC-V MCUs—estimated based on 8 cores with half-precision.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Comparing item by item with the three families of related work, the differences all fall under "stricter constraints, more complete closed loop":

- **Vs. Noisy Student-Teacher [27]**: The latter relies on a teacher trained on 2.5 million corpora to produce high-quality pseudo-labels; this paper has only distance rules initialized with 3 recordings. The latter fine-tunes a complete supervised pipeline; this paper is a lightweight baseline under few-shot open-set ($\le$0.5M parameters).
- **Vs. TinyTrain / LifeLearner [29][30]**: The latter uses 30 annotated samples plus data augmentation; this paper uses 3 samples plus unsupervised environmental data. The latter's contribution is reducing training costs via sparse updates; this paper deliberately uses a "naive training scheme with full-parameter updates" to first run the self-learning closed loop itself, leaving sparse updates as an orthogonally compatible improvement item.
- **Vs. On-Device Domain Adaptation [35][36]**: The latter can only adapt to noise or improve accuracy for known classes; this paper can learn brand-new keywords. The latter does not report labeling-side energy consumption; this paper provides the full-link energy account for labeling and training.
- **Vs. Own Previous Work [13]**: Advances from 1-second fixed-length, frozen features, inference-only (approx. 20 mW) to variable-length streaming, tunable features, always-on labeling 6.1-8.2 mW (Fig. 9) plus training energy estimation (Table IV).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

**Pre-training**: The 500 classes with the most samples from MSWC (Multilingual Spoken Words Corpus), totaling 2.7M samples, approximately 5470 per class on average (Sec. III).

**Evaluation**: Two public wake-word datasets plus one self-collected dataset (Table II):

- HeySnips [2][40]: Adaptation set 5347 positives + 31830 negatives; Test set 20 speakers, average 17.1 positives per speaker; Negative test set 13580 samples, totaling 15.3 hours (another part of the paper text writes 13584 negative sentences, taken from the original HeySnips test set).
- HeySnapdragon [41]: Adaptation set 462 positives + 31830 negatives; Test set 20 speakers, average 22.3 positives per speaker; Negative test set same as above.
- HeySnips-REC (Self-collected, Fig. 4): Played back via speakers from a subset of HeySnips, recorded in real-time by the paper's sensor node in an office (including environmental noise and reverberation); Adaptation set 63 positives + 337 negatives, Test set 20 speakers, 153 negatives totaling 0.2 hours (negative samples total 11.6 minutes). Data is open-sourced (KU Leuven RDR, doi:10.48804/SVEABM).

**Models** (Table I): DS-CNN-S (21k parameters, 2.7 MMAC, 64-dim embedding), DS-CNN-M (132k, 9.6 MMAC, 172-dim), DS-CNN-L (407k, 28.1 MMAC, 256-dim), ResNet15 (482k, 235.1 MMAC, 64-dim). Maximum feature map occupancy ranges from 24k to 537k elements, all of which can fit into the MCU's limited memory.

**Training Configuration**: 20 epochs on public sets, 140 samples per batch ($N_{pB}=20$ pseudo-positives + $N_{nB}=120$ pseudo-negatives + $K=3$ user recordings); on HeySnips-REC, due to less data, changed to 8 epochs, $N_{pB}=10 + N_{nB}=60 + K=3$, $\tau_L=0.4, \tau_H=0.9$. Accuracy experiments were run on an external server (consistent with the on-device algorithm); the on-device side only ran the labeling task and energy consumption measurements.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Per-speaker average accuracy at FAR=0.5/hour operating point (Public Sets)**: Following the protocol of HeySnips/HeySnapdragon original papers [2][41], a threshold is selected on the negative test set to achieve 0.5 false alarms per hour, and then accuracy is reported. The reason for choosing this is that the real-world operating condition of wake-word products is defined by the false alarm rate—a speaker that wakes up dozens of times randomly every day is unusable. Therefore, accuracy must be compared under a fixed false alarm constraint to be meaningful.
- **FAR=0 Operating Point (HeySnips-REC)**: Since the self-collected negative samples are only 11.6 minutes long, a stable threshold for 0.5/hour cannot be statistically derived, so the zero false alarm point is reported instead.
- **Cross-speaker Standard Deviation**: Measures the stability of personalized methods across user differences.
- **Pseudo-Label Quality**: False positive rate in pseudo-positives and false negative rate in pseudo-negatives, directly measuring the signal-to-noise ratio of the labeling loop.
- **System Side**: Average power consumption (decomposed in Fig. 9 into data preparation, Cluster power-on, MFCC, NN inference, IO, idle), training latency/memory/energy (Table IV), and daily energy consumption curves vs. sampling interval (Fig. 10).

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Four comparison objects: pretrained ([13]'s frozen features plus prototype classification, i.e., current SOTA personalized KWS scheme), self (this paper's self-learning, two $\tau_L$ settings), augment ([31]'s data augmentation fine-tuning baseline, DEMAND noise, SNR 0-5 random, synthesized 90 positives 90 negatives), oracle (upper bound with all true labels in the adaptation set). Core results (Table III, FAR=0.5/h, 3-sample initialization):

**HeySnips**: ResNet15 rises from 87.2% to 93.7% (self(0.4,0.9), +6.4%); DS-CNN-L from 84.4% to 91.6% (self(0.3,0.9), +7.2%); DS-CNN-M from 76.8% to 88.1% (+11.4%); DS-CNN-S from 62.5% to 81.6% (+19.2%, the largest increase in the paper). Cross-speaker standard deviation generally narrows (e.g., DS-CNN-S drops from 30.6 to 23.6), indicating that self-learning not only improves accuracy but also stability. The augment baseline is lower than pretrained across the board (ResNet15 -15.6%, DS-CNN-S -11.2%), confirming the phenomenon observed in [13] that "naive fine-tuning with a few samples drops accuracy by 21%"—**fine-tuning with only 3 augmented samples yields negative returns at this scale; unsupervised environmental data is the correct solution**. This is the strongest argument for the existence of this paper's method. The oracle upper bound is 98.4%-100%; the gap between self and oracle is explained by label noise. Regarding label quality, ResNet15's pseudo-negative error rate is only 2% (DS-CNN-S up to 7%), and pseudo-positive error rate is 1-3% (DS-CNN-S up to 19%).

**HeySnapdragon**: ResNet15 from 82.6% to 94.6% (self(0.3,0.9), +11.9%); DS-CNN-L from 81.4% to 93.1%; DS-CNN-M from 77.2% to 93.3% (+16.1%); DS-CNN-S from 68.8% to 84.8% (+16.0%). A phenomenon worth expanding on: the pseudo-positive error rate on this set surges to 48%-84% (negative flip only 1%). The reason lies in the dataset composition—HeySnapdragon has fewer positives (only 462 in the adaptation set) and a harder distribution, yet accuracy still increases significantly. The paper explains this counter-intuitive result using the soft constraints of triplet loss (see Innovation 1). The augment baseline is unexpectedly effective on this set (ResNet15 reaches 99.2%, +16.6%, the highest single-model score in the paper). The paper honestly reports this "opponent wins a round" result and points out that it is consistent with the analysis in the original [31] paper, but emphasizes that on average, the augmentation scheme is unstable (loses completely on HeySnips).

**HeySnips-REC Real Acoustic Environment** (Fig. 7): Pre-trained DS-CNN-L 60%, leading DS-CNN-M/S by 8.5%/14.8%; after self-learning, DS-CNN-L reaches 73.4% (+13.3%, written as 73.3% in the abstract), DS-CNN-M +19.5%, DS-CNN-S +11.9%. ResNet15 is only 57.4%, 13.6% lower than DS-CNN-M—the paper attributes this to its initial model being weaker under these noise conditions (architectural noise robustness issue). This suggests that **rankings on clean sets (ResNet15 is strongest) will flip under real noise**; model selection cannot rely solely on public sets. The per-speaker statistics in Fig. 8 further expose: some speakers (e.g., speaker 0) had fewer than 10 pseudo-positives, so training was never triggered; for individual speakers, accuracy dropped after self-learning (unrelated to user identity and label error rate); oracle also dropped points for individual speakers; in some cases, self-learning even exceeded oracle by +43% (speakers 8-11, DS-CNN-S)—the paper admits that this anomalous phenomenon in the extremely low data volume interval requires subsequent analysis and does not force an explanation.

**System Energy Consumption** (Fig. 9): The always-on labeling task has an average整机 (whole machine) power consumption of 6.1 mW (DS-CNN-S) to 8.2 mW (ResNet15), with a duty cycle of 4%-12%. The decomposition is very informative: ResNet15's NN inference accounts for 36.8% of total power consumption, while DS-CNN-L/M/S drop to 14.6%/5.8%/2.5% respectively; for the two smallest models, the MFCC frontend (83 µJ) consumes more energy than NN inference—**the smaller the model, the more the bottleneck is feature extraction rather than inference**; and the largest chunk is actually the idle stage (audio decoding and buffering), accounting for 70% on DS-CNN-S. The microphone itself is 0.67 mW always-on. In silent sleep mode (microphone 18 µW plus interrupt wakeup, GAP9 deep sleep 45 µW, wakeup only 3.2ms), estimating 10% of time with sound in the environment, the average整机 power consumption drops to 0.66-0.88 mW, corresponding to battery life of 7.5 months (DS-CNN-S) to 5.6 months (ResNet15) (battery capacity assumption not reported in the paper).

**Training Cost** (Table IV, GAP9 @ 370MHz/0.8V, HeySnips-REC scenario): DS-CNN-S full-parameter training 20.6s, 1.5J; DS-CNN-M 68.2s, 4.7J; DS-CNN-L 172.8s (approx. 2.9 minutes), 11.7J; ResNet15 1339.3s (approx. 22 minutes), 86.8J—DS-CNN-L is 7.75 times faster than ResNet15 (written as 7.5 times in the abstract). External RAM overhead: on DS-CNN-S, external RAM energy accounts for 17.6% of total training energy, and read/write only accounts for 11.7% of its bandwidth, with an average power consumption of 12.8 mW. Fig. 10 provides the sustainability criterion: triggering training once every 400 samples collected, the critical sampling interval where training daily energy is 10 times lower than labeling energy is—DS-CNN-S saves one new sample every 6.1s, DS-CNN-M 18.8s, DS-CNN-L 43.6s, ResNet15 262s. That is, for small and medium models, even harvesting data quite densely, training energy consumption is negligible compared to always-on labeling; ResNet15 either needs to lower the sampling frequency or only train partial layers.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

**Threshold Parameter Scan** (Fig. 5, $\tau_L \in \{0.1..0.5\} \times \tau_H \in \{0.6..1.2\}$): Accuracy remains almost unchanged when $\tau_H$ is in the range of 0.7-1.1. The reason is that when $\tau_H$ is lowered, the number of false negatives grows at the same rate as the size of the pseudo-negative set, and the proportion of mislabeled negatives remains approximately constant—this "insensitivity" reduces the burden of field tuning. $\tau_L$, however, is sensitive and fails at both ends: at 0.5, too many false positives cause accuracy to drop (HeySnips); at 0.1-0.2, too few pseudo-positives lead to insufficient training set diversity (e.g., DS-CNN-M on HeySnapdragon). The final setting is $\tau_L \in \{0.3, 0.4\}, \tau_H=0.9$.

**Time Filtering and Window Step Size** (Fig. 6, using full test set: 50 speakers on HeySnapdragon, 61 speakers with at least 11 utterances on HeySnips): Shrinking the step size from 0.25s to 0.125s increases HeySnips accuracy by a maximum of +16% and HeySnapdragon by +4.7% (when $\alpha=1$); shrinking further to 0.0625s only increases it by another 6%/2% but doubles inference energy—0.125s (1/8 of window length) is selected as the accuracy-energy sweet spot. The dataset dependency of filter length is interesting: HeySnips peaks at $\alpha=2$ or 3 (the difference between the two is negligible), while HeySnapdragon is best at $\alpha=1$. The paper's explanation is that "Hey Snips" is pronounced on average shorter and can fit completely into continuous sliding windows, while the longer "Hey Snapdragon" requires shorter filter memory to avoid cross-window blurring. This reveals that filter length essentially matches the timescale of the keyword duration.

**Quantization**: 8-bit PTQ (4-sample calibration) causes no accuracy loss (Sec. V-B, following [13] observation), supporting the training-inference mixed-precision closed loop.

## Main Contributions

1. **The first self-learning framework for personalized KWS on ultra-low-power sensor nodes**: Incrementally fine-tunes the feature extractor using unlabeled data from the target environment after deployment, breaking the accuracy ceiling of frozen feature extractors. On two public datasets, it achieves a maximum improvement of +19.2% (DS-CNN-S, HeySnips) and +16.0% (HeySnapdragon) relative to few-shot SOTA (pre-trained plus prototype scheme [13]). ResNet15 reaches 93.7%/94.6% with only 3 initialization samples.
2. **A pseudo-labeling strategy for variable-length continuous audio**: Sliding window min + low-pass filtering + dual-threshold dead zone, with all parameters ($\alpha, Th_L, Th_H$) automatically calibrated on-device from 6 recordings, requiring no manual tuning or server.
3. **The first complete system-level deployment and energy account**: Always-on real-time labeling on a real node with Vesper microphone plus GAP9, with average power consumption of 6.1-8.2 mW (sleep mode 0.66-0.88 mW, battery life in months); first to provide estimates of on-device training latency/memory/energy (Table IV), and to provide a sampling interval criterion where "training energy = labeling energy/10" (6.1s-262s, depending on model scale).
4. **Methodological side products**: Proves that the soft constraints of triplet loss, combined with anchored true-value samples, can still achieve positive returns under extreme label noise with pseudo-positive error rates up to 84%; proves that fine-tuning with only 3 augmented samples yields negative returns on HeySnips, thereby arguing that unsupervised environmental data is irreplaceable.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Supports only a single new class**: The entire methodology is developed around "single wake word plus unknown." Scenarios with multiple new classes or continual learning where classes increase over time are left for future work (admitted by the authors in the conclusion). Under multiple classes, the overlap of distance distributions between prototypes would directly impact the calibration logic of the dual-threshold dead zone; the current Eq. 6/7 only defines distance criteria to a single positive class prototype.
- **No validation set, no early stopping**: Training runs for a fixed number of epochs and returns the last set of weights (Sec. IV-C). When the pseudo-labeled data distribution is skewed or the quantity is just above the threshold, it cannot prevent overfitting to the pseudo-labeled set—Fig. 8 shows that for some speakers, accuracy drops after self-learning (magnitude usually less than 10%) and is unrelated to label error rate. The paper admits it cannot explain this and leaves it for subsequent analysis. This is the most substantial shadow on the method's robustness.
- **Strong dependence on labeling loop quality**: The ceiling of the entire method is determined by the discriminative power of the pre-trained model in the new domain. On HeySnapdragon, the pseudo-positive error rate is 48%-84%. Although it withstood this thanks to triplet soft constraints, the margin is unsettling. If the deployment domain shift is more extreme (e.g., far-field, strong noise), the dead zone might narrow to the point where no pseudo-positives are collected (speakers in HeySnips-REC already appeared where fewer than 10 positives were gathered, and training was not triggered).
- **Imbalanced training cost for ResNet15**: 1339.3s latency, 58.64MB activation memory, 86.8J energy (Table IV); the 10x energy interval requires a 262s sampling interval; and accuracy is the lowest on the real noise set (57.4%)—large models have the worst cost-performance ratio in this framework. The authors suggest incremental training for partial layers but did not experiment with it.
- **Naive full-parameter updates**: Does not integrate memory-reducing techniques like TinyTL-style bias updates or sparse updates. Activation memory overflows entirely to external RAM (up to 58.64MB), which is not friendly to lower-cost nodes without external RAM.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Decoupling of accuracy and system measurements**: Learning experiments were all run on a server; the on-device side only measured the labeling task. The latency/energy of training were estimates (PULPTrainLib routines plus 8-core half-precision assumption). The paper does not report an end-to-end demonstration of "completing training on the node and refreshing the model." This is the biggest gap with its self-positioning as a "system paper."
- **Self-collected dataset scale is too small**: HeySnips-REC negative samples are only 11.6 minutes long, so only the FAR=0 operating point can be reported, which cannot be directly horizontally compared with the 0.5/h of public sets. Moreover, the playback-recording pipeline introduces speaker and room channel distortions, and the distribution still differs from real human voice direct transmission. Single room, single microphone model, single playback volume—generalization evidence is weak.
- **Narrow comparison scope**: No direct comparison with contemporary methods like dummy prototype [8] or cosine prototype [9] for open-set few-shot learning (comparison objects are mainly own previous work [13], augmentation baseline [31], and oracle upper bound). The augment baseline's ResNet15 reached 99.2% on HeySnapdragon, surpassing self's 94.6%. The paper only brushes this off with "on average more stable" and does not dig deep into the conditional boundaries where the two are complementary.
- **Undisclosed assumptions for system numbers**: The battery capacity assumption for the monthly battery life is not reported in the paper; the 10% acoustic activity rate estimate for sleep mode has not been verified by long-term measurements.
- **Calibration robustness not verified**: The variance of estimating $dist_n$ with K=3 negative samples is large (the negative space itself is open-set). The paper does not report the sensitivity of the calibrated thresholds to the order of negative sample selection.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Extension to multiple classes and continual learning**: The next step explicitly stated by the authors—new classes added over time, dynamic growth of the prototype set. This requires solving dead-zone calibration under multiple prototypes and catastrophic forgetting of old classes (possibly borrowing ideas from LifeLearner-style meta-continual learning).
- **Smarter on-device training**: Partial layer fine-tuning (directly alleviating the 262s interval for ResNet15), sparse updates, bias-level adaptation (TinyTL/TinyOL route) are orthogonal to this paper's self-learning closed loop and can be superimposed. The authors also explicitly point out that these lightweight backpropagation schemes "can be integrated."
- **Adaptive control of label quality**: Introducing uncertainty estimation or confidence weighting (e.g., weighting triplet by the distance of $dist_f$ to the threshold), online recalibration of thresholds, and active rejection for high-noise domains, turning the dead zone from a static parameter into a dynamically adjusted quantity that drifts with the domain.
- **Wider deployment validation**: Different microphones (listed by authors), real far-field, multiple rooms, multiple speakers co-existing, and online collection of calibration negatives in streaming scenarios.
- **End-to-end measurement of the training closed loop**: Actually running incremental training on the node (including external RAM power-on and model refresh), completing the last piece of the self-learning sensor demonstration. Furthermore, the training trigger could also be made event-driven (e.g., triggering once every $N_{pB}$ samples collected, replacing one large training with multiple small-step trainings), which better fits the arrival process of streaming data.
