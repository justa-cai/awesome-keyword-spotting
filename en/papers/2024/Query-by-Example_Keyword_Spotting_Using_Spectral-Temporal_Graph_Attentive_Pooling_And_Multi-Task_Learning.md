## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) systems are tasked with detecting predefined keywords within continuous real-time audio streams, serving as the entry point for user interaction with voice assistants. Traditional KWS keywords are factory-presets: models are trained for one or a few fixed phrases, and the vocabulary remains immutable after deployment. Customized KWS, however, allows users to define their own wake words, which is obviously valuable for personalized interaction—users can choose a phrase that is catchy or has personal significance, rather than sharing the same wake word with millions of other users worldwide.

However, customization is extremely costly under the engineering paradigm of traditional KWS. The paper highlights the core contradiction in the introduction: in addition to the inherent requirements of small memory footprint and low latency for KWS, customization introduces a distribution-level problem—user-defined keyword phrases may not align with the distribution of the training data, leading to significant degradation in detection performance. In other words, a classifier trained for a fixed word has no output neurons available for unseen words and no training data for those words to learn from. This is a structural defect of traditional closed-vocabulary KWS that cannot be solved by hyperparameter tuning.

Query-by-Example (QbyE) is the paradigm-level answer to this problem: the KWS system generates fixed-length embeddings from a few keyword audio samples provided by the user, and then uses similarity in the embedding space to measure the distance between test samples and registered keywords, thereby determining whether the keyword has appeared (Section 1). In QbyE, users register a few sentences as "query examples," and the system recognizes words by comparing them to these examples. This paradigm shifts the process from "retraining a model for each new word" to "register and use," with the cost being that all pressure is transferred to the quality of the embedding space: this space must possess two types of generalization capabilities—vocabulary generalization (keywords evaluated were never seen during training) and speaker generalization (the person registering the keyword may not be the same person actually saying the wake word).

The paper's experimental anchor is a large-scale internal evaluation set with 629 speakers (Abstract). The headline result is: under the proposed framework, the hardware-efficient LiCoNet encoder achieves performance comparable to the heavy Conformer with a 13x efficiency advantage (FRR 1.98% vs. 1.63% at the 0.3 FAs/Hr operating point, Table 1).

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The introduction categorizes existing work into four technical routes, each with specific shortcomings:

- **Data rigidity of predefined KWS**. Predefined KWS with encoder-decoder structures (acoustic encoders produce senone-level posteriors, and decoders are trained independently to interpret encoder outputs as keyword phrases, literature [2][3], i.e., the cFSMN and TD-BHNN series) and end-to-end models that directly output detection results ([4][5]) achieve high detection performance. However, the paper explicitly points out that training dedicated models for predefined keywords usually requires substantial target data to ensure effectiveness. Each word requires data and training, which fundamentally conflicts with the need for "users to casually customize."
- **Early QbyE based on pre-trained ASR**. Early QbyE relied on a pre-trained ASR acoustic model to generate phoneme posteriors for audio streams, and then used Dynamic Time Warping (DTW, a classic algorithm for non-linear alignment of two unequal-length time series to calculate distance) to measure the similarity of posterior sequences between registered keywords and test samples ([6][7][8]); the CTC route uses CTC forward computation to calculate the likelihood of each keyword hypothesis and aggregates detection scores from the N-best list ([9][10]). The fundamental weakness of these methods is that the similarity metric is inherited from general ASR and is not trained end-to-end for the "keyword discrimination" task—the quality of phoneme posteriors is constrained by the ASR model itself, DTW only performs template alignment, and no part of the pipeline has been shaped by a discriminative objective such as "two different words should be far apart, and the same word should be close together."
- **Discriminative embedding encoders and attention routes**. Chen et al. proposed an LSTM encoder to learn acoustic embeddings for custom keywords and achieved good results ([1]); Huang et al.'s work at ICASSP 2021 ([11]) further introduced multi-head attention to extract features, normalized multi-head attention modules for feature aggregation, and added SoftTriplet loss to enhance discriminability, improving both performance and capability. However, attention-based models come with significant computational demands, posing a high runtime memory burden, making them unsuitable for always-on KWS systems. This is a fatal flaw in terms of efficiency.
- **MLPMixer alternative route**. Huang et al. later replaced the attention mechanism with the MLPMixer architecture for QbyE (QbyE-MLPMixer, [12]), which improved both performance and efficiency compared to ViT, but the frequent use of matrix transpose operations still causes significant computational overhead on hardware (as stated in the paper's introduction). Transpose operations are often not a free lunch on general accelerators and edge NPUs.

On top of this, the paper points out two embedding quality issues generally ignored by previous QbyE works:

- **Speaker coupling assumption**. Existing QbyE methods often assume that the system user is the same as the enrolled speaker (existing approaches in QbyE KWS often assume that the system user is the same as the enrolled speaker, Section 2.4.2). Acoustic differences at the speaker level, such as pitch, timbre, and pronunciation habits, have a significant impact on speech modeling (Section 2.4.2). In reality, home devices are woken up by multiple family members; it is the norm, not the exception, for the registrant and the speaker to be different. Once this assumption is broken, a systematic distribution mismatch appears between the registration embedding and the query embedding.
- **Information entanglement in single-task supervision**. When trained only with word-level classification loss, the word information and speaker information in the embeddings learned by the encoder are entangled—the classification head only needs to distinguish 1002 classes and does not care whether it relies on phonetic content or vocal characteristics. Meanwhile, word-level labels are too coarse-grained, and the frame-level phonetic structure is not explicitly supervised, which is unfavorable for generalizing to unseen words outside the training vocabulary.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, this paper aims to simultaneously tackle three mutually constraining challenges: First, under the condition of zero training data for target words, design a metric learning scheme so that the embedding space maintains a discriminative structure for unseen keywords (open-vocabulary generalization); Second, disentangle the speaker factor from the embedding without losing linguistic information (speaker invariance); Third, accomplish the above two things while meeting the always-on compute budget—i.e., maximizing the potential of simpler models (repeatedly emphasized in the paper's abstract). The acceptance criteria are clear: at the 0.3 FAs/Hr operating point, LiCoNet's FRR should approach Conformer's, while having one order of magnitude less computation (46.5M vs. 642.2M FLOPs, Table 2).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall architecture adopts an encoder-decoder structure during training and embedding comparison during testing (Figure 1). During training, the encoder receives acoustic features of a word phrase, produces a sequential representation, and the pooling layer compresses it into a compact and information-rich fixed-length embedding as a dimensionality reduction technique, which is then fed into the decoder for classification supervision. During testing, the user registers several samples of custom keywords, and detection extracts embeddings of test speech within a sliding window, comparing the similarity with registered sample embeddings to finally determine whether the keyword has appeared (Section 2.1).

The design of "using a classification head during training and keeping only the encoder and pooling layer for deployment" has a clear motivation: the classification head is merely a carrier of supervision signals during training and is completely discarded during inference; keywords outside the vocabulary are naturally supported by the proximity in the embedding space—this is exactly the difference between QbyE and predefined KWS. The position of the pooling layer determines it as the bottleneck from sequence information to fixed-length embedding; what is retained and what is discarded in the embedding is largely determined by the pooling strategy, which is also why the paper treats pooling as an independent research dimension (Section 2.3 begins by emphasizing that pooling plays the role of distilling key information from sequence data in neural architectures).

Three architectures are examined on the encoder side (Section 2.2), each choice having deep significance:

- **LiCoNet** (Linearized Convolution Network, [16]): A hardware-efficient architecture designed specifically for the KWS task, built as a streaming convolutional network on a MobileNetV2 backbone ([17]), using equivalent linear operators to ensure efficient inference. Each LiCo-Block is a bottleneck structure consisting of three 1D convolutional layers: the first layer uses a streaming convolution with a kernel size greater than 1, followed by two pointwise convolutions. It represents the "efficiency-first" engineering route.
- **Conformer** (Convolution-augmented Transformer, [18]): Each block contains four serially connected modules—feed-forward, self-attention, convolution, and feed-forward again. The convolution module captures position-specific local features, and the self-attention module captures content-based global interactions (Section 2.2.2). It represents the upper limit of sequence modeling capability and serves as the performance ceiling reference.
- **ECAPA-TDNN**: Composed of 1D convolution, three 1D SE-Res2Blocks, 1D convolution, attentive statistics pooling, and a fully connected layer. ReLU non-linearity and batch normalization are connected after each layer of the SE-Res2Block, and the embedding is taken from the fully connected layer (Section 2.2.3). The deep reason for choosing it is that the QbyE KWS training and testing process is highly similar to speaker verification (SV)—both involve "registering a few samples and comparing embedding similarity" (original text in Section 2.2.3), and ECAPA-TDNN is the most commonly used backbone for the SV task ([22]). Task isomorphism allows architectural experience to be directly transferred.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The loss function system is the methodological core of this article, consisting of a mixed loss composed of word-level discriminative loss, reverse speaker loss, and phoneme loss (Section 2.4).

**Word-level discrimination I: AAM Loss** (Additive Angular Margin, Equation 1):

$$L_{aam} = -\log \frac{e^{s\cos(\theta_{y_i}+m)}}{e^{s\cos(\theta_{y_i}+m)} + \sum_{j=1,j\neq y_i}^{C} e^{s\cos\theta_j}}$$

where $\theta_j$ is the angle between feature $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and the $j$-th column $w_j$ of the fully connected weight of the last layer, $C$ is the number of classes, $s$ is the scaling factor, and $m$ is the additive angular margin. In the experiment, $m=0.2$ and $s=32$ (Section 3.2). AAM is widely used in face recognition (ArcFace, [15]) and speaker recognition ([14]). The reason for choosing it is strongly related to the testing protocol: cosine distance is used for scoring during testing (Section 3.2), and AAM exactly pulls apart class intervals directly on the cosine angle geometry—the training objective and test metric are isomorphic, and adding intervals to the angle is directly optimizing the quantity used during deployment. In contrast, ordinary cross-entropy only indirectly shapes the embedding space via softmax, and its inter-class geometry has no explicit constraints.

**Word-level discrimination II: SoftTriplet Loss** (Equation 2):

$$L_{st}(x_i) = -\log \frac{\exp(\lambda(S'_{i,y_i}-\delta))}{\exp(\lambda(S'_{i,y_i}-\delta)) + \sum_{j}' \exp(\lambda S_{i,j})}$$

where $S_{i,c}$ is the similarity between feature $x_i$ and class $c$, $\delta$ is the predefined margin, and $\lambda$ is the scaling factor. The experiment takes $\lambda=60$, $\delta=0.03$, and $K=10$ (Section 3.2; $K$ is the number of centers per class in the prototype work SoftTriple [25] for SoftTriplet, which is not elaborated in the main text of this paper). SoftTriplet combines triplet loss with softmax loss; its essence is to replace the single center with multiple centers within a class ($K=10$), thereby explicitly modeling intra-class multi-modality—when the same word is spoken by people with different accents, genders, and speeds, it is not a single cluster in the embedding space, but multiple clusters. Triplet sampling is bypassed, making training stable and eliminating the need to pick anchors.

**Mixed Multi-Task Loss** (Section 2.4.4):

$$L(x, y) = L(x, y^w) - \eta L_{aam}(x, y^s) + \mu L_{aam}(x, y^p)$$

where $y=(y^w, y^s, y^p)$ are the word labels, speaker labels, and phoneme label sequences for $T$ frames, respectively, and $\eta$ and $\mu$ are scaling factors, taken as $0.1$ and $0.5$. The word-level term $L$ can be either AAM or SoftTriplet. The most notable feature in this formula is the **negative sign before the speaker term**: it does not aim to minimize the speaker classification loss, but to **maximize** it—achieved through a Gradient Reversal Layer (GRL, [26]). GRL is a parameter-free operator; it is an identity transformation during forward propagation and reverses the gradient during backpropagation (Section 2.4.2). Thus, for the same speaker classification head, the forward pass calculates the classification loss normally, but the backward pass injects the gradient of "how to distinguish speakers" in reverse into the encoder—the encoder is trained to "make the speaker classification head fail to distinguish as much as possible," i.e., actively erasing speaker information from the embedding. The phoneme term aggregates frame-level AAM loss over phoneme labels for all frames (Section 2.4.3), and the phoneme labels come from forced alignment of a pre-trained acoustic model (Section 3.1).

### Key Technical Innovation I: Spectral-Temporal Graph Attentive Pooling (GAP)

The pooling module is the first technical innovation of this paper. The paper compares two pooling methods:

- **ASP (Attentive Statistic Pooling, [23])**: Combines statistical pooling with an attention mechanism. Attention allows the model to dynamically weight the importance of different frames along the time dimension, thereby extracting task-relevant salient features (Section 2.3). Essentially, it is a 1D soft-weighted mean and variance statistic along the time dimension.
- **GAP (spectro-temporal Graph Attentive Pooling, [13][24])**: Uses graph neural networks to understand the complex relationships within spectral-temporal data. Its spectral attention, temporal attention, and spectral-temporal attention modules each contain three graph attention blocks, and each block internally contains a graph attention network and graph pooling (Section 2.3). In the experiment, the pooling ratios for the three modules are taken as 0.71, 0.86, and 0.71, respectively (Section 3.2). This configuration allows the model to dynamically adapt the pooling process and extract key features.

Mechanism interpretation of why GAP is strong: ASP treats the time dimension as a 1D sequence for soft weighting, retaining all frames and only adjusting weights; GAP treats positions on the spectral-temporal feature map as graph nodes, learns relationships between nodes using graph attention, and then **hardly prunes** unimportant nodes by a certain proportion using graph pooling (retention ratios of 0.71/0.86/0.71 mean that each module discards about 30%, slightly more than 10%, and 30% of nodes, respectively). This is a generational difference from "soft-weighted compression" to "relationship modeling plus content-adaptive selection": not all time-frequency positions in speech carry keyword information (silent segments and indistinguishable voiced segments exist in large quantities), and hard selection can directly eliminate these redundant nodes rather than just suppressing their weights. This module originates from the end-to-end spectral-temporal graph attention network by Tak et al. for speaker verification anti-spoofing and voice deepfake detection ([13]). The paper migrates it into QbyE and verifies the effectiveness of the migration in experiments—under mixed loss with word-level AAM, GAP further reduces FRR by 18.5%, 51.1%, and 32.2% on ECAPA-TDNN, Conformer, and LiCoNet, respectively, compared to ASP (Section 3.3, calculated based on Table 1). The paper also specifically points out that this magnitude of improvement is consistent with observations in the speaker verification field ([13]), implying that the discriminative power enhancement brought by graph pooling is consistent across tasks.

### Key Technical Innovation II: Reverse Speaker Loss (GRL Domain Adversarial Speaker Disentanglement)

The argument chain in Section 2.4.2 is worth expanding in full: speaker-related acoustic variations (pitch, tone, pronunciation differences) have a significant impact on speech modeling; existing QbyE methods assume that the user is the same person as the registrant; to disentangle speaker dependence in applications, a reverse speaker loss is added to the method, with the goal of learning speaker-independent embeddings; the specific approach is to design a reverse speaker loss based on AAM, which **maximizes** the speaker classification loss during training through a Gradient Reversal Layer.

The engineering significance lies in solving the registration-query speaker mismatch: if the registration embedding encodes a large amount of the registrant's vocal characteristics, when another person says the same word, the embedding distance will be polluted by speaker differences, making similarity scoring prone to missing true wake-ups and falsely accepting similar words spoken by the registrant. After adversarially stripping away speaker information, the embedding mainly carries the linguistic content of the word, making cross-speaker matching stable. The testing protocol itself echoes this: registrations are randomly picked from any speakers (3 utterances of any speakers were randomly picked as enrollments, Section 3.2), deliberately creating a scenario where the registrant and the querier are different people.

Magnitude of effect (Table 1): Adding reverse speaker loss to the mixed loss configuration with word-level AAM, Conformer's FRR drops from 8.26% to 4.88%, a relative decrease of 40.9% (number given in Section 3.3 of the paper); ECAPA-TDNN drops from 12.09% to 10.81% (calculated relative decrease of about 10.6%), and LiCoNet drops from 9.75% to 7.49% (calculated about 23.2%). A fascinating detail is that Conformer benefits the most and ECAPA-TDNN benefits the least—a reasonable interpretation is that self-attention models have large capacity and are most likely to encode speaker characteristics into the embedding (attention can easily capture global statistical features like voice), so the benefit after stripping is the largest; whereas ECAPA-TDNN's architectural inductive bias is originally designed for "distinguishing speakers" (it is an SV backbone), so GRL is essentially fighting against the architecture's nature, resulting in the most limited improvement. This cross-architecture difference itself is circumstantial evidence that "the amount of speaker information in the embedding is positively correlated with architecture capacity."

### Key Technical Innovation III: Multi-Task Supervision with Phoneme Context

Phonemes are the basic speech units that make up spoken words (Section 2.4.3). A dedicated phoneme classifier is added to the training framework, using AAM loss for phoneme classification, with the loss aggregated from frame-level AAM over phoneme labels for all frames (Section 2.4.3).

Why add phoneme-level supervision in addition to word-level supervision: Word-level labels only tell the model "this is word A, not word B"; a 1000-class classification signal provides very sparse constraints on frame-level representations; phoneme labels break each word into dozens of fine-grained frame-level supervision signals, forcing the encoder's intermediate representations to learn composable sub-word speech structures. And the evaluation keywords do not overlap at all with the training vocabulary (Section 3.1); unseen words can only be located by "spelling out phoneme-level structures"—phoneme supervision is essentially paving the way for open-vocabulary generalization. In Table 1, adding phoneme loss on top of reverse speaker loss, the FRR of all three encoders decreases further (ECAPA 10.81% to 8.95%, Conformer 4.88% to 4.77%, LiCoNet 7.49% to 5.36%, ASP configuration), and LiCoNet's relative benefit is calculated to be about 28.4%, the most obvious among the three architectures.

### Key Technical Innovation IV: SoftTriplet as the Final Form of Word-Level Metric

Under the configuration of mixed loss plus GAP, replacing word-level AAM with SoftTriplet achieves the best performance on all models, where LiCoNet's FRR drops from 3.63% to 1.98%, a relative decrease of 45.4% (Section 3.3). The paper's own explanation is: SoftTriplet has strong **generalization ability** in capturing potential unseen intra-variance in the evaluation data (Section 3.3). Translating this sentence into mechanism language: evaluation words were never seen during training, and their intra-class variance structure cannot be memorized class-by-class by the classification head like training words; AAM relies on global angular margins to pull apart inter-class distances, which is powerless against intra-class multi-modality of unseen classes; SoftTriplet's multi-center structure ($K=10$) naturally assumes that each class is internally multi-clustered, and this structural prior can be transferred zero-shot to unseen words, thus being more friendly to open-vocabulary evaluation. This ablation dimension alone contributes nearly half of the relative decrease on LiCoNet, making it the most cost-effective cut in the entire framework.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

The differences with the four existing routes can be summarized into a 3D comparison:

- **Relative to ASR+DTW/CTC routes ([6]-[10])**: Shifts from "inheriting metrics from general ASR" to "training the embedding space end-to-end for keyword discrimination," with similarity itself becoming the learning target (AAM/SoftTriplet directly optimizes cosine geometry), and DTW's task-agnostic template alignment is completely replaced.
- **Relative to multi-head attention QbyE ([11])**: Retains the idea of its SoftTriplet discriminative loss, but replaces heavy multi-head attention encoding and aggregation with lightweight convolution plus graph pooling, targeting the runtime memory constraints of always-on systems—this is also the main criticism of [11] in the paper's introduction.
- **Relative to MLPMixer route ([12])**: Uses a combination of equivalent linear operators (LiCoNet) and graph attention pooling to avoid frequent matrix transposes, also a choice oriented towards hardware deployment.
- **Relative to predefined KWS ([2]-[5])**: Register and use, no target word data required; the cost is that two new problems must be solved: open vocabulary and speaker mismatch, which are addressed by phoneme multi-task plus SoftTriplet and reverse speaker loss, respectively.

The truly unique combination point lies in: **transferring the three-piece set verified in the speaker verification field—the experience of ECAPA backbone, graph attention pooling (anti-spoofing), and AAM angular margin metric—entirely into QbyE, and using the counter-intuitive multi-task design of "maximizing speaker loss" to simultaneously complete information enhancement (phoneme) and information stripping (speaker)**. The horizontal comparison of the three encoders under the same framework provides clean evidence of "how much the framework itself can lift the score."

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Training data uses LibriSpeech ([27]): 960 hours, 16 kHz sampling, English audiobook reading corpus with transcribed text (Section 3.1). A pre-trained acoustic model is used for forced alignment (i.e., using known text to constrain the acoustic model to align each word's boundaries frame by frame) to cut utterances into individual words, and each word segment is unified to 2 seconds in length through bilateral cropping or zero-padding. Acoustic features are 40-dimensional log Mel filter bank energies, with a 25 ms window length and 10 ms frame shift.

The evaluation uses an internal aggregated de-identified keyword dataset: 275.7k positive utterances from 629 speakers; negative examples total 200 hours in duration (Section 3.1). The evaluation set is completely separated from the training data, and the keywords used in the evaluation are guaranteed not to have appeared in the training set—the paper emphasizes this principled alignment setting for custom keyword detection, and the large-scale data volume ensures the robustness of the experimental conclusions (Section 3.1).

Model and training configurations (Section 3.2): LiCoNet stacks five LiCo-Blocks, expansion factor 6, convolution kernel 5; Conformer has 2 heads per multi-head self-attention layer, 128 input/output nodes, feed-forward linear hidden layer dimension 192, convolution module kernel 7; ECAPA-TDNN has 128 channels for convolutional layers, bottleneck dimension 64 for SE-Block and attention modules, and scale dimension 8 for Res2Block. All models train to predict 1002 targets (top-1k frequent words, Silence, Unknown); batch size 64, 8 GPUs, 40 epochs; Adam optimizer with triangular2 cyclic learning rate strategy ([29]), rising from 1e-8 to 1e-3 in a ring, with 20k warm-up updates. During testing, 3 sentences from any speaker are randomly taken for registration, and the query is compared with the 3 registered embeddings one by one to calculate cosine distance, taking the minimum distance and comparing it with a threshold for detection decision (Section 3.2).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric uses the Detection Error Tradeoff (DET) curve: the x-axis is the number of false accepts per hour (FAs/hr), and the y-axis is the false reject rate (FRR); main results report the FRR at the 0.3 FAs/Hr operating point (Table 1 title, Figure 2).

Interpretation of the rationale for selection: KWS is an all-day running detection task, and the two failure modes directly perceived by users are "waking up falsely a few times a day" (corresponding to FAs/hr) and "no response when called" (corresponding to FRR). The DET curve is a complete trade-off characterization of this cost, more aligned with deployment semantics than a single accuracy rate. Reporting a single point at 0.3 FAs/Hr is equivalent to looking at missed detections under the strict constraint of keeping false wake-ups to "single digits per day"—this is the magnitude of the operating interval commonly used in voice assistant products (this is an engineering common-sense interpretation; the paper does not state the reason for choosing the operating point). Figure 2 left shows the DET curves for Conformer under various loss and pooling combinations (the curve is annotated with five endpoints: 11.98%, 8.26%, 4.77%, 2.33%, 1.63%), and the right side shows the curve comparison of the three encoders under the optimal multi-task configuration (ECAPA-TDNN 5.58%, LiCoNet 1.98%, Conformer 1.63%).

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table 1 is the core result table, giving the FRR (%) of 3 encoders × 6 configurations at 0.3 FAs/Hr:

| Encoder | Word CE (ASP) | Word AAM (ASP) | + Speaker (ASP) | + Phoneme (ASP) | + Phoneme (GAP) | SoftTriplet + Phoneme (GAP) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ECAPA-TDNN | 16.28 | 12.09 | 10.81 | 8.95 | 7.29 | 5.58 |
| Conformer | 11.98 | 8.26 | 4.88 | 4.77 | 2.33 | 1.63 |
| LiCoNet | 13.20 | 9.75 | 7.49 | 5.36 | 3.63 | 1.98 |

Reading from left to right reveals a complete chain of improvement, with the relative decrease of each link (percentages reported in Section 3.3 of the paper, calculated and annotated where not reported based on Table 1):

- **CE to AAM (single task)**: ECAPA drops 25.7%, Conformer drops 31%, LiCoNet drops 26.1% (reported by paper). A single loss function replacement yields a relative decrease of more than a quarter, verifying the value of "training objective is isomorphic to cosine test metric."
- **Adding reverse speaker loss**: Conformer drops from 8.26% to 4.88%, a relative decrease of 40.9% (reported by paper); ECAPA about 10.6%, LiCoNet about 23.2% (calculated). The single-link benefit is the largest in the entire improvement chain.
- **Adding phoneme loss**: All three architectures continue to decrease (8.95/4.77/5.36), with LiCoNet's relative decrease about 28.4% (calculated).
- **ASP to GAP**: Under word AAM mixed loss, ECAPA drops 18.5%, Conformer drops 51.1%, LiCoNet drops 32.2% (reported by paper). The benefit of Conformer switching to GAP is exceptionally large, indicating that the features rich in global structure produced by the attention encoder are exactly the raw materials that graph attention is best at utilizing.
- **AAM to SoftTriplet**: All three architectures drop to their respective optima (5.58/1.63/1.98), with LiCoNet's relative decrease 45.4% (reported by paper).

Cumulative effect: LiCoNet drops from 13.20% (Word CE + ASP) all the way to 1.98%, a relative decrease of about 85% (calculated); Conformer drops from 11.98% to 1.63%, about 86% (calculated). This shows that the benefits of the framework's components are basically additive, with no link eating the benefits of another.

Efficiency dimension (Table 2, model size and computation for 2-second audio):

| Encoder | Parameters | FLOPs |
|---|---|---|
| ECAPA-TDNN | 540K | 39.1M |
| Conformer | 1.4M | 642.2M |
| LiCoNet | 694K | 46.5M |

The paper's conclusion is: ECAPA-TDNN is the most efficient but performance-limited (5.58%); Conformer has the largest model and significantly highest computational demand but dominates in capacity (1.63%); LiCoNet achieves an ideal balance of efficiency and effect—performance comparable to Conformer, computational cost at the same level as ECAPA-TDNN (Section 3.3). Calculated from Table 2, Conformer's FLOPs are about 13.8 times that of LiCoNet (642.2M vs. 46.5M), and parameters are about 2 times, which is the source of the abstract's "13x efficiency advantage"; and the FRR gap between the two is only 0.35 percentage points (1.98% vs. 1.63%). Additionally, the paper gives a qualitative but important scaling observation: shrinking Conformer results in significant performance degradation, while enlarging ECAPA-TDNN and LiCoNet only results in slight performance improvement (Section 3.3, specific numbers not reported by the paper)—attention architectures suffer from severe underfitting at small widths, while lightweight convolutional architectures are already at the capacity saturation point, and stacking more parameters is useless. This explains why the edge route should bet on linearized architectures like LiCoNet combined with a training framework to lift performance, rather than shrinking large models.

One point to note: the paper does not include external systems ([1][11][12] etc.) as baselines in the comparison table; all comparisons in Table 1 occur within the framework itself (self-comparison of different losses, pooling, encoders), and the DET curves also lack external anchors. Therefore, strictly speaking, this is a "framework-internal ablation-driven" comparison, rather than a direct horizontal table against published SOTA.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Table 1 and Figure 2 (left) together form a 3×6 ablation matrix, and the core findings can be summarized into five points:

1. **The impact of metric loss selection is greater than encoder selection**. Under the same ASP pooling and single-task setting, the relative improvement brought by switching CE to AAM (25.7%-31%) is comparable to or even greater than the improvement from switching ECAPA to Conformer (16.28% to 11.98%, about 26%, calculated), while the former is just switching a loss function. The design of the embedding space is the first variable of QbyE.
2. **Speaker disentanglement is the component with the largest single-link benefit**, and the benefit amplifies as encoder capacity increases (Conformer 40.9% vs. ECAPA about 10.6%). This leads to the inference that the amount of speaker information in the embedding is positively correlated with architecture capacity.
3. **The benefits of GAP are unevenly distributed across architectures**: Conformer benefits 51.1%, far exceeding ECAPA's 18.5%. Graph pooling requires structured feature maps to play its role, suggesting that there is an adaptation relationship between pooling strategy and encoder architecture, and it is not a plug-and-play universal gain.
4. **The benefits of SoftTriplet are largest on lightweight models** (LiCoNet 45.4%). The multi-center structural prior is a godsend for capacity-constrained models that cannot rely on model capacity to hard-memorize intra-class variance; for large-capacity models like Conformer, it is relatively icing on the cake (2.33% to 1.63%, calculated about 30%).
5. **Component benefits are additive and do not cancel each other out**, with the cumulative relative decrease from the worst configuration to the optimal configuration exceeding 80%, and at the same time, the gap between the three encoders under the optimal configuration is significantly compressed (under single-task ASP, the gap between ECAPA and Conformer is 4.3 percentage points, under the optimal configuration it is only 3.95 percentage points, and the gap between LiCoNet and Conformer is only 0.35 percentage points)—the role of the training framework is precisely to "lift the ceiling of simple models" (the core argument of the abstract and Section 3.3).

Parts not covered by ablation are also worth recording: the sensitivity of scaling factors $\eta$, $\mu$ (only a single point of 0.1/0.5 is reported), the basis for choosing the three pooling ratios of GAP (only a single point of 0.71/0.86/0.71 is reported), the K-value scan for SoftTriplet, and non-optimal combinations other than "Due to limited space, we only show the best results from SoftTriplet" (Section 3.2)—the paper does not report the complete grid.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed a complete QbyE KWS framework**: Spectral-temporal graph attentive pooling (GAP) plus multi-task learning of "word-phoneme information enhancement, speaker information stripping," systematically solving the two QbyE-specific pain points of open-vocabulary discrimination and speaker mismatch (Abstract, Section 2).
2. **Successfully migrated mature components from the speaker verification field to QbyE**: GAP comes from SV anti-spoofing ([13]), AAM comes from face and speaker recognition ([14][15]), and ECAPA-TDNN comes from SV itself. The migration is quantitatively verified as effective by the ablation in Table 1, providing a positive example for cross-task component reuse.
3. **Provided the first systematic verification of reverse speaker loss (GRL + AAM) in QbyE**: Breaking the "user is the registrant" assumption, a single component brings a relative FRR decrease of up to 40.9% (Section 3.3).
4. **Filled the gap of lightweight encoders with a training framework**: LiCoNet achieves comparable FRR of 1.98% vs. 1.63% to Conformer (Table 1) with an FLOPs advantage of about 13.8 times (calculated from Table 2, stated as 13x in the abstract), and shrinking Conformer results in significant degradation, while enlarging lightweight models only results in slight improvement (Section 3.3), providing a clear signal for architecture selection for edge-side always-on custom wake words: bet on linearized convolution plus training framework, rather than shrinking large models.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Inherent fragility of GRL adversarial training**: Maximizing speaker loss is essentially a minimax adversarial optimization, and training stability relies on the manually scaled $\eta=0.1$; if $\eta$ is too large, speaker stripping dominates optimization and word information is damaged; if too small, disentanglement is incomplete. The paper does not report the sensitivity of this parameter and training dynamics.
- **Dilemma of speaker invariance and discriminative information**: Stripping speaker information as noise may also cut off word discrimination clues related to voice (e.g., prosodic differences relying on fundamental frequency trajectories); the loss is limited in the English scenario, but when migrating to tonal languages (such as Chinese), the fundamental frequency carries tones rather than speaker identity, and stripping them all has the risk of collateral damage. The paper does not discuss this boundary.
- **Fixed 2-second word segment assumption**: Word segments in training and evaluation are unified to 2 seconds (Section 3.1); longer words or multi-word phrases will be cropped; the window length, step size, and streaming detection details of the sliding window during testing are not reported by the paper, and the latency characteristics of actual deployment cannot be inferred from the text.
- **GAP's own computational overhead is not reported separately**: The parameters and FLOPs in Table 2 are the encoder's account; the additional cost of the graph attention module (three modules, each with three graph attention blocks) is not broken out. The claim of "13x efficiency" is strictly a comparison at the encoder level. A lightweight encoder paired with a potentially not-so-light pooling layer may not have an equally optimistic total account, which the paper does not report.
- **Residual vocabulary closure**: The training target is 1002 classes (top-1k words, Silence, Unknown), and the discriminative structure of unseen words relies entirely on the generalization of SoftTriplet multi-center prior and phoneme supervision. Their failure modes (e.g., new words highly similar to the phoneme sequence of training words) are not analyzed.
- **Absolute gap of 0.35 percentage points remains under the optimal configuration**: Whether the gap between LiCoNet's 1.98% and Conformer's 1.63% at a strict operating point affects product experience depends on the target operating point, which the paper does not discuss.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Evaluation set is internal non-public data** (275.7k positive examples, 629 speakers, 200 hours of negative examples), making it impossible for the community to reproduce, and there are no comparison numbers on public QbyE benchmarks, discounting the external validity of the conclusions.
- **No direct baseline comparison with external systems**: There is no same-table comparison row with its strongest predecessors ([11] multi-head attention plus SoftTriplet, [12] MLPMixer), and their overhead defects can only be cited at the narrative level, making it impossible to quantify "how much better this framework is compared to published SOTA."
- **Single condition**: All experiments are trained on English reading corpus (LibriSpeech), with no noise, far-field, or multi-lingual evaluation; the registration protocol is fixed to random 3 sentences, and the sensitivity of the number of registration sentences and audio quality, the variance of cross-registration sampling, and statistical significance are not reported.
- **Efficiency metrics stop at FLOPs and parameters**: There are no measurements of latency, memory, and power consumption on real hardware, and the claim of "hardware efficiency" lacks endorsement from edge-side实测.
- **Incomplete ablation grid**: Due to space limitations, only the best results from SoftTriplet are shown (original text in Section 3.2), and hyperparameters such as $\eta$, $\mu$, pooling ratios, and K values are reported as single points; the DET x-axis in Figure 2 only goes up to 0.4 FAs/hr, and the behavior at lower false wake-up operating points (e.g., below 0.1 FAs/hr) is unknown.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Real edge-side verification and streaming**: Measure latency and power consumption on ARM/DSP/NPU, complete the streaming protocol for sliding window detection, and connect with VAD to provide end-to-end system metrics—this is a necessary step to solidify the "13x efficiency" claim into a product.
- **GAP overhead optimization**: Prune graph attention blocks with adaptive proportions or distillation, report the FLOPs bill for the pooling layer itself, and verify whether the total cost of lightweight encoder plus GAP is still significantly lower than the entire Conformer family.
- **Hyperparameter and uncertainty system scan**: Grid search and training stability analysis for $\eta/\mu$, pooling ratios, and K values, with variance reporting for multiple random seeds.
- **Robustness and multi-lingual expansion**: Add noise and reverberation data augmentation, far-field evaluation, and re-examine the boundary between speaker stripping and prosodic information when expanding to tonal languages and multi-lingual vocabularies.
- **Combination with self-supervised pre-training**: Use SSL pre-trained features or encoder initialization to further improve the capacity ceiling of small models, which may be another better ceiling route than "shrinking Conformer" (the paper has already proven that shrinking large models results in significant degradation).
- **Registration experience engineering**: Adaptive number of registration sentences, registration quality weighting (replacing the current equal-weight minimum distance strategy), and mixed QbyE of audio registration and text phoneme sequence registration, transforming the framework's capabilities into a user-perceivable "register and use" experience.
