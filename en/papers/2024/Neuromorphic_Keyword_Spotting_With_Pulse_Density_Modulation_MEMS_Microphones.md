## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is inherently an "always-on" task: devices must continuously listen to the audio stream to capture wake-word commands. This determines its cost model not as "how much compute is spent per inference," but as "how much power is consumed over a month of continuous operation." The introduction cites literature [1][2] indicating that the academic community has explored multiple routes to improve the energy efficiency of KWS devices. Among these, neuromorphic devices have been proven superior to traditional solutions due to their binary, event-driven, bio-inspired characteristics [3][4]. The meaning of "event-driven" is "no computation without spikes," meaning power consumption approaches zero during silence. This aligns naturally with the load profile of KWS, where the device spends most of its time listening to noise and only a tiny fraction of time processing speech.

However, a typical neuromorphic KWS pipeline (Figure 1 in the paper) must pass through four stages of processing: Analog-to-Digital Conversion (ADC), feature extraction, spike encoding, and SNN classification. Each stage represents real circuitry and power consumption: the decimation filter must run continuously in the DSP for low-pass filtering, downsampling, and quantization (Section 2.1); feature extraction requires multiply-accumulate arrays for STFT or MFCC; and the spike encoder must translate continuous features back into spikes once again. In other words, even if the SNN itself is optimized to the limit, the frontend feeding it data still relies on traditional digital signal processing, locking the lower bound of end-to-end power consumption at the frontend.

Meanwhile, MEMS microphones are standard components in smartphones, tablets, laptops, and automotive devices [7]. The mainstream interface for modern digital MEMS microphones is indeed Pulse Density Modulation (PDM): the sigma-delta converter inside the microphone encodes analog sound pressure directly into a high-speed single-bit stream on the sensor side [8][9]. PDM has become the mainstream digital interface because single-bit streams are robust against interference on traces, have simple wiring, do not require clock alignment for multi-bit data, and offer low device and system wiring costs (design considerations for analog and digital MEMS in [14]). The physical form of PDM—a string of pulses whose density varies with amplitude—is highly similar to the dense timing of spike trains in biological neural communication [10]: high amplitude corresponds to high pulse density, and low amplitude to low pulse density. This is precisely the basic encoding method in Spiking Neural Networks where "firing rate carries analog values." Some works have specifically demonstrated the parallelism between time-based signal representations in neurobiology and energy-efficient engineered systems [10]. This observation is the seed of the entire paper: since the microphone is already "emitting spikes," why demodulate the spikes back to PCM, extract features, and re-encode them into spikes? Two out of three format conversions are self-inflicted. In other words, the traditional pipeline flattens the "event stream" into "numerical frames" and then pulls it back into an "event stream." The two paradigm shifts in between come at a cost, paying only for the inertia of spectral feature engineering accumulated over decades.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

- **Front-end redundancy is a structural problem.** The complete link of a conventional pipeline is: PDM bitstream → DSP decimation → PCM (e.g., 48 kHz, 16-bit) → STFT/MFCC → continuous features → spike encoder → spike sequence → SNN. Three intermediate transformations each introduce computation, latency, and power consumption. Moreover, after PCM conversion, the digital stream must be continuously transported at the 48 kHz level, and the decimation filter cannot stop running on the DSP.
- **Existing "PDM-friendly" solutions still add stages rather than removing them.** Gutierrez-Galan et al. [11] connected PDM microphones to pulse frequency modulation (PFM) pulse systems to build neuromorphic auditory sensors (NAS); Bensimon et al. [12] used Spike Continuous-Time Neurons (SCTN) for PDM signal preprocessing. However, as stated in the paper's abstract, "these methods still add supplementary processing stages between the microphone and the SNN." They solve interface compatibility issues but do not question whether the intermediate stages themselves should exist.
- **Cochlear model routes are heavy and difficult to jointly train with the backend.** Cramer et al. [5] used a hydrodynamic basilar membrane model for time-frequency decomposition, cascaded with hair cell and dendritic cell models to complete spike conversion (the Heidelberg SSC dataset is encoded this way); Jiménez-Fernández et al. [6] implemented a binaural neuromorphic auditory sensor on an FPGA. Such bio-simulation cascades have many parameters, heavy computation, and fixed features that cannot be end-to-end optimized for classification loss.
- **SOTA networks in the SSC track are large with low accuracy.** Section 4.4 cites [23]: networks reported on SSC range from 280K to 10M parameters, with accuracy intervals of only 50% to 80.69%. Note that SSC inherently includes time-frequency decomposition at the encoding stage (the cochlear model feeds "semi-processed" information to the model), so the task the model bears is actually lighter, yet it still fails to break through 81%.

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

- **The magnitude of sampling rate:** PDM must oversample to ensure fidelity. For a target of 48 kHz and an oversampling ratio of 64, the PDM sampling rate reaches 3 MHz (Section 2.1); this paper's experiments use GSC's 16 kHz multiplied by 64, i.e., 1.024 MHz. SNNs are typically simulated recursively by time steps. Sequence-based BPTT for million-level time steps is completely infeasible; training and inference require new parallelization schemes.
- **Learning features from a single-bit stream that is almost entirely noise:** Reducing from 16-bit to 1-bit typically introduces a degradation of quantization noise by about 90 dB ([9], Section 4.2). By cutting out feature extraction, the network must simultaneously perform "demodulation, denoising, feature extraction, and classification." This is a true test of representation learning capabilities.
- **Dataset format barriers:** GSC is in 16-bit PCM format. To train a network that "eats PDM," all 105,829 audio clips must first be converted to PDM. The classic conversion algorithm is a serial recursion with a feedback loop (Algorithm 1), looping sample by sample. The conversion cost might conversely bottleneck the rhythm of the entire training iteration.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall approach is a "shortcut" pipeline: the PDM bitstream from the MEMS microphone is directly connected to a 1D Convolutional SNN, outputting 35-class decisions (the shortcut in Figure 1). The three frontend stages of decimation, feature extraction, and spike encoding disappear entirely. The network consists of 5 layers (Section 3.1):

- **Layer 1:** 128 ParaLIF-D neurons, 1D convolution, kernel size $3\alpha$, stride $\lfloor 3\alpha/2 \rfloor$, where $\alpha$ is the oversampling ratio;
- **Layers 2, 3, 4:** Each has 128 ParaLIF-D neurons, 1D dilated convolution, kernel size 3, stride 3, dilation factor 2;
- **Layer 5:** 35 Leaky Integrate (LI) neurons that do not emit spikes; they accumulate membrane potential over time to serve as scores for each class (following the surrogate baseline readout method in [16]).

Breakdown of design motivations:

- **Why choose 1D Convolutional SNN:** PDM is a very long temporal binary signal. 1D convolution has been proven to learn representations end-to-end directly from time-domain audio [18] (Abdoli et al.'s work on environmental sound classification). Moreover, convolution is naturally parallelizable—the prerequisite for running training on 1 MHz-level inputs. The paper states this choice is "based on its ability to learn representations from time-domain audio signals."
- **Why choose ParaLIF-D:** This is the Delta variant of the parallelizable LIF proposed in the authors' previous work [19]. It rewrites the step-by-step recursion into a time-parallel form, specifically addressing "fast processing of long input sequences." The choice is not coincidental: for an input of 1 million samples per second, sequence-based LIF simulation would degenerate into a million-step for-loop.
- **Why Layer 1 kernel scales with $\alpha$:** Kernel=$3\alpha$, stride=$\lfloor 3\alpha/2 \rfloor$ ensures the receptive field in "real seconds" remains constant for the first layer. When $\alpha=64$, the sampling rate is 64 times higher, and the kernel scales up 64 times synchronously, so the physical duration covered by the convolution window remains unchanged. The benefit is that changing the oversampling ratio does not require redesigning the network; only two hyperparameters in the first layer need to be changed, with only slight changes in parameter count (185K to 210K, Table 2). Section 4.2 specifically emphasizes this flexibility.
- **Why Layers 2 to 4 use stride 3 stacked dilated convolutions:** Downsample layer by layer and expand the receptive field using dilation, stacking large time windows in deep layers with extremely small kernels (3). Based on stride calculation (derived by the author, not in the original paper): when $\alpha=64$, the first layer stride is 96. The 1.024 MHz input is reduced to approximately 10.7 kHz after the first layer. Then, after three layers of stride 3 (dividing by 27 in total), the time resolution of the Layer 4 output drops to the ~400 Hz level. The entire network itself constitutes a "demodulation decimation chain," replacing the multi-stage decimation filters in the DSP.
- **Why add random fixed axonal delays:** A random fixed delay is added after each hidden layer. Each neuron's delay is uniformly sampled from [0-30] time steps, with the time step value depending on the input sampling rate of that layer (Section 3.1, same approach as [17]). The delay line converts "when a spike is emitted" into "which neuron is hit simultaneously": spikes emitted at different times can align into synchronous inputs at the target neuron after passing through different delays. Since SNN neurons are sensitive to spike synchrony, [23] indicates that this mechanism can improve performance, even if the delays are randomly fixed.
- **Why add recurrence in Layers 3 and 4:** Recurrent version of ParaLIF (details in [19]). The modification in this paper is that the recurrence term feeds back from the hidden layer membrane potential after ReLU. ReLU truncates negative feedback, preventing membrane potential oscillation and divergence in the loop. Recurrence allows the network to learn long-term dependencies beyond the convolutional receptive field. The cost is an increase of 33k parameters ($2 \times 128 \times 128$ weights plus $2 \times 128$ biases, Section 4.1).
- **Training configuration:** Surrogate gradient [20] for error backpropagation; Adamax optimizer, initial learning rate 0.002; reduce-on-plateau scheduler, decay factor 0.7, patience 10 (Section 3.1).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The theoretical pillar of the entire paper is the isomorphism between the PDM modulator and the Integrate-and-Fire neuron (Section 3.3). The classic PCM→PDM algorithm (Algorithm 1, derived from [21]) executes for each sample: $q_e \leftarrow q_e + x[n]$; if $q_e > 0$ then $y[n] = 1$ else $y[n] = -1$; $q_e \leftarrow q_e - y[n]$. The original version normalizes input to $[-1, 1]$ and outputs $\pm 1$. This paper changes line 8 $y[n] \leftarrow -1$ to $y[n] \leftarrow 0$, making the input normalized to $[0, 1]$ and the output 0/1. The modified algorithm is essentially: accumulate input $x$ into the error variable $q_e$; if the error is positive, emit a spike and subtract 1 from the error. As the paper states, this is "equivalent to a soft-reset Integrate and Fire neuron with a firing threshold of 1." The key to soft reset is that after firing, $q_e$ subtracts the threshold rather than being cleared; the remainder is retained. This is exactly the soft reset semantics of LIF. The physical meaning is: the sigma-delta loop in the microphone and the first-layer neurons of the SNN execute the same dynamics. "Sensor as spike encoder" is not an analogy but a mathematical equivalence.

The parallel version of the conversion (Algorithm 2) completes in three steps: Step 1, compute cumulative sum of input $x \leftarrow \text{cumsum}(x)$; Step 2, integer divide by threshold $x \leftarrow \lfloor x \div th \rfloor$ ($th=1$) to obtain a step curve; Step 3, take the difference between adjacent samples and keep positive values $y \leftarrow x[1..s] - x[0..s-1] > 0$. The equivalence comes from an observation: in the serial algorithm, the $k$-th spike occurs at the moment the cumulative sum crosses the $k$-th integer multiple of the threshold. $\text{floor}(\text{cumsum}/th)$ exactly accumulates the count of total spikes emitted. The positions where the difference is positive are the positions of new spikes. Cumulative sum, integer division, and difference are all array-level operations with no data dependencies, allowing them to be mapped to GPU parallelism as a whole. This拆s the $O(s)$ serial feedback loop into a round of vectorized tensor operations. In tests on a Tesla V100, the longest sequence (oversampling ratio 64) achieved a speedup of up to 100,000 times (Section 3.3).

Mathematics of the readout layer: Layer 5 LI neurons do not fire. The class score takes the cumulative sum of the membrane potential over time, equivalent to treating the "average membrane potential over the entire duration" as classification evidence, while preserving both frequency encoding and temporal encoding information (following [16]).

There is also background mathematics worth supplementing (scattered in Sections 2.1 and 4.2): The fidelity of PDM depends on the oversampling ratio. At a target sampling rate of 48 kHz and an oversampling ratio of 64, PDM is actually sampled at 3 MHz (Section 2.1). Conversely, to convert PDM back to PCM, decimation must be performed in the DSP—low-pass filtering, downsampling, and quantization again. These three steps are indispensable, which is exactly the heavy work this paper aims to bypass. Why dare to talk about fidelity with single-bit? The core capability of the sigma-delta structure is noise shaping. Quantization noise is pushed to the high-frequency band by the feedback loop, leaving very little residual in the signal band. For every doubling of the oversampling ratio, the in-band signal-to-noise ratio improves by a fixed number of decibels. This is the signal-theoretic root of the experimental phenomenon in Section 4.2 that "accuracy grows logarithmically with the oversampling ratio." The set of convolutional filters learned by the network is essentially integrating in the signal band and attenuating in the high-frequency noise band, filtering out the quantization noise that was pushed up. Therefore, what oversampling buys is not more bits, but the "space where quantization noise and signal are separated in the spectrum." The first layer of the network is the executor of this separation operation.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Direct Shortcut from Microphone to SNN

Connect the PDM bitstream directly as SNN input (Section 2.4, Figure 1). The foundation of feasibility is that the inherent binary nature of PDM aligns naturally with the binary spike input of SNNs. The benefit is "bypassing all intermediate stages, significantly reducing the overall computational cost of the system" (Abstract). A deeper mechanistic understanding (author's analysis): the local pulse density of the PDM bitstream is proportional to the local amplitude. The Layer 1 convolution kernel performs a weighted sliding average on the 0/1 bitstream, which is itself a learnable low-pass/band-pass filter. The network learns "which filter to use for demodulation" from the data itself, rather than having it pre-specified by DSP engineers. This is precisely why the Conv baseline in Section 4.1 can reach 77.35%.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Parallel PCM→PDM Conversion Algorithm

As described in the previous section, Algorithm 2 changes the serial feedback loop into a fully parallel pipeline of cumsum plus integer division plus difference, solving Challenge 3: "PDM dataset preparation stalling training." The paper also changes the output convention from $\pm 1$ to 0/1, consistent with SNN spike notation, making the equivalence relationship "converter as I&F neuron" directly visible. This is an infrastructure contribution at the method level: without it, large-scale format conversion before each experimental iteration would be unbearable.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Incremental Combination of Convolution, Recurrence, Random Delay, and Data Augmentation

The network body is not a single invention, but a superposition of lightweight components that can be incrementally verified (1D convolution skeleton + Layer 3/4 recurrence + random fixed delay after hidden layers + temporal translation augmentation), with ablation experiments quantifying contributions item by item (Table 1). The design philosophy of the combination is: each component serves the single goal of "learning representations from high-speed time-domain bitstreams"—convolution handles parallelism and local frequency, recurrence handles long-term dependencies, delay handles spike synchronization, and augmentation handles temporal robustness.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to NAS [11] and SCTN [12]: They add processing stages between the microphone and SNN to solve interface issues; this paper directly cancels the intermediate stages. Compared to the cochlear model route [5][6]: They use fixed artificial cochleae for time-frequency decomposition, making features untrainable; this paper allows the first-layer convolution to learn filter banks from data, enabling end-to-end differentiability. Compared to SSC track SOTA [23]: Network sizes range from 280K to 10M parameters, with accuracy from 50% to 80.69%; this paper's largest network has 210K parameters and achieves 91.54±0.23% accuracy (Section 4.4). The essence of the difference is changing the mindset from "manufacturing spikes for SNN" to "discovering that the sensor is already emitting spikes"—the problem shifts from how to encode to how to save encoding.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

GSC (Google Speech Commands) v2 [13]: 105,829 one-second speech clips, 35 words; split into 84,843 training, 9,981 validation, and 11,005 test samples; all 16 kHz, 16-bit PCM WAV (Section 3.2). Since the network requires PDM input, the entire dataset is first oversampled according to the target oversampling ratio $\alpha$, then encoded into PDM using the conversion algorithm (Section 3.3). Cross-method comparisons use literature numbers on SSC [5] (the cochlear-encoded version of GSC). The paper does not use other datasets.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Three key metrics (beginning of Section 4): classification accuracy (percentage of correctly detected words), network spike rate SR (average number of spikes per second across the network), and number of learnable parameters; Table 2 additionally supplements Input Sampling Rate ISR and Relative Spike Rate RSR (SR/ISR, unit: spike/sample). The rationale reflects the neuromorphic perspective: the energy consumption of event-driven chips is approximately proportional to the number of events, not the number of samples. The number of parameters corresponds to synaptic storage and synaptic operation costs. Therefore, accuracy plus spike rate plus parameter count together form the complete evidence chain for "energy efficiency potential." Reporting accuracy alone is meaningless. All experiments were repeated 5 times with random initialization, running for 150 epochs, reporting mean ± standard deviation.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The core comparison is in Section 4.4: SOTA [23] networks published on SSC have sizes from 280K to 10M parameters, with accuracy from 50% to 80.69%; this paper's largest network has 210K parameters, the smallest reported in the literature, with accuracy 91.54±0.23%, about 10.9 points higher than the SSC SOTA upper limit. The paper specifically points out the asymmetry in task difficulty: SSC has already performed time-frequency decomposition at the encoding stage, "requiring less from the model," whereas this paper's setting is end-to-end from raw bitstreams, which is harder. Superiority in both scale and accuracy supports the conclusion of "efficient and effective." It should be noted: the paper does not compare with non-pulse SOTA on GSC (ANN tier is generally above 97%); the benchmarking scope is limited to the neuromorphic track. The accuracy gap between the two tracks (91.54% vs. above 97%) is the precision cost paid for choosing the pulse computing paradigm, which the paper does not discuss in detail.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Table 1 (all performed at oversampling ratio 10, Section 4.1):

| Architecture | Train (%) | Valid (%) | Test (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conv | 87.83±0.12 | 79.08±0.31 | 77.35±0.40 |
| Conv+Rec | 96.06±0.25 | 85.06±0.13 | 84.01±0.25 |
| Conv+Rec+Delay | 97.51±0.15 | 87.39±0.20 | 86.12±0.47 |
| Conv+Rec+Delay+Aug | 90.34±0.28 | 90.07±0.22 | 89.29±0.26 |

- **Pure convolution baseline reaches 77.35%**: 1D convolution directly eating PDM bitstreams can learn diverse filters to extract frequency components (citing [18]). Why it works: the weighted sliding average of the convolution kernel on the bitstream is equivalent to a set of learnable low-pass/band-pass filters. The first layer simultaneously completes demodulation and frequency division.
- **Adding recurrence**: Training set increases by about 9 points, test set by about 7 points (text Section 4.1). Recurrent connections supplement the ability to learn temporal dependencies beyond the convolutional receptive field—previously, this ability could only be indirectly obtained through the time constant of the spiking neuron's membrane potential. The paper also notes that the combination of convolution and recurrence has precedents ([22], convolutional recurrent networks for small vocabulary KWS). The two take the best of both worlds: convolution handles local translation-invariant frequency structures, while recurrence handles temporal context spanning multiple convolution windows. The cost is an addition of 33k parameters ($2 \times 128 \times 128$ weights plus $2 \times 128$ biases). Note that the modification of the recurrence term passing through ReLU only retains positive membrane potential feedback, effectively letting the network decide "what to remember" rather than unconditionally bringing back the state from the previous moment.
- **Adding axonal delay**: Training increases by about 1 point, test by about 2 points. Delays cause spikes to arrive simultaneously, enhancing network activity even if firing times differ (neurons are sensitive to synchrony). This phenomenon has been reported in [23], including cases where delays are randomly fixed. A detail worth chewing here: the delay range is [0-30] time steps, but the physical duration of a time step differs per layer (depending on the input sampling rate of that layer, Section 3.1). Deeper layers have longer time steps, so the same "30 steps" correspond to much larger physical delays in deeper layers. The network thus free-gets a multi-scale delay spectrum across time scales, effectively baking coarse-grained multi-scale temporal alignment into the architecture, with zero parameters and zero computational cost (delay lines are just buffer shifts).
- **Adding temporal translation augmentation** (random $[-0.3, 0.3]$ seconds): Training set drops by about 7 points, test set rises by about 3 points. The accuracy of the three sets converges to around 90 for the first time—a typical trade-off of fitting ability for generalization ability, suppressing overfitting.

Impact of oversampling ratio (Section 4.2, Figure 2-A): $\alpha=1$ yields 70.44±0.30%; $\alpha=2$ yields 81.55±0.13% (up 11%); $\alpha=4$ yields 86.52±0.17% (up another 5%); $\alpha=8$ yields 88.69%, $\alpha=16$ yields 89.83%, $\alpha=32$ yields 90.65% (data labeled in Figure 2-A); $\alpha=64$ reaches the best 91.54±0.23%. The paper summarizes the pattern as accuracy growing logarithmically with the oversampling ratio. Why logarithmic: the SNR of PDM improves by a fixed number of decibels for every doubling of oversampling (a classic characteristic of sigma-delta modulation). The ~90 dB noise degradation introduced by 1-bit quantization [9] needs to be bought back bit by bit through oversampling, leading to diminishing marginal returns. $\alpha=1$ (equivalent to directly eating 16 kHz 1-bit coarse quantized signal) still achieves 70.44%, which the paper uses to emphasize the system's robustness to noise. Additionally, the oversampling ratio only affects the first layer's kernel and stride, with parameter count rising only from 185K ($\alpha=1$) to 210K ($\alpha=64$) (Table 2).

Impact of connection sparsity (Section 4.3, Table 2 and Figure 2-B): Fix $\alpha=64$, and progressively reduce the inter-layer $128 \times 128$ fully connected layers to $128 \times 64$ (50% sparse), $128 \times 32$ (75%), $128 \times 16$ (88%), $128 \times 8$ (94%). Parameters drop from 210K to 71K (about 3x reduction), with accuracy dropping only about 5 points to 86.42±0.13% (Figure 2-B); at 50% sparsity, it is 90.57% (labeled in Figure 2-B); there is another intermediate label of 87.93% (corresponding to 75% or 88% tier, not specified one by one in the text; the specific accuracy for the 88% tier is not reported by the paper). This provides a second working point: 71K parameters, 86.42% accuracy.

An interesting counter-intuitive phenomenon (Table 2): the sparser the connections, the higher the network spike rate—206K (0%), 229K (50%), 286K (75%), 317K (88%) spike/s. Cutting synapses results in more events. A reasonable explanation (author's analysis): after the averaging and inhibition provided by full connections are weakened, neurons are more likely to fire individually by crossing the threshold. This means that "saving 3x parameters" and "1.4x more events" must be折算 (converted) according to the unit price of synaptic operations and soma firing on real neuromorphic hardware. One cannot simply declare power savings based on parameter count alone.

Activity sparsity (Section 4.3): Among 512 spiking neurons (4 hidden layers $\times$ 128), with 16 kHz input, an average of 7.67 neurons are activated per sample (1.5%); with 1024 kHz input, only 0.2 per sample (0.04%). The relative spike rate drops from 7.67 to 0.20 spike/sample: input sampling rate increases 64 times, but absolute spike rate only rises from 122K to 206K spike/s (1.7x). Why this is important: event-driven hardware bills by events. The network's event budget hardly explodes with sampling rate. This is the hardest evidence for "low energy consumption potential." The paper's conclusion lists the 0.04% activation rate, connection sparsity, and frontend omission as the three pillars of low-energy neuromorphic KWS systems.

## Main Contributions

1. Proposed and verified the direct shortcut from microphone to SNN: PDM bitstream directly enters SNN, completely removing the three frontend stages of decimation, feature extraction, and spike encoding (Figure 1). Achieved 91.54±0.23% on GSC, surpassing SSC track SOTA (50% to 80.69%, [23]), with the largest network having only 210K parameters, smaller than the minimum reported in literature (Section 4.4).
2. Clarified and utilized the mathematical isomorphism between PDM modulator and I&F neurons (soft reset, threshold 1, Section 3.3), elevating "sensor as spike encoder" from intuition to a statement-able equivalence relationship.
3. Proposed a parallel PCM→PDM conversion algorithm (Algorithm 2), achieving a speedup of up to 100,000 times for the longest sequence on Tesla V100 (Section 3.3), reducing PDM dataset preparation from a training bottleneck to a one-time preprocessing step, while also providing infrastructure for the community to conduct PDM-related research.
4. Systematically quantified the impact of oversampling ratio and connection sparsity on accuracy, parameter count, and spike rate (Table 2, Figure 2), providing two deployable working points: 210K parameters/91.54% and 71K parameters/86.42%, and revealing the trade-off phenomenon where sparse connections elevate spike rate (206K to 317K spike/s).
5. Open-sourced PyTorch implementation (Footnote 1: github.com/NECOTIS/Keyword-Spotting-with-PDM). All experiments were repeated 5 times with standard deviation reported, ensuring good reproducibility.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Energy efficiency is inferred, not measured:** The "low energy consumption" claims in the entire paper come from three indirect evidences—activity sparsity as low as 0.04%, connections can be sparse up to 94%, and three frontend stages are omitted. The conclusion chapter explicitly admits that the next step is to implement on hardware devices and verify with measured power consumption. Table 2 shows that sparse connections actually elevate spike rate (206K to 317K spike/s), indicating that real energy consumption depends on the unit price structure of synaptic operations and soma firing. Extrapolating energy consumption based solely on parameter count is risky.
- **Bitstreams are simulated:** The PDM used for training and evaluation is converted from GSC's PCM (Section 3.3). There are gaps between the device self-noise, DC offset, noise shaping spectrum of real PDM MEMS microphones and ideal conversion. The paper does not address the domain gap from simulation to device.
- **First-layer computation expands with oversampling ratio:** Kernel=$3\alpha$ means when $\alpha=64$, the first-layer convolution window is 192 points, with an input rate of 1.024 MHz. Training relies on the parallelism of V100-level GPUs. The paper does not discuss the event throughput and latency pressure of this layer during edge-side deployment, nor does it report inference latency and real-time performance.
- **Readout and delay components are fixed/random:** Axonal delays are randomly fixed rather than learnable ([23] has proven that learnable delays are effective), leaving known performance margins untapped.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- Ablation experiments were performed at an oversampling ratio of 10 (Section 4.1), while the best configuration is 64. Whether the interaction effects of each component at 64 and the ablation conclusions migrate is not reported by the paper.
- The comparison with SSC SOTA crosses dataset encoding methods (SSC inherently has cochlear time-frequency decomposition, while this paper uses raw bitstreams). The paper itself admits "SSC requires less from the model." This comparison direction favors this paper, but the reverse fair baseline—ANN or quantized networks under the same PDM input—is completely absent.
- There is no control against non-pulse SOTA on GSC (the tier above 97%). Readers cannot evaluate how large the precision cost of the pulse paradigm really is. There are also no robustness experiments under harsh acoustic conditions such as noise and reverberation; data augmentation only used temporal translation.
- There is a gap between the evaluation format and the real KWS deployment format: GSC is a cut one-second isolated word classification task, while product-level wake-word detection is a continuous streaming task requiring sliding window inference and suppressing false wake-up rates on open-set negative samples (a typical metric is false triggers per hour). This paper does not perform any streaming inference or false wake-up evaluation. The trade-off between two types of errors (missed detection and false trigger) is not reported. For the "always-on listening" scenario that forms the basis of this paper, this is a gap closer to product acceptance than accuracy.
- Training-side costs are not disclosed: the training duration, VRAM usage for 5 repetitions $\times$ 150 epochs on V100, and the actual overhead of surrogate gradient backpropagation over million time steps are not reported by the paper. The energy efficiency claim only covers the inference side, making it impossible to evaluate the break-even point between training cost and deployment benefit.
- Hardware-level metrics related to energy consumption (inference latency, energy per inference, memory bandwidth) are not measured at all, creating an evidence gap with the energy efficiency claims in the abstract.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- Hardware implementation and measured power consumption: This is the next step listed by the authors in the conclusion chapter and is the final judge of all energy efficiency claims in this paper.
- Replace random fixed delays with learnable delays, and make connection sparsity a learnable or gradually annealed parameter to further compress the accuracy loss of the 71K working point.
- Use data collected from real PDM MEMS microphone frontends for training and evaluation to bridge the domain gap between simulated bitstreams and device noise.
- Make engineering trade-offs on the logarithmic return curve of oversampling ratio (Figure 2-A): After $\alpha$ exceeds 64, marginal returns are very small. Edge-side products are more likely to choose a "sufficient" sampling rate in the 16 to 32 range, combined with connection sparsity, to form a power-optimal working point.
- Conduct strict controlled experiments with quantized ANN and binary networks under the same PDM input to answer the paradigm-level question: "Is the direct SNN really more power-efficient than a strong quantized frontend plus a small ANN?"
