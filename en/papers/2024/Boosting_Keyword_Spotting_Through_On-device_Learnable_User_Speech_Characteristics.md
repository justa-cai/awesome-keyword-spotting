# Boosting keyword spotting through on-device learnable user speech characteristics

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Cristian Cioflan (Integrated Systems Laboratory, ETH Zurich); Lukas Cavigelli (Zurich Research Center, Huawei Technologies); Luca Benini (ETH Zurich IIS, and DEI, University of Bologna)
- **Date**: March 12, 2024 (arXiv v1); presented at the tinyML Research Symposium 2024 (April 2024, Burlingame, CA, USA)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07802
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, on-device learning, user embeddings, speaker adaptation, TinyML, few-shot learning, feature fusion

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS), also known as closed-set vocabulary speech recognition, is a persistent voice entry point for smart speakers, smartphones, and various consumer IoT devices. The task involves recognizing a predefined set of keywords from a continuous stream of user speech. Unlike general speech recognition, the typical deployment mode for KWS is "always-on": devices must continuously monitor audio streams under constraints of milliwatt-level power budgets, hundreds of KB to a few MB of on-chip storage, and real-time inference requirements of tens of times per second. This represents a typical TinyML scenario characterized by five-fold constraints: storage, memory, latency, floating-point operations (FLOPs), and power consumption.

The paper argues that the traditional machine learning paradigm of "train-once-deploy-everywhere" is becoming ineffective. For audio systems, disturbance sources in deployment environments include not only acoustic interferences such as background noise, reverberation, and echo, but also easily overlooked disturbances: the speaker's "unremarkable" speech characteristics—paralinguistic features like pitch and intonation, speech impediments such as stuttering, and distributional skew caused by dataset collection biases (gender, accent). The cumulative result of these factors is that when speakers who are underrepresented in the dataset use the audio system, the system's accuracy systematically declines. Related studies cited in the paper indicate that paralinguistic features in voice significantly affect perceptual judgments, and the impact of gender and accent biases on KWS benchmark accuracy has been documented in existing dataset research.

Faced with this user-side domain shift, the industry has two paths. The first is to collect larger, more diverse datasets and pre-train larger, more general models. This path is costly and long-tailed, never catching up with the diversity of real users. The second is to adapt already deployed networks to the specific speech attributes of target users. The paper explicitly chooses the second path and further requires that this adaptation must occur on the device: after distributing the pre-trained model to the device, self-calibration is performed using a very small amount of the target user's speech. This is a more efficient engineering approach because it shrinks the infinite problem of "generalizing to everyone" into the finite problem of "adapting to the current single user."

The pain point lies in the direct conflict between on-device adaptation and TinyML constraints. Classic backpropagation learning and its variants are memory-hungry and computationally expensive: backpropagation requires retaining intermediate activations in memory to compute gradients, and the memory required to fine-tune complex deep networks is often unavailable on microcontrollers (MCUs); simultaneously, repeated gradient calculations reduce battery life. Furthermore, reliable fine-tuning typically requires a large number of labeled samples, whereas in real scenarios, users will not record dozens of minutes of labeled speech for a device.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper reviews the defects of existing solutions from two directions.

The first direction is **end-to-end ASR systems with speaker adaptation**. These methods (such as the classic i-vector, x-vector, s-vector) first use a deep network pre-trained on a speaker identification task to generate vector embeddings for the target speaker, then concatenate these vectors to the input features of the acoustic model to form a two-stage speaker-adaptive ASR. The problem with this approach is that the two-stage structure increases inference latency, which is a hard metric for real-time KWS; the additional forward overhead of the speaker encoder on edge devices may directly break real-time performance. Additionally, it requires maintaining an extra pre-trained speaker identification network, incurring double overhead in storage and computation. The paper points out that such strategies are "unsuitable for edge devices due to increased latency."

The second direction is **one-time fine-tuning of the backbone network** (backbone refinement), which fine-tunes the backbone network to the target speaker's vocal domain, compressing the adaptation cost into a one-time overhead. This route has four specific shortcomings: First, the memory required to fine-tune complex deep neural networks is usually unavailable on MCUs—on-device training work cited in the paper (e.g., on-device training under 256 KB memory) has already reached its limits, while fully fine-tuning a medium-sized KWS network requires memory far exceeding this magnitude (Table 3 in this paper shows that full updates for DS-CNN M require up to 5.5 MB of memory); Second, the computational cost of backpropagation shortens the battery life of KWS systems; Third, existing work requires 0.9 to 30 minutes of labeled user data to achieve performance improvements, making the collection and labeling process cumbersome and unacceptable from a user experience perspective; Fourth, when fine-tuning the backbone, it is necessary to ensure class balance in the presented data—in closed-set vocabulary KWS, speech randomly spoken by users cannot naturally be class-balanced, which leads to catastrophic forgetting: gradient updates for new categories will wash away the discriminative knowledge of old categories.

Furthermore, recent on-device training frameworks (citing MCUNet series' 256 KB memory training, low-precision floating-point optimization on MCUs, TinyOL online learning, etc.) have moved training to the device, but their core action remains updating backbone weights. Therefore, the absolute overhead in memory and computation remains considerable, and they also face the problem of scarce labeled samples.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

The question the paper seeks to answer can be precisely stated as: How to design a KWS system that satisfies all of the following constraints when facing **unseen speakers at deployment time**—First, the adaptation requires extremely few labeled samples (the paper ultimately achieves as low as 4 training samples per class, plus 1 validation and 1 test sample per class); Second, the computational, memory, and energy overhead of adaptation must fall within the TinyML budget (the paper achieves approximately 1 MFLOP per epoch with a 23.7 k parameter scale, and intermediate result storage below 4 KB); Third, the adaptation process must not destroy the general acoustic knowledge learned by the backbone network, i.e., avoid catastrophic forgetting in scenarios with class scarcity (where only partial class samples are available during the online learning phase); Fourth, a model copy cannot be stored for each new user, otherwise storage expands linearly with the number of users; Fifth, the inference structure after adaptation must not introduce significant additional latency. These five constraints are mutually restrictive—wanting accuracy requires touching the backbone, but touching the backbone violates memory and forgetting constraints. The core contribution of the paper is to find a design point that satisfies all constraints simultaneously.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The system consists of three parts (Figure 1): a frozen DS-CNN backbone network, a user embedding layer, and a fused fully connected classifier. Audio input first undergoes MFCC feature extraction and enters the backbone network: the backbone includes an initial 2D convolution block using asymmetric 10×4 convolution kernels to aggregate information over a 220 ms time window; this is followed by several DS-CNN blocks extracting coarse-grained features, each consisting of a depthwise separable convolution structure—i.e., a depthwise conv followed by a pointwise conv, each followed by batch normalization and ReLU; the convolutional features are finally fed into the fully connected classifier to output class predictions.

The motivation for choosing DS-CNN as the backbone is twofold. On one hand, the efficiency of DS-CNN in KWS inference has been widely verified (citing works like Hello Edge, DS-CNN S has only 23.7 k parameters and 2.95 MFLOPs inference overhead, DS-CNN M has 138.1 k parameters and 17.2 MFLOPs, DS-CNN L has 416.7 k parameters and 52.1 MFLOPs, see Section 4.3); On the other hand, equally critical but less emphasized, DS-CNN has been proven to be **suitable for both inference and on-device training**—the authors' previous works (Towards On-device Domain Adaptation for Noise-Robust KWS, and low-precision floating-point on-device training on MCUs) are all built on DS-CNN, providing empirical evidence for the numerical stability of on-device training.

The user embedding layer is the pivot of the entire design. It is a look-up table that maps a speaker's unique identifier ID to an n-dimensional vector. This design directly responds to the problem of "one model copy per user": the traditional approach saves one pre-trained model plus one fine-tuned copy for each target user, with storage growing linearly with the number of users; under the look-up table scheme, for each new speaker, only **one new row** needs to be added to the weight matrix, the values of which can be trained end-to-end jointly with the backbone or trained independently. The length of each dictionary entry is carefully chosen to match the size of the intermediate activations at the fusion point (labeled FUSE in Figure 1) in the backbone.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The optimization objectives of the entire system differ between the pre-training phase and the online learning phase. In the pre-training phase, backbone parameters and embedding rows for all pre-trained users are trained jointly, with the loss being standard cross-entropy:

$$L_{CE} = -\sum_{c} y_c \log \hat{y}_c, \quad \hat{y} = \text{softmax}(W_{cls} \cdot \text{fuse}(a(x), e_u))$$

where $a(x)$ is the activation vector produced by the backbone for input speech $x$ at the fusion point, $e_u$ is the embedding vector for speaker $u$, $W_{cls}$ is the classifier weight, and fuse is the fusion operator. The paper investigates three fusion operators: element-wise multiplication (multiplication), element-wise addition (addition), and concatenation (concatenation), with fusion performed along the channel dimension.

Multiplicative fusion $a \odot e_u$ is mathematically equivalent to a **channel-level gating**: each dimension of the embedding vector scales and modulates the corresponding channel of the backbone activation. When $e_{u,i} > 1$, the channel is amplified; when $e_{u,i} < 1$, it is suppressed; when $e_{u,i} \to 0$, it is turned off. This endows it with clear semantics—each speaker learns not a general bias, but a set of channel gains tailored to their own vocal characteristics. For example, if a speaker has a low fundamental frequency, the spectral channels sensitive to them can have their weights lowered overall. Additive fusion $a + e_u$ can only perform translational bias, offering weaker modulation capability.

Concatenation fusion has two implementations, and the design trade-offs here are worth elaborating. The first is **backbone-compatible** (BC) concatenation: the dictionary entry length equals the number of backbone channels (e.g., 64 for DS-CNN S). After concatenation, the feature length doubles, so the input dimension of the classifier must be modified. The second is **classifier-compatible** (CC) concatenation: the sum of user features and backbone channels equals the number of input neurons in the classifier (also 64). In this case, it is necessary to reduce the output channels of the last depthwise separable convolution layer of the backbone to make space. That is, regardless of the implementation, concatenation fusion requires "touching the structure"—either changing the classifier shape or reducing backbone capacity—whereas multiplicative and additive fusion do not change the dimensions of any layers at all. This is a practical advantage for on-device deployment (the operator graph and memory layout of the pre-trained backbone can be reused as-is).

The online learning phase is the core mechanism of the method: **the backbone and fully connected classifier are frozen, and only the embedding vector of the current target user is updated**. The error brought by the new speaker's speech features is measured using cross-entropy loss, and gradients are backpropagated only to $e_u$:

$$e_u \leftarrow e_u - \eta \nabla_{e_u} L_{CE}$$

Since $e_u$ only affects the output through the fusion point, gradient calculation does not need to pass through any layer of the backbone—the depth of the backpropagation computation graph is 1. This is the mathematical root of memory and computation savings: there is no need to store intermediate activations of the backbone (the backbone forward pass is done only once, and activations are calculated offline before training begins), no need to compute gradients for convolutional layers, and the number of trainable parameters drops from 23.7 k to just 64 floating-point numbers (one embedding row). Simultaneously, catastrophic forgetting is structurally avoided: backbone weights do not change at all, old category knowledge remains intact, and the only change is the channel modulation pattern for the current user.

The paper also points out that the structure of this weight matrix naturally supports **multi-speaker simultaneous learning**—each speaker occupies an independent row, interfering with each other—provided that the system can associate each input speech segment with a uniquely identifiable speaker.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Moving Adaptation from Weight Space to Feature Space

Traditional fine-tuning performs adaptation in weight space (modifying tens of thousands of backbone parameters), while this paper moves adaptation to feature space (modifying a 64-dimensional gating vector). This "relocation" brings three chain-reaction benefits, each backed by data in Table 3: Computationally, full training of DS-CNN S requires 354 M FLOPs per epoch, while learning only the embedding requires 1.04 M FLOPs, a reduction of 340 times; In terms of memory, full training requires 1530 KB peak memory, while learning only the embedding requires 3.6 KB, and for the largest model DS-CNN L, it is 15.5 KB vs 12,998 KB, a difference of about three orders of magnitude; In terms of energy consumption, in estimates on the Vega SoC (50 mW, 79 GFLOP/s/W), full training consumes 4481 µJ per epoch, while learning only the embedding consumes 13.22 µJ. The accuracy cost is limited: the error reduction achieved by learning only the embedding reaches 78% of the benefit of full training (DS-CNN M: 0.94/1.20 percentage points, Table 3). This is a typical engineering sweet spot of "buying back most of the benefit with 1% of the cost."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Multiplicative Late Fusion and Operator Ablation

The paper does not stop at "adding an embedding," but systematically compares four fusion methods (addition, multiplication, BC concatenation, CC concatenation) and deliberately places the fusion point **late** (after the backbone, before the classifier) rather than early (at the input). The motivation for late fusion is stated plainly in the paper: to reduce retraining costs—the compute required for gradient calculation and the memory for storing intermediate results both grow with the length of the trainable path. Injecting user information late means that backpropagation only needs to cover the small path from the embedding vector to the loss function. The ablation results in Table 1 show that multiplicative fusion has the lowest average error across four scenarios (dropping from 27.66% without embedding to 25.85% on GSC 35, a relative reduction of 6%; from 4.46% to 4.26% in the six-sample tier of GSC 10), which is consistent with the semantic explanation of gating modulation.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Online Learning Protocol for Class-Scarce Few-Shot Scenarios

The paper designs an evaluation protocol close to real deployment: each target speaker represents an independent learning session, initialized from the same offline pre-trained backbone; the test set is fixed; training samples are as low as 4 per class; it even allows the online learning phase to see only partial classes (e.g., only 20 classes have samples out of 35). This last point is the most insightful experimental design in the paper—in closed-set vocabulary KWS deployment, users will not necessarily speak each keyword uniformly several times; "class scarcity + domain shift" is the norm. Table 2 specifically examines this, finding that user features become more important as classes become scarcer (see Experimental Results section for details).

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Compared to two-stage schemes like i-vector/x-vector: This paper does not require an additional speaker identification network and its forward latency; the embedding is a tiny look-up table, adding only one element-wise multiplication to the inference path. Compared to backbone fine-tuning schemes: Memory drops from MB level to KB level (Table 3), labeling requirements drop from 0.9-30 minutes to a few short commands per class, and it is naturally immune to catastrophic forgetting. Compared to general on-device training frameworks: Those frameworks update the backbone and need to fit the complete backpropagation infrastructure on the MCU; this paper compresses the trainable surface to one row of weights. Compared to the naive scheme of "one model copy per user": Storage increment drops from one complete model (tens to hundreds of KB) to one embedding row (64 floating-point numbers, approx. 256 B). The cost is: the association problem of "which user does the current speech belong to" must be solved, and the upper limit of adaptation capability is lower than full fine-tuning (full training still earns about 2 percentage points more when samples are sufficient, Table 2).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

All experiments are based on the Google Speech Commands (GSC) dataset, which contains 2,618 speakers, with varying numbers of recordings per class per person. The paper evaluates on two task difficulties: GSC 10 ten-class problem (classes are yes, no, up, down, left, right, on, off, stop, go) and GSC 35 thirty-five class problem (target classes are the same as those in the cited attention model and LETR Transformer works). There is a noteworthy detail in the construction of GSC 10: the paper removes the silence and unknown classes from the common 12-class problem to ensure balance in "samples per word per speaker"—this is both a requirement of the evaluation protocol and echoes the method's sensitivity to class balance. The pre-training set is split 90:10 into training and validation, and results are the average of five random seed runs.

Speaker selection logic for the online learning phase: From 2,618 speakers, speakers with at least 3, 6, or 9 samples per class were filtered, resulting in only 362, 6, and 1 speakers respectively. The remaining 2,256, 2,612, and 2,617 speakers were used for pre-training. The few-shot adaptation experiment (Section 4.2) fixedly uses those 6 speakers with at least 6 samples per class: 1 sample per class per person goes to the test set, 1 to the validation set, and the remaining 4 to 22 are used for training; each speaker is an independent learning session, five seeds, totaling 30 experiments averaged. Training configuration: pre-training up to 40 epochs, early stopping patience 10, Adam optimizer, initial learning rate $10^{-3}$ with plateau learning rate scheduling, batch size 128; online learning phase learning rate drops to $10^{-5}$, patience drops to 5, batch size 10.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The core metric is classification error rate (percentage), calculated on a fixed test set. The rationale: it is the standard metric for closed-set vocabulary classification and facilitates scale-consistent comparison across different update strategies (pre-training baseline, embedding-only update, backbone-only update, full training). The second dimension consists of TinyML feasibility metrics: FLOPs per epoch for training, peak memory (KB), and energy consumption per epoch (µJ). Energy consumption is analytically estimated on the Vega SoC—a dedicated SoC with 50 mW power, 1.6 MB L2 SRAM, and 2 MB on-chip L3 memory, with an energy efficiency of 79 GFLOP/s/W, which has been verified in the authors' previous works as suitable for extreme edge on-device training. Inference latency is not reported in the paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Comparison 1 (Table 1, validation error rate) answers "is user information useful": After setting aside speakers with 3, 6, and 9 samples per class in the pre-training phase, the no-embedding baseline has errors of 6.37%, 4.46%, and 5.35% on GSC 10, and 27.66% on GSC 35; With user embeddings introduced, additive fusion yields 6.98%, 4.31%, 4.84%, and 25.81%; multiplicative fusion yields 6.99%, 4.26%, 4.61%, and 25.85%; BC concatenation yields 6.77%, 4.29%, 4.91%, and 26.54%; CC concatenation yields 7.63%, 4.29%, 5.35%, and 26.46%. In both scenarios, user information reduces error to 4.26% (multiplicative), and on GSC 35, multiplicative embedding reduces relative error by 6% to 25.85%, and has the lowest average error across all four scenarios. Notably, there is an counter-intuitive phenomenon in Table 1: when setting aside 9+ sample speakers (i.e., taking away only 1 person from pre-training), the baseline error of 5.35% is actually higher than in the two other tiers of the scenario with 362 people set aside. The paper candidly attributes this to training noise (such as weight initialization), as the overlap of speakers between the two splits is as high as 99.8%.

Comparison 2 (Table 2, test error rate) is the main result of the paper. The pre-training baseline is 5.33% for GSC 10 and 30.08% for GSC 35. When there are exactly 4 training samples per class (=4): For GSC 10 ten-class, embedding-only update reaches 4.60%, backbone-only update 4.20%, full training 4.20%; For GSC 35 full class (35 classes), embedding-only update is 24.34%, backbone-only update 24.40%, full training 24.54%; For GSC 35 with only 20 classes visible, embedding-only update is 27.00%, backbone-only update 26.87%, full training 26.80%. When there are at least 4 samples per class (≥4, actually 4 to 22 samples): For GSC 10 ten-class, embedding-only update is 4.34%, backbone-only update 2.14%, full training 2.00%; With 8 classes visible, embedding-only update is 4.41%, backbone-only update 3.61%, full training 3.27%; For GSC 35 full class, embedding-only update is 24.81%, backbone-only update 24.07%, full training 24.20%; With 30 classes, 25.54%, 25.74%, 25.41%; With 20 classes, 26.94%, 26.80%, 26.54%.

Three layers of conclusions can be read from Table 2. First layer: Compared to the pre-training baseline, embedding-only update reduces error rate in **all** scenarios, down to a low of 4.34% for GSC 10 and 24.34% for GSC 35, with a maximum relative reduction of 19% (from 30.08% to 24.34%, corresponding to "from 30.1% to 24.3%" in the abstract); This also covers the statement in the introduction about "up to 18% improvement for out-of-domain speakers" (from 5.33% to 4.34%, relative 18.6%). Second layer: When only partial classes are visible during the online learning phase (e.g., only 20 classes in GSC 35), learning speech features still brings a 10% relative improvement (from 30.08% to 27.00%). Third layer, most critical: User features are **more important as classes become scarcer**. When samples are sufficient and classes are complete (GSC 10 ten-class, ≥4 samples), backbone-only update (2.14%) is significantly better than embedding-only update (4.34%)—after all, there are two orders of magnitude more trainable parameters; But when only 8 (GSC 10) or 20 (GSC 35) classes are visible during the online phase, i.e., there is a domain shift, the situation reverses: updating embedding plus backbone reduces error by an average of 9.4% more than backbone-only update (GSC 10 eight-class, ≥4 tier from 3.61% to 3.27%; however, in the =4 tier, full training 4.87% is slightly worse than backbone-only update 4.80%, indicating this advantage depends on sample size), and full training improves by another 0.9% in GSC 35 twenty-class (from 26.80% to 26.54%)—the paper's explanation is that introducing user information mitigates the overfitting risk of few-shot fine-tuning. Figure 2 further shows that on GSC 35, learning only embeddings converges faster than full training (all methods use early stopping with patience 5).

Comparison 3 (Table 3, cost-accuracy trade-off, GSC 10 with four samples per class per speaker, baseline 5.33%): Full training reduces error by 1.13, 1.20, and 1.27 percentage points on DS-CNN S/M/L, at the cost of 354/2064/6252 M FLOPs per epoch, 1530/5467/12,998 KB memory, and 4481/26,126/79,139 µJ energy consumption; Updating only the classifier reduces error by 0.47, 0.73, and 0.40 percentage points, requiring only 0.07/0.20/0.33 M FLOPs, 8.1/21.5/34.4 KB, and 0.98/2.62/4.20 µJ; Updating only the embedding reduces error by 0.73, 0.94, and 0.30 percentage points, requiring 1.04/2.80/4.50 M FLOPs, 3.6/9.7/15.5 KB, and 13.22/35.53/57.01 µJ. Three conclusions: First, learning only user features achieves up to 78% of the benefit of full training with 340 times fewer FLOPs (M model: 0.94 vs 1.20 percentage points); Second, although updating only the classifier has higher energy efficiency (0.98 µJ vs 13.22 µJ on S, approx. 13 times), its error reduction is 58% less than learning embeddings (0.47 vs 0.73 percentage points on S, calculated as approx. 55% based on two decimal places in the table, but the paper text takes 58%), so learning embeddings is the optimal accuracy-energy trade-off; Third, freezing the backbone and using late fusion keeps the peak memory of the largest model under 16 KB (15.5 KB in the table for L), three orders of magnitude lower than the fully trainable version—this magnitude means that while full updates of DS-CNN M (up to 5.5 MB) cannot fit on most MCUs, embedding updates can fit entirely within on-chip storage hierarchies.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Ablation 1 is fusion operators (Table 1). Multiplication is optimal after averaging over four scenarios; Addition follows closely (25.81% on GSC 35 is even slightly better than multiplication's 25.85%); Both concatenations lag behind (BC 26.54%, CC 26.46%), and concatenation is comprehensively worse than other operators outside the baseline in the GSC 10 three-sample tier. A deeper finding is a **negative result**: In the "3 samples per class" split (i.e., taking away 362 speakers from pre-training, close to one-fifth of the total number), all fusion methods on GSC 10 are worse than the no-embedding baseline (6.98%-7.63% vs 6.37%)—user embeddings are not a free lunch; when the backbone itself is compromised by pre-training data, the overfitting risk brought by embeddings may backfire. The paper does not further analyze this phenomenon. Ablation 2 is pre-training data scale (three tiers of speaker set-asides in Table 1), finding that speaker coverage has a much greater impact on error than the choice of fusion method (6.37% vs 4.46%), and the difference between setting aside 1 person and 6 people (5.35% vs 4.46%) can only be attributed to training noise against the background of 99.8% speaker overlap. Ablation 3 is sample number and class number (Table 2, elaborated above), with the pattern being that error decreases monotonically with the number of training samples per class, and the relative value of user information increases with class scarcity. Ablation 4 is model scale (Table 3), finding that the benefit of embedding learning does not grow monotonically with backbone scale—S and M achieve reductions of 0.73 and 0.94 percentage points respectively, while the largest L achieves only 0.30 percentage points, and on L it is lower than updating only the classifier's 0.40—the large backbone's own capacity may have partially internalized speaker diversity, which the paper does not explain.

## Main Contributions

1. Proposes and validates an on-device personalized KWS architecture of "frozen backbone + user embedding look-up table + multiplicative late fusion," moving speaker adaptation from weight space to feature space, structurally avoiding catastrophic forgetting, where registering a new user only requires adding one row to the weight matrix.
2. Under domain shift from unseen speakers, updating only the user projection reduces GSC 35 error rate from 30.08% to 24.34% (relative 19%) and GSC 10 from 5.33% to 4.34% (relative 18.6%) (Table 2), and proves that this method has advantages over backbone fine-tuning in domain shift scenarios with class scarcity (seeing only 8/10 or 20/35 classes during the online phase).
3. Provides complete TinyML cost quantification: approximately 1 MFLOP per epoch for on-device training under a 23.7 k parameter backbone (Table 3: S model 1.04 M FLOPs, 13.22 µJ), intermediate result storage for updates below 4 KB (Table 3: 3.6 KB), embedding update cost per sample below 0.3% of inference cost, and peak memory not exceeding 16 KB.
4. Systematically ablates fusion operators (multiplication/addition/BC concatenation/CC concatenation, Table 1), samples per class, classes per speaker (Table 2), and backbone scale (Table 3), providing evidence for the few-shot lower bound that "4 samples per class is sufficient for effective learning."
5. Establishes quantitative conclusions on the accuracy-energy trade-off in the energy efficiency dimension: learning embeddings achieves 78% of the error benefit of full training with 340 times fewer FLOPs, and 58% more error reduction than updating only the classifier.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, **speaker association is a hard prerequisite**. Embeddings are indexed by speaker ID, and multi-user parallel learning is "conditional on being able to associate each input speech segment with a uniquely identifiable speaker"—this effectively offloads the speaker identification or explicit user switching环节 to the system design. The paper only has one sentence on this, providing no solution and not discussing performance degradation in case of association errors (recording speech under the wrong user name). Second, **no security and anti-spoofing analysis**. A wake-up model customized by user naturally raises the problem of attacker impersonation (whether an attacker can "train" the device to respond to their voice by speaking a few sentences), and the paper does not report any related experiments. Third, **cold start still requires guided registration**: requiring at least 1 validation, 1 test, and 4 training samples per class means users must read each keyword several times to the device, which is dozens of pronunciations for ten words; the registration experience is not zeroed out. The paper also does not explore the feasibility of using TTS synthesis or data augmentation to replace some real samples (although there are precedents for TTS speaker adaptation in the literature they cite). Fourth, **embeddings are static vectors**: one fixed row per user, not dynamically adjusting for distance, emotion, speech rate, health status (e.g., cold), and no discussion of continuous updates and forgetting management between sessions. Fifth, **domain shift only covers the speaker dimension**: This paper's experiments only construct domain shift in the speaker dimension; other real disturbances such as noise, reverberation, and echo do not appear in the experiments (the authors' previous works handled noise domain adaptation, but this paper does not combine them). Sixth, **energy consumption is analytical estimation** rather than Vega hardware measurement; the µJ numbers in Table 3 are obtained by dividing FLOPs by 79 GFLOP/s/W, not including memory access energy consumption and sparse update scheduling overhead; inference latency and end-to-end measured power consumption are not reported in the paper.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

First, the online learning evaluation only has 6 speakers, and the test set has only 1 sample per class per person—the test set scale is extremely small, and the average of 30 experiments can only partially offset variance, casting doubt on the generalizability of the conclusions to a broader population. Second, the negative result in Table 1 (all fusion methods worse than the no-embedding baseline in the three-sample tier on GSC 10) is not analyzed, which is precisely important evidence for the method's boundaries. Third, all baselines are self-built (four update strategies of the same pre-trained model), with no empirical comparison against two-stage schemes like i-vector/x-vector or catastrophic forgetting mitigation methods (such as regularization, replay); the claim of "saving latency compared to two-stage schemes" remains at the argumentation level rather than the measurement level. Fourth, the statement in the conclusion "measured error reduction exceeding 6 percentage points on both GSC 10 and GSC 35" is not entirely consistent with the tables: in Table 2, the maximum gap for embedding-only update relative to the baseline on GSC 10 is about 1 percentage point (5.33% vs 4.34%), and full training is only 3.33 percentage points (5.33% vs 2.00%); on GSC 35, embedding-only update is 5.74 percentage points (30.08% vs 24.34%), none of which exceed 6 percentage points on both tasks simultaneously; the text is somewhat optimistic. Fifth, implementation details such as embedding entry length, number of DS-CNN blocks, and MFCC dimensions are not fully reported in the paper, affecting reproducibility. Sixth, the conclusion that "the optimal fusion operator is multiplication" is scenario-dependent: in Table 1, addition is slightly ahead on GSC 35, and in the class-scarce scenarios of Table 2, the gap between multiplication and other operators is not separately verified.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

First, extend the same framework to multi-domain joint adaptation (speaker plus noise plus reverberation) and unify evaluation with the authors' previous noise domain adaptation methods. Second, make speaker association endogenous to the system: use lightweight voiceprint scores to decide which embedding row the current speech belongs to, and simultaneously include impersonation detection in the evaluation. Third, research on-device embedding updates on the quantization domain (their cited low-precision floating-point optimization work suggests int8 or even lower precision training is feasible), further suppressing the 13 µJ/epoch energy consumption. Fourth, use TTS or voice conversion to synthesize registration data, reducing the user burden of "4 real samples per class" to "1 or even 0 samples per class." Fifth, provide end-to-end numbers for latency, measured energy consumption, and memory usage on real hardware like Vega. Sixth, explore the temporal evolution of embeddings: online rolling updates as the user uses the device, allowing the gating vector to track the slow drift of user states.
