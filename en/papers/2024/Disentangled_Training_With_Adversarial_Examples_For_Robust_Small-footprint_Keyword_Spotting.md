- **Authors/Affiliations**: Zhenyu Wang (University of Texas at Dallas, UT Dallas; this work was completed during his internship at Meta); Li Wan, Biqiao Zhang, Yiteng Huang, Shang-Wen Li, Ming Sun, Xin Lei, Zhaojun Yang (Meta AI)

- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, adversarial examples, disentangled training, auxiliary batch normalization, depth-wise separable convolution, SimAM parameter-free attention, on-device robustness

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS), or wake-word detection, serves as the persistent entry point for voice assistant devices: the model continuously monitors the audio stream on the device, and only "triggers" the system to send audio to the cloud for subsequent parsing when a wake word is detected. This entry mechanism subjects the model to three mutually squeezing engineering constraints. First, hardware limitations: the memory and compute budgets of edge chips are small; the model must achieve both low memory footprint and high computational efficiency, otherwise, the power consumption of continuous operation is unacceptable. Second, the attack surface is infinite: because the model runs 24/7, the vast majority of audio it hears has never been seen during training. There are at least three dimensions of variation—Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), noise type, and speaker accent. Third, the cost of failure is asymmetric. The paper makes a noteworthy statement: a false negative (missing a wake word) frustrates users, while a false positive (environmental noise triggering the wake word) is not only annoying but is characterized in the paper as an "infraction of user privacy," because a false trigger means environmental audio without user interaction intent is uploaded to the cloud. This characterization elevates the severity of the false alarm rate to the level of privacy compliance, not just user experience. The superposition of these three constraints means that "small" and "stable" must hold simultaneously; this is the core tension of this paper's topic.

Another pain point stems from the form of streaming inference. On-device KWS does not feed an entire audio segment to the model; instead, it slides a fixed-length window (900 ms, i.e., 90 frames, for the internal dataset; 1 second, i.e., 100 frames, for public datasets) across the continuous audio stream, making a decision every time the window moves by 10 frames. This means the model is called approximately ten times per second, and each decision independently faces the acoustic conditions at that moment and place—quiet one second, noisy the next, with no opportunity for the model to catch its breath or correct errors. Continuous operation also brings a product effect of power consumption and scale: every additional parameter and every additional multiply-accumulate operation is amplified into battery drain by the listening duration. These engineering details compress "small and stable" from a slogan into a hard constraint.

From a technical route perspective, the paper outlines three generations of KWS solutions in the introduction: keyword retrieval based on Large Vocabulary Continuous Speech Recognition (LVCSR) lattices, which has good accuracy but too high computational overhead for on-device use; small-size, low-latency schemes based on Hidden Markov Models (HMM, a probabilistic graphical method for modeling speech state sequences), using the keyword as a whole or single phonemes as modeling units, which is the classic scheme; and recent methods using Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) to predict keyword internal units frame-by-frame, followed by Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to model time-frequency structures. The success of the CNN route in small-size tasks comes from its effective expression of time-frequency structures and low memory bandwidth requirements. However, regardless of which generation, "robustness in unseen environments" remains a gap—this is exactly what this paper aims to attack.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The current gap addressed by the paper can be decomposed into three layers. The first layer is the blind mixing of conventional data augmentation. Industrial-grade KWS training pipelines are typically a combination of multiple augmentation sources: clean audio, audio with added background noise and speed perturbations, followed by spectral distortion (SpecAugment, an augmentation method that randomly masks time-frequency blocks on the Mel spectrogram). Existing practices treat all augmented copies as the same distribution and "stew" them together into the training data, with BatchNorm (BN) batch statistics estimated across augmentation sources. However, common sense dictates that the feature statistics of noisy data and spectrally masked data are obviously different—this distribution mismatch between augmentation sources has never been explicitly modeled.

The second layer is the "label leaking" problem of adversarial examples. Adversarial examples are samples obtained by adding perturbations to the original input that are imperceptible to the human ear but sufficient to cause a trained network to make incorrect predictions. Adversarial examples are a "free and infinite" data resource; existing studies have shown that training with them brings unexpected benefits: feature representations become more aligned with the salient characteristics of the data, and they are more robust to corruption concentrated in the high-frequency domain. Previous work by Wang et al. at ICASSP 2019 already used adversarial examples as augmented data for attention-based KWS (i.e., the AT baseline of this paper, direct mixed training in Equation 4). However, the adversarial training community has long discovered that directly mixing adversarial examples with original data for training causes the network to overfit to that "specialized adversarial distribution," leading to performance degradation on normal data. This is label leaking. The root cause is that although adversarial and original samples are indistinguishable to the human ear, they fall into different regions of the feature space; sharing a set of BN statistics is equivalent to forcing two different distributions to share the same normalization parameters, contaminating each other.

The third layer is the capacity ceiling of small models. Once the parameter count of edge models is compressed to the hundreds of thousands, the path of expanding capacity by stacking parameters is blocked, requiring "zero-cost" capacity injection methods. Existing plug-and-play attention modules (SE, CBAM, GCT) all introduce learnable parameters, which is not a free lunch for footprint-sensitive scenarios.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

Consolidating the above gaps, the core question this paper seeks to answer is: How to "use correctly" the free resource of adversarial examples—enjoying the robustness benefits it brings without sacrificing accuracy due to distribution mismatch; and to generalize the concept of "disentanglement" from the binary opposition of "adversarial vs. original" to a multi-path scenario of "augmentation source vs. augmentation source," handling all possible distribution mismatches in the training data at once; meanwhile, the entire solution must fit within a small model of approximately 250,000 parameters (derived by summing layer by layer in Table 1), without introducing additional inference overhead.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The backbone network is called MN7-45, a tailored variant of the MobileNetV2 architecture for the KWS task. The core component of MobileNetV2 is the bottleneck residual block: first, a 1×1 pointwise convolution expands the input from $m$ channels to $t \cdot m$ channels (expansion factor $t$); then, a 3×3 depth-wise separable convolution (depth-wise convolution, where each channel is convolved independently, with far fewer parameters than standard convolution) with stride $s$ is applied; finally, a linear bottleneck transformation compresses it back to $n$ channels, combined with an inverted residual connection. The paper states the reason for choosing this structure plainly: the efficient modeling capability of depth-wise separable convolution on mobile devices is well-recognized, and the advantage of the CNN family in small-size KWS comes from good time-frequency structure expression and low memory bandwidth.

Table 1 gives the complete configuration of MN7-45: an input 3×3 standard convolution (45 filters, stride 2, only 405 parameters—inferred from $405 = 3 \times 3 \times 1 \times 45$, the 40-dimensional Mel feature map is treated as a single-channel $40 \times T$ "image"); followed by 7 bottleneck residual blocks, all with $t=6, n=45$, strides of 1, 2, 2, 2, 1, 2, 1 respectively, each block having 26.7K parameters (cross-validation: $45 \times 270 + 270 \times 9 + 270 \times 45 \approx 26.7K$, consistent with the table, indicating the block consists of "expand 1×1 → 3×3 DW → compress 1×1" three stages); then a 1×1 convolution expands to 1280 channels (57.6K parameters), global average pooling, and finally a 1×1 convolution output (2 dimensions, 2560 parameters). Summing the parameters layer by layer yields approximately 247,000—the paper does not report this total directly, but it is derived from Table 1. Compared to the original MobileNetV2, MN7-45 reduces the number of blocks but widens the initial layers; the paper states this ratio is "empirically more effective for KWS," but provides no corresponding ablation experiments, which is a case of giving conclusions without evidence. The output dimension of 2 corresponds to the binary classification perspective of "keyword/negative sample" on the internal dataset; on Google Speech Commands, the model outputs 11 classes (10 command words plus unknown). Additionally, a SimAM attention module is inserted after the depth-wise convolution of each residual block, which is the only incremental addition on the architecture side.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

Three mathematical components. First, the energy function of SimAM (Equation 1): for a neuron $x$ in the feature map $X \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$, the minimum energy is defined as:

$$e^*_x = \frac{4(\hat{\sigma}^2 + \lambda)}{(\hat{x} - \hat{\mu})^2 + 2\hat{\sigma}^2 + 2\lambda}$$

where $\hat{\mu}$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2$ are the mean and variance of the $H \times W$ positions within that channel, and $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter (0.0001 in this paper). The statistics are shared among all neurons within the channel, so the entire module has no learnable parameters. Its theoretical basis comes from neuroscience: studies of the macaque striate cortex show that neuron energy is negatively correlated with its importance (surround suppression phenomenon); neurons with lower energy are more important. Based on this, feature refinement is written as Equation 2: $\hat{x} = \sigma(1/E) \otimes x$, where $E$ collects all $e^*_x$ energy values, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. Translating intuitively: if a neuron deviates significantly from the mean of its channel (large denominator), it carries more discriminative information and has lower energy, so $\sigma(1/E)$ gives it a gain close to 1; conversely, neurons submerged in the mean are suppressed.

Second, PGD adversarial example generation (Equation 3): $x^{adv}_{t+1} = \Pi_{x+S}(x^{adv}_t + \epsilon \cdot \text{sgn}(\nabla_x L(\theta, x, y)))$, i.e., iteratively adding perturbations along the sign direction of the loss gradient with respect to the input, and using the projection operator $\Pi$ to pull the sample back into the allowed perturbation space $S$ at each step, where $\epsilon$ is the step size. This paper uses 8-step PGD, with perturbation strength $\epsilon$ taken from the range $[0.1, 0.4]$.

Third, the mixed training objective (Equation 4): $\arg\min_\theta \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}[L(\theta, x, y) + L(\theta, x^{adv}, y)]$, summing the losses for original and adversarial samples for joint optimization—this is the AT baseline, and the starting point for all improvements in this paper.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Auxiliary BN Disentangled Adversarial Training (DAT)

The approach of DAT originates from the work of Xie et al. at CVPR 2020 on image recognition: each training step maintains two sets of BN—main BN processes only the original mini-batch, and auxiliary BN processes only the corresponding adversarial mini-batch; the rest of the network parameters (all convolution weights) are jointly optimized for both data paths; during evaluation, the auxiliary BN is discarded, keeping only the main BN. Why this design is effective deserves deeper digging: the running mean/var of BN is essentially an explicit modeling of the input distribution. Although adversarial perturbations are imperceptible to the human ear, they are signals deliberately pushing samples to the other side of the decision boundary, so feature statistics must shift. Sharing BN is equivalent to forcibly declaring "these two distributions are the same," causing statistics to contaminate each other; the network, trying to accommodate both data paths simultaneously, can only overfit to the specialized adversarial distribution—this is the mechanism of label leaking. After disentanglement, normalization layers keep their own accounts, while backbone convolution parameters are still shared, so the information flow of representation learning is not broken, only the "distribution assumption" layer is split. Looking further at the implementation level: BN uses current batch statistics for normalization during training, and uses sliding average statistics accumulated during training for inference. Therefore, "sharing or splitting" affects not only training dynamics but also the final set of inference statistics written into the model—after splitting, what is written into the main BN is pure original data statistics, so the model is not biased by the mean and variance distorted by adversarial copies during testing. The cost is running two forward passes for statistics during training, with zero overhead during inference (auxiliary BN is simply deleted).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Data Source-Aware Disentangled Adversarial Training (DA DAT)

This is the most important generalization in the paper. The paper's insight is: distribution differences exist not only in the "adversarial vs. original" pair, but also between various augmentation sources within the original training data—clean audio, audio with added noise and speed perturbations, and SpecAugment spectral distortion audio are inherently three different distributions; why should they share one set of BN? The specific approach of DA DAT (information flow in Fig. 2): clean data $DS_1$ goes through the main BN; each augmented data source $DS_n$ ($n>1$) is assigned a dedicated auxiliary BN; each adversarial sample $Adv_n$ ($n \ge 1$) corresponding to each data source is also assigned an auxiliary BN. During evaluation, only the main BN of the clean data is retained, and all auxiliary BNs are deleted, based on an explicit assumption: the test data distribution is closer to clean data than to artificially augmented distributions. Whether this assumption always holds is questionable (expanded in the Limitations section), but it is self-consistent within the paper's experimental setup. The incremental significance of DA DAT relative to DAT is: it upgrades auxiliary BN from a "companion for adversarial examples" to a "general cross-data-source adaptation technology"—the paper itself points this out in Section 5.1.

Incidentally, the paper also attempted an intermediate form, FG DAT (Fine-Grained Disentanglement): for each training sample, an adversarial copy is generated at multiple perturbation strengths ($\epsilon$ takes multiple values within $[0.1, 0.4]$), and each perturbation level is paired with a dedicated auxiliary BN. This design failed in the experiments, which will be discussed in detail in the ablation section—it is the most informative negative example in the paper.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: SimAM Parameter-Free Attention Injection

Insert a SimAM after the depth-wise convolution of each residual block. The design motivation is clear: the capacity ceiling of small models is low, and attention is one of the few means to "almost freely" expand capacity—relative to plug-and-play modules like SE, CBAM, and GCT that require learnable parameters, SimAM has zero parameters and zero inference structure changes (just an element-wise weighted sum), with the footprint remaining completely unchanged. The paper treats it as an architectural increment orthogonal to the training strategy and validates it separately in experiments.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the adversarial KWS of Wang et al. at ICASSP 2019 (i.e., the AT baseline), the difference is not in "whether to use adversarial examples," but in "whether to acknowledge that the two distributions are different when using them": AT forces mixed training, while DAT/DA DAT分流 (diverts) at the normalization layer. Compared to the original DAT of Xie et al., this paper generalizes the binary (adversarial/original) disentanglement to multi-path disentanglement at the data source level, where each augmentation source and its adversarial copy have independent statistical paths, covering a wider mismatch surface. Compared to conventional augmentation pipelines, augmentation sources are no longer "stewed" together; each keeps its own ledger. Compared to contemporaneous attention-based KWS schemes, SimAM has zero parameters. It must be pointed out that on the architecture side, this paper is basically an assembly of existing components (MobileNetV2 blocks plus SimAM); all true novelty lies on the training strategy side—this paper should be read as a "training strategy paper" rather than an "architecture paper."

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Internal Dataset: Meta's internally aggregated, de-identified, crowdsourced multi-keyword data, containing 4 keywords ("take a picture", "volume down", "volume up", "play music"). Training set: 130K positive samples (~32K per word) plus 100K negative samples; Test set: 32K positive samples (~8K per word) plus 10K negative samples. Notably, the design of the accent dimension: it covers 6 English accents (American, Australian, Canadian, New Zealand, British, Latin American). The training data contains only American accents, while the other 5 accents are used exclusively for testing—meaning the model has never seen non-American accent training samples. Accent generalization is an implicit assessment item, which is closer to the dilemma of real products (where training data often concentrates on a single mainstream accent) than common random splits. Google Speech Commands V1: 64,727 natural environment recordings of 1 second at 16kHz, 30 words, 1,881 speakers; organized into an 11-class task (10 command words up/down/left/right/yes/no/on/off/go/stop as positive classes, other words plus background noise as the unknown class), split into training/validation/test at 8:1:1, following the settings of Warden and Rybakov et al. to ensure comparability.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The internal dataset uses False Rejection Rate (FRR, the proportion of wake words spoken but rejected) and False Acceptance Rate (FAR, the proportion of triggers when no wake word was spoken), plotting DET curves (Detection Error Tradeoff curves, with FAR on the x-axis and FRR on the y-axis), and summarizing FRR at the 1% FAR operating point and AUC (Area Under the Curve). The rationale for this set is fundamental: KWS is a detection task, not a classification task; real-world deployment cares about the false alarm level at a specific operating point (e.g., product specifications require false alarms not to exceed 1%), and a single accuracy number would mask the trade-offs between operating points; AUC provides a comprehensive view of all operating points. The Google dataset follows previous work's top-1 classification accuracy, additionally reporting the relative decrease in Classification Error Rate (CER RD), for comparability with existing literature.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table 2 gives the FRR and AUC at the 1% FAR operating point on the internal test set, under two noise conditions (SNR 20dB and 10dB, test noise types different from training). At 20dB: Vanilla 9.03% (AUC 0.956), NoiseAug 7.33% (0.959), NoiseAug+SpecAug 6.28% (0.968), AT 6.20% (0.967), DAT 5.77% (0.970), FG DAT 7.47% (0.963), DA DAT 4.98% (0.975), DAT+SimAM 5.70% (0.971), DA DAT+SimAM 4.52% (0.976). At 10dB:respectively, 13.59% (0.939), 12.55% (0.942), 11.98% (0.944), 10.04% (0.949), 8.59% (0.960), 10.48% (0.951), 7.15% (0.964), 8.77% (0.956), 8.47% (0.961). Fig. 3's DET curves show the same ranking relationship.

Reading down this baseline chain reveals a detail the paper does not explicitly point out but is crucial to its argument: the benefit of AT relative to the strongest conventional baseline is not symmetric across the two noise conditions. At 20dB, NoiseAug+SpecAug is 6.28% and AT is 6.20%, almost持平 (level)—meaning under light noise conditions, the benefit brought by adversarial training without disentanglement is largely eaten up by distribution mismatch, making adversarial examples almost white noise; but at 10dB, the baseline is 11.98% and AT is 10.04%, where adversarial training begins to show value, and further down to DA DAT's 7.15%, disentanglement fully cashes in this value. This comparison constitutes the paper's most powerful argument: adversarial examples are useful themselves, but must be paired with correct statistical modeling to be released, and the harder the conditions, the more thoroughly they are released. Another detail is the limited marginal benefit of SpecAugment: at 20dB, it drops from 7.33% (NoiseAug) to only 6.28%; at 10dB, from 12.55% to 11.98%. Conventional augmentation methods are approaching their ceiling at this data scale, and the marginal benefit of continuing to stack augmentations is diminishing—this also highlights the scarce value of adversarial examples as a new type of augmentation resource.

Several key readings. First, the improvement of DA DAT over the strongest non-adversarial baseline (NoiseAug+SpecAug): FRR relative decrease of 20.7% at 20dB, and 40.3% at 10dB—the 40.31% in the abstract comes from the 10dB condition (the hardest one) (recalculated from Table 2 original values: $(11.98-7.15)/11.98 = 40.3\%$, consistent with the abstract). The heavier the noise, the greater the benefit, indicating that the value of adversarial examples is realized exactly under hard conditions, which aligns with the intuition that "adversarial perturbations play the role of worst-case noise." Second, Table 3's Google results: NoiseAug+SpecAug baseline 97.32%, AT 97.64% (CER RD 11.94%), DAT 97.74% (15.67%), DA DAT 97.81% (18.28%), DA DAT+SimAM reaches 98.06% (27.61%)—the gain chain of training strategies holds on public datasets as well, and the SimAM architectural increment cuts the error rate relative by nearly another 10%. Third, it must be pointed out: the paper does not compare horizontally with any externally published SOTA models (such as MatchboxNet, BC-ResNet, etc., which have public numbers on GSC V1); all comparisons are within its self-built baseline chain; there is no horizontal table for parameters, FLOPs, and on-device latency—the claim of "small-footprint" is backed only by the layer-by-layer parameters in Table 1. This is a substantial omission for evaluating the paper's relative position.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The ablation design revolves around four variables: disentanglement or not, granularity of disentanglement, data source awareness, and SimAM. Finding 1: The gain of DAT over AT: The paper states in Section 5.1 a relative FRR improvement of 7.23% (20dB) and 13.94% (10dB). A detail worth nitpicking here: recalculating from Table 2 original values, $(6.20-5.77)/6.20 \approx 6.9\%$ and $(10.04-8.59)/10.04 \approx 14.4\%$, which has a slight discrepancy with the paper's stated 7.23%/13.94% (likely due to rounding or denominator differences); fortunately, the magnitude and direction are unaffected, and deep reading should prioritize the original Table 2 values. This comparison confirms that "distribution mismatch between original and adversarial data truly exists, and auxiliary BN can bridge it." Finding 2: The gain of DA DAT over DAT: FRR relative decrease of 13.69% (20dB) and 16.76% (10dB) (recalculation matches Table 2 exactly), indicating that cross-data-source disentanglement earns an additional benefit, with richer multi-source representations. Finding 3, and the most informative negative result in the paper: FG DAT not only failed to surpass DAT but was even worse than the non-disentangled AT (7.47% vs. AT's 6.20% at 20dB; 10.48% vs. 10.04% at 10dB). The paper's explanation is: for a single dataset, one perturbation strength is usually sufficient to constitute an effective attack; multiple perturbation levels instead bring confusion to modeling. Understanding deeper: the benefit of disentanglement comes from "each BN path corresponding to a real existing distribution"; artificially cutting the same distribution into multiple pseudo-distributions is equivalent to thinning the sample for statistical estimation plus forcing the network to distinguish differences that do not exist—the granularity of disentanglement must align with the number of real distributions, not finer is better. Finding 4: The effect of SimAM is not stable: at 20dB, DA DAT+SimAM (4.52%) shows the "marginal improvement" mentioned by the paper compared to DA DAT (4.98%), but at 10dB, adding SimAM degrades from 7.15% to 8.47% (AUC also drops from 0.964 to 0.961)—the paper does not mention this at all, only stating the marginal improvement at 20dB in Section 5.1; this regression must be caught by the reader. Finding 5: Hyperparameter sensitivity: PGD perturbation strength is optimal at 0.2 for the internal dataset and 0.1 for the Google dataset, indicating that perturbation strength is not a global constant and needs tuning per dataset; 8-step PGD, Adam learning rate 0.005 with cosine annealing, 15 epochs, input 40-dimensional log Mel filter bank energies (25ms window, 10ms shift), internal 900ms (90 frames) window, Google 1s (100 frames) window, inference sliding window shift 10 frames, SimAM $\lambda=0.0001$. Finding 6: The implicit assessment of the accent dimension deserves separate emphasis: the internal test set spans five accents never seen during training (Australian, Canadian, New Zealand, British, Latin American). All results in Table 2 were achieved under zero non-American accent training conditions; DA DAT still achieved the largest decrease in the entire table under this setting, indirectly indicating that a significant portion of the benefit from distribution disentanglement comes from generalization to accent variations; however, the paper does not provide a breakdown by accent, nor does it report which accent gains the most and which remains a短板 (shortboard). The training overhead of adversarial generation, and the memory and throughput costs of multi-path BN: not reported by the paper.

## Main Contributions

First, generalizing auxiliary BN disentanglement from the "adversarial/original" binary framework of image recognition to data source-aware multi-path disentanglement (DA DAT), and validating its effectiveness with internal multi-accent data and GSC V1 dual datasets—this is the core contribution at the methodological level, and the judgment that "auxiliary BN is a general cross-data-source adaptation technology" has transfer value. Second, disclosing the negative result of FG DAT and providing a mechanistic explanation (disentanglement granularity must align with the number of real distributions); the value of such failed experiments for avoiding pitfalls in subsequent work is no less than that of positive results. Third, quantitative gains on the training strategy side: relative FRR decrease of 40.31% at 1% FAR on the internal dataset (relative to the strongest non-adversarial baseline, 10dB condition), and 98.06% accuracy with a relative error rate decrease of 27.61% on GSC V1. Fourth, the zero-parameter attention assembly of MN7-45 plus SimAM, validating the applicability of parameter-free attention to small KWS models (although the gain is marginal and unstable under heavy noise). Fifth, positioning adversarial examples as "free and infinite data resources" and providing a safe mining method; this perspective has universal reference significance for data-constrained edge tasks. From a larger coordinate system, this paper stands at the intersection of three lines: the line of adversarial training moving from the security community to a general regularization tool, the line of auxiliary BN migrating from vision to speech, and the line of on-device KWS shifting from competing on architecture to competing on training strategy. Its contribution is not in how new a single point technology is—each component has its origin—but in the systematicity of the combination: treating "distribution mismatch" as a unified perspective, handling adversarial mismatch and data source mismatch within the same framework, and using progressive ablations to cleanly decompose the contribution of each mismatch.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, the assumption of retaining only the clean data main BN at inference has inherent tension: the paper explicitly assumes "the test distribution is more like clean data than artificially augmented distribution," but all internal testing is conducted under added noise (10/20dB) conditions; results under clean conditions are not reported by the paper—if the deployment environment is continuous noise (real working conditions of speakers and headphones), the rationality of using clean BN as inference statistics lacks direct verification. A more thorough solution would be to select or interpolate statistics at the inference end based on the environment, which the paper does not explore. Second, training cost is completely omitted: 8-step PGD means each batch requires 8 additional forward passes and 1 backward pass to generate adversarial examples, plus running BN statistics for multi-path data; the cost in training time and VRAM relative to ordinary training may double—the paper does not report any training overhead numbers, which is a key gap for engineers wanting to reproduce. Third, there is no automated mechanism for hyperparameters: perturbation strength $\epsilon$ is manually tuned per dataset (0.2 vs. 0.1), SimAM's $\lambda$ is fixed at 0.0001, and the migration cost for changing datasets is unknown. Fourth, the failure of FG DAT has only a phenomenological explanation, without deep digging at the statistical level (e.g., how small the batch for each BN path becomes, how much the estimation variance increases). Fifth, the paper does not discuss the regression of SimAM at 10dB (degradation from 7.15% to 8.47%), and the behavior of attention modules under heavy noise lacks analysis.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

The most prominent is the lack of horizontal comparison: on GSC V1, no numbers from any externally published models are cited; none of the contemporaneous strong baselines for GSC like MatchboxNet and BC-ResNet are compared; there is no comparison table for parameters, FLOPs, and latency, so the claim of "small-footprint" lacks quantitative alignment with peers. Secondly, there is no variance and significance testing for multiple random seeds, and 15 epochs of training is also on the short side (whether convergence is sufficient is not discussed); differences of a few percentage points should be viewed with discount in the absence of variance reports. Thirdly, the noise conditions are synthetic (training SNR sampled from $[0dB, +20dB]$, speech and music noise; testing 10/20dB, different noise types), with no real far-field recordings, reverberation, or multi-speaker competition scenarios, leaving a step away from the variation spectrum of real deployment. Fourthly, FG DAT is omitted in Table 3 "for brevity"; it is unknown whether this negative result is reproduced on public datasets. Fifthly, the training of the accent dimension only includes American accents, which is close to reality, but it also means there is no controlled experiment to answer "whether the gain of DA DAT still holds after adding multi-accent training." Sixthly, the relative improvement numbers of DAT over AT (7.23%/13.94%) have a slight inconsistency with the recalculated values from Table 2 original values; although it does not affect the conclusion, it reflects a lack of rigor in the management of numerical口径 (caliber).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Naturally extending along the logic of this paper leads to several paths. First, adaptive statistics at inference: upgrading "keeping only clean BN" to selecting or interpolating multiple sets of BN statistics based on environmental SNR (conditional normalization), removing that suspicious clean distribution assumption, which is a rigid demand for continuous noise deployment scenarios. Second, reducing the training tax of adversarial generation: replacing 8-step PGD with single-step FGSM or free adversarial training, combined with disentanglement, to verify whether cheap adversarial examples plus disentanglement can retain most of the benefit. Third, generalizing data source-aware disentanglement to more sources: reverberation, far-field simulation, multi-speaker, multi-language accent training data; each is a natural independent distribution, and the DA DAT framework can directly accommodate them. Fourth, digging deep into the failure mechanism of FG DAT: the relationship between the number of BN paths and effective batch size, and the growth of statistical estimation variance with the number of paths, which would turn "disentanglement granularity aligns with the number of real distributions" from an empirical rule into a calculable design criterion. Fifth, conducting orthogonal combination experiments with on-device compression methods such as quantization and distillation, to verify whether disentanglement adversarial training still retains gains after full-link compression—this determines whether it can truly enter products. Sixth, addressing the phenomenon of SimAM regression under heavy noise, exploring noise-condition-gated attention or replacing it with other parameter-free attention forms.
