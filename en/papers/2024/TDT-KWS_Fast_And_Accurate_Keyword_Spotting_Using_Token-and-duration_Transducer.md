- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, Token-and-Duration Transducer, RNN-T, streaming decoding, frame-asynchronous search, on-device acceleration, noise robustness

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) refers to the task of detecting predefined keywords in streaming audio. With the rapid development of the Internet of Things (IoT) and smart cockpits, KWS systems, represented by Wake Word Detection (WWD), have become deeply integrated into daily life. The paper clearly points out the engineering essence of such systems: finding a balance among "minimal false alarms, maximal recall, and minimal computational burden." The first two are accuracy constraints—too many false alarms disturb users inexplicably, while too many missed wake-ups render the interaction interface useless; the third is a resource constraint—wake word models are deployed on edge devices and must use lightweight networks to accommodate limited computing power and storage. The tension among these three factors is sharp because wake word systems run continuously: unlike apps called once, they are night watchmen consuming computing power every moment, and any unnecessary computation is amplifiedaround the clock.

The Transducer (RNN-T) has recently achieved success in speech recognition, speech translation, and KWS, making it one of the mainstream architectural options for this task. However, since wake word models must be deployed on the edge, the network must be lightweight. Tiny Transducer (ICASSP 2021, [15]) provided a mature paradigm: using DFSMN blocks for the encoder, a stateless network for the predictor, and a linear layer for the joiner to compress parameters, specifically designed for edge-side streaming recognition; subsequent small-footprint wake word works ([12]) and CaTT-KWS ([14]) all followed the same network architecture, and this paper also partially adopts its configuration. This lineage indicates that "using Transducer for edge-side KWS" has reached a consensus at the architectural level; the remaining issues lie in the decoding and search layers.

The pain point lies precisely here. Despite the excellent performance of Transducer, its autoregressive decoding is computationally intensive and introduces considerable computational latency, which is particularly fatal for KWS running on limited hardware resources. More specifically, regarding the search method: existing KWS search algorithms generally follow a frame-synchronous route, repeating search decisions for every frame—yet the vast majority of frames are unrelated to keywords (silence or non-keyword speech). Paying the full frame-by-frame search cost for a word that appears only once every few seconds is the structural root of computing power waste in such systems. The first technical judgment in the paper's abstract comes directly from this: since most frames are unrelated to keywords, why make decisions for every frame?

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper points out that existing Transducer-based KWS systems share a common technical route and its defects: to the best of the authors' knowledge, all existing Transducer-based KWS systems ([10][11][12][13][14]) adopt the straightforward approach of "running standard ASR decoding and then checking if the decoded output contains keywords." The shortcomings of this approach can be broken down into three layers:

- **Unconstrained search space**. ASR decoding searches for complete hypotheses across the entire vocabulary space, whereas KWS only cares about whether fixed keywords are present or absent. Allocating probability mass and search computing power to a large number of token paths unrelated to the task is neither effective nor efficient (as stated in the paper). Digital evidence can be found in the paper's Table 2: RNN-T KWS using greedy search achieves a macro-recall of only 79.7 on Hey Snips, and beam search with a beam width of 10 achieves only 89.4, whereas constraining the search space to the keywords themselves (Proposed row) reaches 98.7; the gap is even more pronounced on the harder test-other set (57.6 and 60.0 vs. 88.1), a difference of nearly thirty percentage points.
- **Autoregressive dependency increases decoding cost**. In ASR decoding, the predictor is recursively fed partial hypotheses, and each hypothesis token must update the predictor state before joiner computation. The decoding computation grows with hypothesis length, which is costly on resource-constrained hardware.
- **Frame-synchronous decision-making itself is wasteful**. RNN-T search requires a decision at every frame (the step size $d$ in the RNN-T branch of Algorithm 1 is always 1), even if the frame is pure silence or speech completely unrelated to keywords. If frames cannot be skipped, this portion of computation cannot be saved.

On the other hand, the recently proposed Token-and-Duration Transducer (TDT, ICML 2023, [17]) has achieved better performance than standard RNN-T and significant acceleration across a series of sequence modeling tasks by jointly predicting tokens and their durations. Its ingenious design and advantages make it naturally suitable for Transducer-related KWS systems—but TDT's default search algorithm is also designed for ASR tasks, ignoring KWS task characteristics. Using it directly is still neither effective nor efficient. This is the gap this paper aims to fill: the potential of TDT needs a KWS-specific decoding algorithm to be fully unleashed.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The paper aims to answer three interlinked questions. First, how to design a KWS-specific streaming decoding algorithm for Transducer: constraining the search space to fixed keywords while supporting keywords starting at any moment in a continuous audio stream—wake-ups can occur at any frame, which is the essential difference between streaming wake detection and fixed utterance classification; the uncertainty of the starting point makes naive path enumeration infeasible. Second, how to introduce TDT into KWS and utilize duration prediction to achieve frame-asynchronous search, obtaining real, measurable inference acceleration without sacrificing recall, rather than just theoretical acceleration on paper. Third, whether acceleration comes at the cost of robustness: real devices operate in noisy environments; maintaining recall, suppressing false alarms, and sustaining acceleration under low SNR constitute a complete solution usable on edge devices.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The network skeleton of TDT-KWS follows the configuration of Tiny Transducer: the encoder consists of 6 layers of DFSMN, with hidden and projection dimensions of 512 and 320, respectively; the left and right context frames for all DFSMN layers are set to 8 and 2; the predictor uses the stateless implementation in NeMo, with a context size of 2 and embedding dimension of 320; the joiner transforms the 320-dimensional encoder output and decoder output into 256-dimensional representations, which are activated and projected to the final output. The modeling unit is 70 monophones (from the CMU pronunciation dictionary cmudict-0.7b) plus one blank symbol; the TDT model's output units additionally include optional duration outputs. Both models have approximately 2M parameters, fitting the scale of edge-side KWS. RNN-T and TDT use completely identical network configurations, differing only in the duration head—this makes the comparison between the two in Table 3 a clean controlled variable experiment.

The "whys" behind several design choices. First, why use DFSMN for the encoder instead of LSTM or Conformer: DFSMN is a feedforward memory block structure with high parameter efficiency; Tiny Transducer has already validated this route on edge-side streaming recognition, and subsequent small-footprint wake word works and CaTT-KWS have followed suit, making it an engineering-wise, repeatedly confirmed low-risk choice; the paper states it follows the encoder architecture and hyperparameter settings of Tiny Transducer. Second, why use phoneme-level modeling units: wake words are small tasks with fixed vocabularies; phoneme units allow any keyword to be composed of a small set of vocabulary (the 20 keywords in LibriKWS-20 in this paper are all covered by the same set of 70 phonemes), resulting in a lightweight output layer, and changing keywords does not require changing output units. Third, why the overhead of the duration head can be ignored: the extra parameters in TDT used for duration prediction account for only 0.1% of the total parameters (Section 3.2 of the paper), i.e., on the order of two thousand parameters, yet it brings a multiple-fold decrease in search steps—a typical case of gaining large benefits at a very small cost.

Training configurations also follow the conventions of this series: online speech perturbation (perturbation factors randomly sampled from 0.9, 1.0, 1.1); acoustic features are 40-dimensional log-Mel filter bank coefficients, with a window length of 25 ms and frame shift of 10 ms; SpecAugment uses a maximum frequency mask of F=10 and a maximum time mask of T=50, with two applied per sample; 5 frames of context are concatenated on both sides to form 440-dimensional features (40 dimensions * 11 frames), and a frame hop parameter of 3 is set to achieve 3x subsampling. The organization of training data varies by dataset: on LibriKWS-20, LibriSpeech-960h is used directly for training; on Hey Snips, a model pre-trained on LibriSpeech-960h is used for initialization, and then jointly trained on all positive samples of Hey Snips plus an equal number of sentences randomly sampled from LibriSpeech-960h. The reason negative samples of Hey Snips are not included in training is that the negative samples in this dataset do not have complete transcribed text and can only be used for false alarm evaluation—this also explains why sentences from LibriSpeech are additionally sampled to balance the training distribution.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The Transducer consists of three modules: encoder, predictor, and joiner. The encoder takes acoustic features and captures the acoustic characteristics of speech signals; the predictor (also called decoder in the paper) takes text input, usually language units such as phonemes, graphemes, or subwords; the high-level representations of both are sent to the joiner, which generates a distribution $P(v|t,u)$ at each grid point, where $v$ can be any token in the vocabulary or a special blank symbol, and $t$ and $u$ are the acoustic frame index and text token index, respectively. The three modules are trained jointly, with the goal of maximizing the probability of the correct annotation given the acoustic input, and this probability is obtained by summing over all possible alignment paths between input and output—the introduction of alignment is achieved by inserting blanks into the annotation sequence. This is exactly why Transducer-based models require "search": during training, forward-backward algorithms implicitly enumerate alignments, and during inference, the optimal path is found on the grid graph.

TDT makes a key extension on this basis: the output of the joiner is upgraded from $P(v|t,u)$ to the joint distribution $P(v,d|t,u)$, with the new variable $d$ being the predicted duration at that position. The original text adopts a simple conditional independence assumption (Equation 1): $P(v,d|t,u) = P_T(v|t,u) \cdot P_D(d|t,u)$, meaning the token distribution and duration distribution are decoupled, each occupying an output head. The engineering value of this assumption lies in two layers: the duration head can be made extremely small (0.1% of parameters), and the two heads are computed in parallel, introducing no new serial dependencies. Duration prediction $d$ guides the decoder to skip input frames during inference: $D_{max}$ is a hyperparameter preset before training; for example, when $D_{max}=4$, the selectable duration set is 0 to 4; the larger $D_{max}$, the more aggressive the model's frame-skipping behavior. With the introduction of duration modeling, TDT performs frame-asynchronous search, achieving significant acceleration compared to frame-synchronous RNN-T-based systems—this is the direct motivation for introducing TDT into KWS in this paper.

The mathematical skeleton of the KWS decoding algorithm is as follows. Let the acoustic feature sequence be $x$, and the keyword token sequence be $y = \{y_0=\text{blank}, y_1, \dots, y_U\}$. Define the emission probabilities (Equations 2 and 3) using standard Transducer notation: $y(t,u) = P(y_{u+1} | x[1:t], y[0:u])$, $\phi(t,u) = P(\phi | x[1:t], y[0:u])$, i.e., given the first $t$ frames of acoustics and the first $u$ tokens of keywords, the probability that the next token is the $(u+1)$-th keyword element or blank. In the decoding grid graph of Fig. 1 in the paper, each node $(t,u)$ represents the score of the optimal path that has output the first $u$ elements of the keyword up to time $t$; vertical arrows correspond to the probability $y(t,u)$ of emitting a keyword token, and horizontal arrows correspond to the probability $\phi(t,u)$ of emitting a blank. Define $\delta(t,u)$ as the score of the highest-scoring path among all paths reaching node $(t,u)$, using dynamic programming recursion (line 5 of Algorithm 1): $\delta(t,u) = \max( \delta(t,u-1) \cdot y(t,u-1), \delta(t-d,u) \cdot \phi(t-d,u) )$. The keyword confidence at time $t$ is given by Equation 4: $\text{Score}[t] = \delta(t,U) \cdot \phi(t,U)$, i.e., the score of the optimal path that exactly outputs all $U$ keyword tokens by time $t$ multiplied by the probability of continuing to emit a blank at that grid point. Understanding this in conjunction with Fig. 1, multiplying by blank adds a "ending constraint" to the confidence, implying "the keyword ends here, rather than being a prefix of a longer word."

### Key Technical Innovation I: KWS-Specific Streaming Decoding Algorithm

This is the core contribution of the paper (Algorithm 1), applicable to standard Transducer and TDT, and can be generalized to other Transducer variants. The algorithm consists of three interlocking designs.

**Design 1: The predictor only consumes the keyword token sequence.** Unlike ASR decoding, which recursively feeds partial hypotheses to the predictor, this algorithm only feeds the decoded keyword token sequence to the predictor. The reason is that the main goal of KWS is to determine whether the keyword is present or absent, not to generate complete hypotheses. The paper analogizes this to the teacher forcing strategy commonly used in parallel computation loss of RNN-T or training of autoregressive models. The significance of this change goes far beyond saving a little computation: the input to the predictor is fixed from "prefixes of uncertain length" to "keyword prefixes of length at most $U$," allowing probabilities at grid points $(t,u)$ to be systematically calculated based on keyword prefixes, providing the foundation for the well-defined dynamic programming. From an algorithmic structure perspective, the DP overhead per frame is of the same order as the number of keyword tokens $U$ and independent of the vocabulary size—keyword-level tokens are only a dozen or so, so the search overhead is naturally small.

**Design 2: $\delta(t,0)=1$, supporting keyword detection starting at any moment.** In streaming scenarios, wake words may start at any moment; the algorithm sets $\delta(t,0)$ to 1 at each time step $t$ (line 3 of Algorithm 1), effectively allowing the hypothesis "the keyword restarts from this frame" to enter for free. This step solves the most subtle starting point problem in streaming wake detection: there is no need to explicitly enumerate all possible starting moments; the DP automatically maintains the optimal path starting from any point. The paper emphasizes that this is the mechanism by which the algorithm seamlessly supports wake word detection in continuous speech.

**Design 3: Duration-guided frame skipping in the TDT branch.** In the RNN-T branch, the step size $d$ is always 1, degenerating into frame-synchronous search; in the TDT branch, at each step, calculate $d = \arg\max P_D(d|t,G)$ and $v = \arg\max P_T(v|t,G)$, append the predicted token $v$ to the decoded history $G$, and then directly set the Scores of skipped frames $t+1$ to $t+d-1$ to zero, advancing $t$ by $d$ frames (lines 11 to 18 of Algorithm 1). The skipped frames do not produce confidence outputs nor trigger search computations—this is the carrier of frame-asynchronous search. A noteworthy detail here: $y(t,u)$ and $\phi(t,u)$ on the DP grid are conditioned on the keyword prefix $y[0:u]$ (teacher forcing style), while the duration and token distributions determining how many frames to skip are conditioned on the greedy decoding history $G$—the two sets of conditions serve their respective purposes: the former ensures the computability and consistency of path scores, while the latter provides decoded context for frame-skipping decisions.

Why is this algorithm more accurate than ASR decoding? The explanation given in Section 4.1 of the paper is: the search space is limited to the keywords themselves rather than all possible decoding sequences, so the model does not need to disperse probability mass on vocabulary paths unrelated to the task. The numbers in Table 2 are hard evidence: on RNN-T KWS, greedy search improves from 79.7 to 98.7 (Snips) with the proposed algorithm, and from 57.6 to 88.1 on test-other; on TDT-KWS, greedy search improves from 85.4 to 98.9. Based on this, all subsequent experiments in the paper uniformly use only the KWS-specific decoding algorithm.

### Key Technical Innovation II: Introducing TDT into KWS and Converting to Real Acceleration

TDT originates from a previous first-author work by the same team ([17]), originally applied to general sequence modeling tasks. The judgment of this paper is: the design and advantages of TDT make it adaptable to Transducer-related KWS systems—because the vast majority of frames in KWS audio are unrelated to keywords, which is exactly the scenario where duration-guided frame skipping can play its best role. The mechanistic explanation of acceleration: RNN-T requires a search decision at every frame, so the number of model calls equals the number of frames; TDT can span multiple frames at once, so the number of model calls decreases as $D_{max}$ increases. Table 3 shows that acceleration increases monotonically with $D_{max}$: when $D_{max}=2$, relative search acceleration is 1.44 to 1.57 times; when $D_{max}=4$, it is 3.20, 2.03, 1.98 times; when $D_{max}=8$, it is 4.09 times; when $D_{max}=10$, it is 4.19 times (all in the Snips column of Table 3). The $(t,u)$ grid heatmap in Fig. 2 of the paper provides intuitive evidence: for an example sentence in test-clean with the keyword "everything," the significantly lower search frequency of TDT is clearly visible, with vertical yellow dashed lines marking word boundaries obtained from forced alignment. The summary number given at the abstract level is 2 to 4 times inference acceleration (second contribution in the introduction).

It is worth mentioning the two口径 (metrics) of acceleration: relative search acceleration only measures the inference module (search algorithm) itself, while relative runtime acceleration measures the entire system. Frame skipping only acts on the search side, so feature extraction and encoder overhead remain unchanged, meaning system-level acceleration is slightly lower than search-level acceleration (e.g., when $D_{max}=10$, search is 4.19 times and system is 3.58 times on Snips, Table 3)—reporting them separately allows one to see the source of acceleration and the real end-to-end benefits, which is a detail in the paper's metric design.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the "ASR decoding plus keyword matching" route (all works in [10] to [14]), this paper constrains the search space to keywords, fixes the predictor input to the keyword sequence, and explicitly defines keyword confidence using DP path scores, rather than performing post-hoc string matching on freely decoded text—benefiting both accuracy and speed (Table 2 and Table 3). Compared to native TDT-ASR, the default search algorithm of native TDT is designed for ASR tasks and does not consider KWS characteristics; this paper grafts TDT's duration frame skipping onto the KWS-specific DP search; the conclusion in the abstract is that TDT-KWS achieves wake detection performance equal to or better than standard RNN-T and traditional TDT-ASR systems, while significantly accelerating—corresponding to the comparison in Table 2 where TDT-KWS greedy is 85.4 vs. the proposed algorithm's 98.9. Compared to frame-synchronous RNN-T KWS, under completely identical ~2M parameter and encoder configurations, TDT-KWS has equal or better recall on three test sets (Table 3), gaining multiple-fold acceleration additionally, and the advantage further widens under noise (Fig. 3).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Experiments cover three scenarios: fixed single-keyword utterance detection, keyword detection in continuous speech, and detection in noisy environments, supported by three data sources respectively.

- **Hey Snips** (open-source KWS dataset): The keyword is "Hey Snips," with continuous pronunciation between the two words without pauses. The training, development, and test sets have 5876, 2504, and 2588 positive sample utterances, respectively, and 45344, 20321, and 20821 negative samples. Since negative samples lack complete transcriptions, they are only used for false alarm evaluation and do not enter training. The false alarm evaluation set is composed of all negative samples from training, development, and test sets, totaling approximately 97 hours of audio.
- **LibriSpeech and Self-built LibriKWS-20**: LibriSpeech is a 960-hour read English corpus with transcriptions. To simulate the scenario of detecting wake words in a continuous speech stream, 20 words are selected as keywords to form the KWS version corpus LibriKWS-20. The keyword list is shown in Table 1 of the paper (almost, anything, behind, captain, children, company, continued, country, everything, hardly, himself, husband, moment, morning, necessary, perhaps, silent, something, therefore, together); the test-clean and test-other test sets use the same set of keywords. The false alarm set is composed of audio from the test sets that does not contain the selected keywords, with each test set having approximately 3 hours.
- **WHAM!** (noise corpus): Environmental noise recorded in real urban environments such as restaurants and bars, covering various real scenarios. Its test part is mixed with positive samples of Hey Snips at different signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) for robustness evaluation.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Recall is reported at a specific False Alarm Rate (FAR). The rationale: the actual operating point of a wake word system is determined by the false alarm budget—the number of false alarms per hour is the disturbance frequency directly perceivable by users; comparing recall at the same disturbance level has engineering significance. The operating point on Hey Snips is FAR=0.02 times/hour, i.e., 2 false alarms on the 97-hour false alarm set (title of Table 3); on LibriKWS-20, it is average FAR=0.67 times/hour, i.e., 2 false alarms in 3 hours. Since LibriKWS-20 contains 20 keywords, macro-recall and average FAR across keywords are adopted: under multiple keywords, overall performance should not be dominated by a single keyword; macro-averaging gives equal weight to each keyword, which is the standard processing for multi-class retrieval tasks. Inference speed uses two metrics: system-wide inference acceleration (relative runtime acceleration) and inference module execution acceleration (relative search acceleration). The reason for defining them separately is as stated in the methodology section: frame skipping only acts on the search module; mixing them in reporting would dilute the real source of acceleration. Both are measured relative to the RNN-T baseline.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Decoding Algorithm Comparison (Table 2, Macro-Recall)**: RNN-T KWS using greedy search achieves 79.7, 82.5, 57.6 on Snips, test-clean, and test-other respectively; beam search with beam width 10 achieves 89.4, 82.7, 60.0; the proposed algorithm achieves 98.7, 97.5, 88.1—on test-other, it is 30.5 points higher than greedy and 28.1 points higher than beam search. On TDT-KWS, greedy achieves 85.4, 84.6, 63.2; the proposed algorithm achieves 98.9, 98.3, 87.9. The paper notes that for fair comparison, the false alarm levels of each system are kept similar; the beam search implementation for TDT was not available at the time of writing (note in Table 2), so there is no beam search row for TDT. This set of experiments shows that the first source of performance improvement is not changing the model but changing the search: with the same model and same training, simply changing decoding from free search to keyword-constrained search improves recall by ten to thirty points.

**RNN-T vs. TDT (Table 3)**: The RNN-T baseline achieves recalls of 98.1, 99.0, 90.2 on Snips, test-clean, and test-other. Under various $D_{max}$ settings for TDT-KWS: $D_{max}=2$ achieves 98.2, 97.9, 89.3 (relative search acceleration 1.44, 1.57, 1.57 times); $D_{max}=4$ achieves 98.0, 98.5, 91.9 (3.20, 2.03, 1.98 times), where the 91.9 on test-other exceeds the baseline of 90.2; $D_{max}=6$ achieves 97.7, 99.1, 90.3 (3.63, 2.06, 2.03 times), where the 99.1 on test-clean exceeds the baseline of 99.0; $D_{max}=8$ achieves 96.2, 98.8, 90.9 (4.09, 2.06, 2.00 times); $D_{max}=10$ achieves 98.6, 98.7, 89.5 (4.19, 2.15, 2.05 times). Relative runtime acceleration (system-level) ranges from 1.39 to 1.54 times at $D_{max}=2$ to 3.58, 2.04, 1.97 times at $D_{max}=10$. The overall conclusion: under the premise of equal or better recall, the search module accelerates by approximately 1.4 to 4.2 times, and the overall system accelerates by approximately 1.4 to 3.6 times.

**Noise Robustness (Fig. 3)**: Noise from WHAM! is added to positive samples of Hey Snips, with SNR set to 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 dB; the model is not retrained on the noisy data; the legend compares the TDT-10 (i.e., $D_{max}=10$) model, and the x-axis also includes +inf indicating no noise. The results (left subplot of Fig. 3) show that as noise increases, the performance gap between RNN-T KWS and TDT-KWS further widens, with TDT consistently better; meanwhile, TDT-KWS consistently maintains search acceleration over RNN-T at each SNR (right subplot of Fig. 3). The exact point-by-point values at each SNR are not reported in table form by the paper but are presented as a curve graph (Fig. 3). The paper's conclusion is that the TDT model not only performs better in noise but also maintains efficiency through consistent and stable acceleration, making it a promising choice for KWS in noisy environments. As for why TDT is more advantageous in noise, the paper only reports the phenomenon without explaining the mechanism; speculating from a mechanistic perspective (author's analysis, not the paper's viewpoint), frame-synchronous search makes a decision at every frame, and noise makes per-frame token judgments more uncertain, with erroneous decisions accumulating frame by frame, whereas TDT's duration mechanism aggregates acoustic evidence in the time dimension and skips uncertain frames, reducing the opportunity for error propagation.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The most systematic ablation in the paper is the $D_{max}$ scan (Table 3), revealing a task-dependent divergence phenomenon.

- **Hey Snips is insensitive to large $D_{max}$**: As $D_{max}$ increases from 2 to 10, Snips recall fluctuates between 96.2 and 98.6; $D_{max}=10$ still achieves 98.6, and the 96.2 at $D_{max}=8$ is the only obvious low point. The explanation in Section 4.2 of the paper: each positive sample consists of a silence segment plus the single phrase "Hey Snips," so the pattern is relatively simple; the model easily learns which frames contain key speech information, and even if a large number of frames are skipped, it can confidently locate the key frames, thus performing excellently under large $D_{max}$.
- **LibriKWS-20 has a sweet spot**: test-clean reaches 99.1 at $D_{max}=6$ (the highest in that column of the entire table), and test-other reaches 91.9 at $D_{max}=4$ (the highest in that column of the entire table); performance gradually degrades after $D_{max}$ exceeds 6. The paper's explanation is two-fold: on harder corpora, a not-too-large $D_{max}$ allows the model to selectively ignore interfering frames and focus on frames containing key phoneme information, actually improving performance—frame skipping unexpectedly becomes a regularization method for increasing accuracy; but when $D_{max}$ increases significantly, the model may skip tokens that should have been predicted, leading to degradation. This set of findings outlines the trade-off boundary between performance and computational efficiency: $D_{max}$ is a knob, and its sweet spot position is determined by task difficulty.
- **Acceleration increases monotonically with $D_{max}$ but with diminishing marginal returns**: The search acceleration in the Snips column increases from 3.20 times at $D_{max}=4$ to 4.09 times at $D_{max}=8$ and then to 4.19 times at $D_{max}=10$, with gains tapering off; on test-clean and test-other, acceleration basically plateaus around 2.0 to 2.05 times after $D_{max}$ reaches 4. A reasonable explanation is that the effective amount of frame skipping is physically constrained by the number of continuously skip-able frames in the corpus; the length of silence segments has an upper limit, so no frames can be skipped even if $D_{max}$ is larger.
- Another set of generalized ablations is the comparison of the decoding algorithm itself (Table 2, see previous subsection), proving that the first source of benefit is search space constraint rather than model difference; the heatmap in Fig. 2 further corroborates the occurrence of frame-skipping behavior from a microscopic perspective of a single utterance.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed a KWS-specific streaming decoding algorithm for Transducer**: Supporting keyword starting at any moment via $\delta(t,0)=1$, feeding only the keyword token sequence to the predictor, and explicitly calculating keyword path confidence using dynamic programming. This algorithm is general for standard Transducer and TDT, significantly surpassing standard ASR decoding algorithms on three test sets (Table 2: e.g., from 57.6 to 88.1 on test-other).
2. **Proposed TDT-KWS, introducing Token-and-Duration Transducer into KWS for the first time**: Achieving performance equal to or better than standard RNN-T KWS on open-source Hey Snips and self-built LibriKWS-20 (Table 3), with an additional 2 to 4 times inference acceleration (abstract and introduction), while the parameter cost of duration prediction accounts for only 0.1% of the total.
3. **Verified robustness advantages under low SNR**: WHAM! noise addition experiments (Fig. 3) show that the greater the noise, the larger the performance gap between TDT-KWS and RNN-T KWS, and acceleration is maintained at different SNRs—practically significant for suppressing false alarms and maintaining wake-up rates in extreme environments.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Modeling loss from the conditional independence assumption**: Equation 1 decouples the token distribution and duration distribution. This assumption brings extremely small overhead (0.1% parameters) and simple implementation, but token identity and duration are naturally correlated—the duration distributions of different phonemes vary greatly, and decoupled modeling may lose some joint information. The paper does not conduct controlled experiments on the assumption itself, so its impact is unknown.
- **Confidence scores are not normalized**: $\text{Score}[t]$ is the product of path probabilities, and its value monotonically decreases as the number of keyword tokens $U$ increases; theoretically, keywords of different lengths should not share the same trigger threshold. The paper avoids the explicit threshold calibration problem by using macro-averaged evaluation at fixed FAR, but length bias may manifest in actual multi-keyword system deployments. The paper does not discuss this point.
- **Frame-skipping decisions depend on greedy history and are non-backtrackable**: The duration prediction in the TDT branch is conditioned on the greedy decoding history $G$ (lines 11, 12 of Algorithm 1); greedy errors will propagate into frame-skipping decisions, and skipped frames cannot be recovered. The "degradation caused by skipping tokens that should have been predicted" observed at large $D_{max}$ in Section 4.2 is a direct manifestation of this mechanism's lack of error correction capability.
- **Lack of beam search comparison on the TDT side**: Table 2 notes that the beam search implementation for TDT was not available at the time of writing; TDT-KWS only compared the decoding algorithm with the greedy version of ASR decoding, without directly confronting stronger beam search ASR decoding, making the decoding algorithm comparison incomplete on one side.
- **Search cost for multiple keywords is not quantified**: The algorithm is designed for fixed keywords, with 20 keywords evaluated via per-keyword macro-averaging; when the number of keywords increases, each keyword needs to maintain its own DP search, and how its cost scales is not reported by the paper.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **LibriKWS-20 is a self-built simulated scenario**: Selecting words from read corpora to simulate continuous speech detection still has distance from the colloquial pronunciation, far-field reverberation, and channel differences of real wake words; the false alarm set is only about 3 hours, and the operating point of FAR=0.67 times/hour corresponds to a sample size of only 2 false alarms, with limited statistical confidence.
- **Hey Snips negative samples were not involved in training**: Due to the lack of transcribed text, the 97 hours of negative samples were only used for evaluation. The lack of negative supervision may affect the model's suppression of easily confused words; the paper did not attempt to use this data in an unsupervised manner to suppress false alarms.
- **Lack of comparison with non-Transducer routes**: No comparison was made with classic or mainstream small-footprint KWS architectures such as cFSMN, DS-CNN, MatchboxNet, nor a direct comparison with CaTT-KWS, another Transducer-series multi-stage solution, so the position of TDT-KWS in the larger KWS landscape cannot be located.
- **Only a single operating point is reported**: Table 3 only compares at two single points, FAR=0.02 times/hour and 0.67 times/hour, without complete ROC or DET curves, making the relative advantages under different false alarm budgets invisible.
- **Acceleration is relative, lacking absolute values and hardware information**: The paper does not report the test hardware platform, absolute real-time rate, memory usage, and power consumption, only the multiple relative to RNN-T, so the real benefits and energy costs of edge-side deployment cannot be directly converted.
- **Single dimension of noise evaluation**: Only WHAM! additive noise is used without retraining (lack of multi-condition training), and real noise types such as reverberation, multi-speaker, and music are not covered; the point-by-point values in Fig. 3 are also not listed in a table.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper's conclusion itself points out two directions: further exploring the potential of TDT-KWS in more complex acoustic environments; and continuing to optimize the decoding algorithm. Combining the above limitations, several specific routes can be deduced: making the duration head token-conditioned or jointly modeling to relax the conditional independence assumption in exchange for higher accuracy upper bounds; normalizing confidence by length or introducing a calibration method for a unified threshold for multiple keywords, making the trigger logic more stable in macro-averaging scenarios; stacking the KWS-specific decoding algorithm with edge-side compression methods such as quantization and pruning to verify compound acceleration benefits; conducting multi-condition training on real far-field wake-up data and re-verifying noise conclusions; utilizing untranscribed negative samples for false alarm suppression training (e.g., contrastive learning or negative fine-tuning) to further suppress FAR; and generalizing the decoding algorithm to custom wake words and multi-keyword shared search structures to reduce search overhead brought by the increase in the number of keywords.
