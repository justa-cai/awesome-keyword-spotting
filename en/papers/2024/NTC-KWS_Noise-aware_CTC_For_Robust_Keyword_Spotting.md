- **Keywords**: noise-aware CTC, keyword spotting, WFST decoding, wildcard arcs, noise robustness, low SNR, false alarm control

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the primary entry point for human-computer interaction on smart speakers, earbuds, in-car systems, and IoT devices. These systems are deployed on-device and run continuously throughout the day. As application scenarios become increasingly diverse, improving noise robustness under resource-constrained conditions has become a key research direction in the KWS field. CTC-based end-to-end KWS systems are typically deployed on low-resource computing platforms. The dual constraints of model size and computational power create bottlenecks in complex acoustic scenarios: the direct consequence of these constraints is that models with limited capacity are more prone to overfitting, and under strong noise, they confuse background noise with target keywords, thereby increasing the false alarm rate. The number of false alarms per hour directly determines user experience; a speaker waking up on its own amidst TV sounds, music, or restaurant chatter is the most frustrating failure mode for users.

The paper's introduction provides a crucial preliminary experimental observation, which forms the foundation of the entire method: First, under low Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), strong noise energy naturally leads to a decrease in recall. Second, training with simulated data containing extremely low SNR causes the model to overfit to the noise, ultimately degrading overall performance, not just performance at the low SNR level. The authors attribute this to the fact that noise in challenging training data masks parts of the keyword speech, causing the model to confuse noise with target keywords.

This attribution is worth expanding upon because it explains why CTC training becomes "poisoned" on noisy data. The goal of CTC training is to align input frame sequences to label sequences, where the labels provided in the training corpus are transcriptions of clean recorded speech. Figure 1 in the paper provides a specific example: the phoneme sequence for the clean speech "Hey Snips" is HH EY1 S N IH1 P S; when mixed with WHAM! noise, the actual content presented by the speech becomes HH <noise> EY1 S <noise> N EH0 P S. Comparing the two sequences reveals three types of errors: noise frames are inserted between phonemes (insertion errors marked in blue in the figure), IH1 is distorted into EH0, and phonemes are masked or replaced (masking and interference errors marked in red in the figure), resulting in a mismatch with the clean transcription. Under this mismatched supervision, CTC training has no choice: the loss function requires aligning noise-dominated frames to the keyword phonemes in the transcription, which is equivalent to using gradients to repeatedly tell the model "this noise is IH1." The random patterns of noise are thus written into the parameters as evidence for phoneme discrimination. The model learns that "certain noise looks like the wake-word," leading to simultaneous deterioration of false alarms and low recall. This is not a problem of data volume, but rather that the training criterion does not provide a legitimate destination for noise-dominated frames.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing noise-robust KWS works into two branches. The first branch adds single-channel or multi-channel speech enhancement frontends before the KWS module to clean the speech before recognition. The second branch designs new training strategies or network architectures to directly achieve noise-robust KWS. The key commonality pointed out by the paper is that regardless of the route taken, simulated noisy data plays a critical role. However, the second observation from the preliminary experiments恰恰 indicates that simulated data itself has a toxicity boundary—pressing the SNR too low to "train" the model actually damages it. This shifts the problem from "is the data harsh enough" to "how should the training criterion digest noise."

At the level of training criteria, the CTC-based Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) field already has a batch of wildcard modeling works: W-CTC introduces wildcards in the loss to handle inaccurate transcriptions, Star Temporal Classification handles partially annotated data, and Bypass Temporal Classification (BTC) and its successors use bypass arcs to handle defective transcriptions. However, the starting point of all these methods is errors on the text side—transcriptions are wrong, missing, or unannotated. Noise-induced speech-side errors (where the speech content itself is eaten by noise) are dual in problem structure to the former: the former is "labels have less information than speech," while the latter is "speech contains things that labels do not have." No one has previously moved the wildcard idea to acoustic-side noise modeling in KWS, nor has anyone inserted wildcard paths into the KWS WFST decoding graph for training-free inference enhancement. The latter is a key link in how "noise-aware" in the title lands on the decoding end, and it is what distinguishes this paper from all methods that only modify training.

From the perspective of edge deployment, there is another implicit shortcoming: adding a speech enhancement frontend means adding parameters, computation, and pipeline latency. Since KWS is a module that runs resident all day, every milliwatt of continuous power consumption is sensitive. Modifying training strategies often requires redesigning data ratios. The path chosen by this paper is to neither move the acoustic model structure nor add a frontend, but only to change the graph in the training loss and the graph during decoding—the layer most friendly to deployment.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the paper aims to solve three mutually constraining challenges simultaneously. First, how to explicitly model noise-dominated speech segments in the CTC training criterion, so that the model is no longer forced to align noise into phonemes, blocking overfitting to noise at the source. Second, how to truly realize the noise modeling capability learned during training in the decoding stage—if there are no corresponding paths in the decoding graph, the capabilities learned during training are dead assets. Third, relaxing decoding constraints naturally carries a risk of false alarms: giving the decoder the freedom to "skip phonemes" and "absorb noise" allows pure noise to potentially piece together a keyword path. A strict balance must be struck between improving recall at low SNR and controlling false alarms per hour. Transfer costs, exponential decay penalties, and the discarding of all-wildcard paths in the method design are all safety valves centered around the third challenge.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall pipeline of NTC-KWS is: forty-dimensional log-Mel filter bank features are extracted from raw audio, with a window length of 25 milliseconds and a frame shift of 10 milliseconds; the current frame is concatenated with the previous five and next five frames to form the model input, and a frame skip factor of 3 is applied for downsampling to reduce computation; subsequently, it enters a six-layer Depthwise Separable Feedforward Sequence Memory Network (DFSMN) encoder, with 512 hidden layers and 320 projection layers, and left/right context orders of 8 and 2 for each layer; the encoder outputs frame-level phoneme posteriors, where the modeling units are 70 monophones from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary plus one blank symbol; finally, WFST decoding graph token-passing search is performed, and the search results calculate confidence to obtain keyword activation scores. Compared to CTC, NTC introduces at most two special symbols: the self-loop symbol @ and the bypass symbol *. However, note that these two symbols do not increase the output units of the neural network—their posteriors are derived from existing phoneme posteriors via Equation 5 (see below). This is a very restrained design choice.

The reason for choosing DFSMN as the backbone is stated plainly in the paper: it is a lightweight and effective speech encoder, the mainstream architecture for KWS tasks, and previous works by the same team, such as TDT-KWS, have used it. More noteworthy is that all comparison models (CTC baseline, NTC, and various ablation variants) are initialized from the same pre-trained checkpoint and use the same SGD optimizer—strictly locking variables to the training criterion and decoding graph. This allows every numerical difference in the subsequent ablation tables to be cleanly attributed.

The motivation for choosing CTC over end-to-end closed models can be read from the experimental configuration: phoneme-level modeling units combined with WFST compositional decoding mean that changing the wake-word only requires changing the grammar graph without retraining the acoustic model. This is a structural advantage of CTC-KWS relative to end-to-end black boxes (this point is not explicitly stated in the paper, but can be reasonably inferred from its configuration of 70 monophones and a compact grammar graph). Meanwhile, the KWS grammar graph only contains keyword paths and optional background paths, not expanding the entire acoustic space, so the search overhead is naturally controllable.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

First, let's look at how the paper rewrites CTC using WFST. The input acoustic feature sequence is a matrix of frames, and the output label sequence is a sequence of phoneme tokens. Because a blank symbol is introduced to model empty outputs per frame, the number of frames is much larger than the number of labels. The alignment sequence is a sequence of taking ordinary phonemes or blanks at each frame. The goal of CTC is to minimize the sum of negative log-likelihoods of all valid alignment paths given the input, i.e., Equation 1: the loss equals the negative log of the sum of probabilities of all alignment paths that can map back to labels, where the mapping compresses valid alignments back to labels (removing blanks and merging duplicate tokens). Decoding is Equation 2: finding the most likely label, approximated by performing Viterbi in the alignment space to find the optimal path.

The key to WFST implementation is writing the search space as a composition of three transducers: the search space equals the composition of the CTC merge transducer, the lexicon transducer (mapping words to tokens), and the grammar transducer, intersected with the dense emission graph modeling acoustic posteriors. Thus, Equation 3 writes the training as a single expression: CTC loss equals forward computation on the composed graph followed by taking the negative; Equation 4 writes decoding as Viterbi on the composed graph. Token passing is an efficient implementation of Viterbi on the graph; when a token reaches the final node, a decoding path is completed. The value of this formalization is that changing the training criterion and changing the decoding strategy become the same thing—changing the graph. Consistency between training and decoding has a unified mathematical carrier.

KWS differs from ASR in this framework in only two places: the grammar graph is more compact and lightweight, containing only keyword paths plus optional background paths; and the search results need to be converted into keyword activation scores. The paper follows the confidence metric proposed in Reference 22.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Noise-Aware CTC Training Criterion (Wildcard Arcs with Exponential Decay Penalty)

The core action on the training side is to introduce two types of wildcard arcs onto the keyword phoneme sequence in the training graph. Self-loop arcs @ model insertion errors: noise frames inserted between two keyword phonemes, corresponding to the blue noise segments in Figure 1. The self-loop allows the path to stay on the original phoneme for any number of extra frames to "absorb" these noise frames. Bypass arcs * model substitution and deletion errors: phonemes masked or distorted by noise (such as IH1 changing to EH0), corresponding to the red errors in Figure 1. The bypass allows the path to directly skip the eaten phonemes. The concept expressed by combining these two types of arcs is described in the paper's original words as "excessive speech noise and interference."

The acoustic posterior on the wildcard arcs is given by Equation 5: the posteriors for the self-loop symbol and the bypass symbol are equal, both equal to the sum of the posteriors of all vocabulary tokens except the blank at that frame, divided by the length of the non-blank vocabulary. That is, the average of all non-blank phoneme posteriors. Why take the average rather than letting the network directly output two additional categories? Because noise-dominated frames do not have a specific phoneme identity; any non-blank phoneme could appear. The average exactly expresses the neutral assumption of "uncertain which phoneme it is, but it could be some phoneme in the speech." A more practical benefit is that it does not introduce new parameters, does not change the output layer structure, and does not increase training instability. Edge-side models areat a premium, and this design reduces the modification cost of the training criterion to the graph layer. The cost is also obvious: the average erases the discriminative information of "which kind of noise should take the wildcard," leaving room for subsequent improvements.

The second key mechanism is the penalty scheduling in Equation 6: to ensure the model prioritizes learning correct alignments over wildcard paths, a large initial penalty is assigned to both types of arcs at the beginning of training. Subsequently, the penalty decays exponentially with the number of training epochs, where the penalty at epoch $l$ equals the initial penalty multiplied by the decay coefficient raised to the power of $l$. Why is this scheduling necessary? If wildcard arcs were available at zero cost from the first round, the model would find a shortcut—throwing all difficult-to-distinguish frames to the wildcards, resulting in weak phoneme discrimination. This is the classic risk of wildcard-based methods. Heavy penalties in the early stages force the model to first learn phonemes well on clean alignments. As the penalty gradually anneals, wildcard paths slowly become available, and the model gradually learns to "only take the bypass when noise truly dominates." In terms of hyperparameter values, the initial penalty for both types of arcs is -4. The decay coefficient for the self-loop arc is 0.999, and for the bypass arc is 0.975—the bypass arc's penalty anneals significantly faster, meaning the training allows skipping phonemes earlier. The paper does not explain in detail the reason for the difference in values between the two. From the data distribution perspective, insertion-type noise (which introduces large segments of noise frames whenever noise is added to a sentence) is far more common than phonemes being masked. The self-loop arc needs a longer protection period to avoid being used too frequently. This asymmetry can be noted as a reasonable inference.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Training-Free Wildcard Decoding Strategy (Expanding WFST Decoding Search Space)

The second innovation answers an easily overlooked question: if training is improved, what about decoding? The paper observes that although the model learns to use wildcard paths to handle noise-dominated parts of keywords through NTC training, standard CTC-KWS decoding graphs do not contain these arcs. The capabilities learned during training cannot be used during decoding, especially with severe waste at low SNR. To align training and decoding, the grammar graph is similarly modified on the decoding side: taking the keyword "A B C" and a vocabulary containing "A B C D E" plus blank as an example, self-loop arcs and bypass arcs are inserted between adjacent sub-words of the keyword sequence on the grammar graph to obtain a new grammar graph, which is then composed with the transducer and lexicon to form the NTC search space.

Compared to the standard search space, the benefits of the expanded graph are clearly stated by the paper in two aspects. The first is the alignment of training and decoding methods, ensuring consistency—this is a principled reason. The second is more substantial: when noise dominates, requiring the path to recognize each token of the keyword one by one is harsh. Relaxing decoding constraints in complex acoustic environments is a reasonable way to improve recall because the model gains the option to choose wildcard arcs in noise segments. Correct keyword paths will not be pruned by the search due to the collapse of phoneme posteriors in individual frames. The toy example on the right side of Figure 1 illustrates this mechanism intuitively: blue arcs catch inserted noise, and red arcs let go of masked phonemes.

Relaxation inevitably brings false alarm risks. There are two safety valves. The first is to set transfer costs for both types of arcs (the two cost coefficients in Figure 1) to balance recall and false alarms. The second is a hard constraint: if a decoding path consists entirely of wildcard tokens and contains no keyword phonemes, the path is directly discarded. The second constraint plugs the most dangerous loophole: pure noise piecing together a keyword. Training-free is the most engineering-valuable attribute of this technology: applying NTC decoding to a model trained with standard CTC yields benefits without retraining. Subsequent ablation experiments prove this benefit is an average of 2.7%—for systems already online where retraining costs are high, this is an upgrade path that can be directly deployed.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the speech enhancement frontend route, this paper adds no modules, parameters, or pipeline latency. Modifications occur entirely on the graphs of the loss function and decoding, with the deployment form being completely consistent with the original system. Compared to general noise-robust training (multi-condition data augmentation, adversarial training, etc.), this paper does not force the model to harden against noise, but explicitly gives noise-dominated frames a legitimate destination in the training objective, eliminating the erroneous supervision of "noise forced to align into phonemes" from the alignment structure. Compared to wildcard predecessors in the ASR side such as W-CTC, StarTC, and BTC, the problem is dual: they fix label errors on the text side, while this paper fixes speech damage on the acoustic side; moreover, this paper is the first to introduce noise modeling paths into WFST-based KWS decoding and proposes that this strategy can take effect independently without NTC training. Compared to end-to-end KWS, it retains the flexibility of WFST composition, where changing words only requires changing the graph. Compared to the team's own TDT-KWS, the backbone is the same but the problems are orthogonal: the former solves speed and accuracy, while this one solves noise.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Three open-source corpora were constructed for all experiments. LibriSpeech: a widely used English speech recognition corpus, 960 hours with transcriptions, used as pre-training data for the base model and general speech data for KWS fine-tuning. Hey Snips: an open-source KWS dataset centered around the wake-word "Hey Snips"; since negative sample speech lacks transcriptions, and CTC-based models cannot be trained on untranscribed speech, negative sample data is used only for evaluation, not for training—the paper merges all negative samples from the training, development, and test sets into a large test set (following the data preparation method of their TDT-KWS), with the new negative test set lasting approximately 97 hours. WHAM!: contains various environmental noises such as music and restaurant sounds, used to synthesize noisy speech for training and evaluation. Training data construction is divided into three steps: first, pre-train the base encoder with clean LibriSpeech; second, fine-tune the KWS model with Snips positive samples plus an equal amount of LibriSpeech; finally, perform noisy simulation, mixing each clean waveform from Snips and LibriSpeech with randomly selected noise samples from WHAM!, with SNR uniformly sampled from 0 to 20 dB. Clean and noisy speech are merged into the training set. Evaluation data: the negative test set is mixed with noise using the same method as training; positive test speech is mixed with noise at five fixed SNR levels: 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 dB; another set of negative data at -5 dB is created, representing extremely low and out-of-distribution SNR, to examine out-of-distribution limits.

Model and feature configuration: The K2 toolkit is used to build differentiable WFST for training, and OpenFST is used to build the decoding system; six-layer DFSMN, 512 hidden layers, 320 projections, left/right context 8 and 2; output 70 monophones plus blank; two data augmentation methods—online speed perturbation (perturbation factor uniformly sampled from 0.9, 1.0, 1.1) plus SpecAugment (two frequency masks max 10, two time masks max 50 per speech); hyperparameters: decay coefficients 0.999 and 0.975, initial wildcard cost for training -4, decoding coefficients tuned during testing, maximum 20 active tokens during token-passing inference. Edge-side metrics such as parameter count, inference latency, and real-time factor are not reported in the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric is recall at each SNR level, given a specific number of false alarms per hour. Published SOTA models typically report recall at 0.5 or 1.0 false alarms per hour. For fairness, the baseline comparison follows the same setting. However, the paper explicitly points out that this standard is relatively loose—performance across systems is generally strong under such a high false alarm budget, resulting in low discrimination. To reduce the impact of bias and randomness, after the initial comparison, all systems are uniformly evaluated under a much stricter threshold of 0.05 false alarms per hour. The rationale is sound: strict false alarm levels amplify the true differences between systems. A 97-hour large negative test set combined with strict levels is a practice that balances discrimination and stability in KWS evaluation. This metric selection itself is also an implicit contribution of the paper, revealing the illusion of convergent reported numbers across systems under loose levels.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table 1 compares the performance of end-to-end baselines and the paper's CTC and NTC systems on clean Hey Snips. The upper part uses official Snips data: RIL KWS has recalls of 96.47% and 97.18% at 0.5 and 1.0 false alarms per hour, respectively; the WaveNet baseline has 99.88% at the 0.5 level; WeKws's MDTC has 99.88% at the 0.5 level and 99.92% at the 1.0 level. The lower part reconstructs the data setting because negative samples lack transcriptions: WeKws-MDTC is trained only on Snips positive samples (denoted as the positive sample set), and on the 97-hour negative test set, recalls at 0.05, 0.5, and 1.0 false alarms per hour are 89.52%, 98.85%, and 99.29%, respectively; CTC-KWS is trained with Snips positive samples plus an equal amount of LibriSpeech, with recalls of 98.77%, 99.72%, and 99.76% at the three levels; NTC-KWS has 98.97%, 99.64%, and 99.72%. Three conclusions: First, the paper's model performance is comparable to the best end-to-end system under official settings; Second, when trained with the same data, CTC and NTC models exceed end-to-end baselines at the 0.5 and 1.0 levels; Third, at the harshest 0.05 level, the absolute recall advantage of CTC-based models exceeds nine percentage points—strict levels amplify the gap between CTC-based models and end-to-end baselines, conversely indicating that the "tie" under loose levels masks true advantages. Under clean conditions, NTC has only a微弱 (slight) advantage of 0.2 percentage points over CTC (98.97% vs 98.77%), which is expected: clean speech has few noise segments for wildcards to play a role; NTC's main battlefield is noise.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Table 2 is the most informative ablation in the paper: a 2x2 combination of training criterion (CTC or NTC) and decoding graph (CTC or NTC), with recall at six SNR levels from -5 to 20 dB. The four rows of data are: CTC training plus CTC decoding, recalls of 41.6%, 75.1%, 90.2%, 95.8%, 97.5%, 98.3% at each level, average 83.1%; NTC training plus CTC decoding, 41.7%, 74.3%, 89.8%, 95.8%, 97.5%, 98.1%, average 82.9%; CTC training plus NTC decoding, 52.0%, 79.8%, 91.7%, 96.2%, 97.4%, 98.0%, average 85.8%; NTC training plus NTC decoding, 56.6%, 83.4%, 93.2%, 97.6%, 98.2%, 98.7%, average 88.0%.

The first finding is counter-intuitive: replacing only training with NTC while keeping decoding as CTC results in a slight performance drop (average 82.9% vs 83.1%, and even dropping from 75.1% to 74.3% at 0 dB). The paper explains that NTC training itself does not force the model to treat noise as part of speech; wildcard paths are allowed but not forced to be used. Thus, the model's behavior is close to that of a CTC model trained on clean, high-quality speech. This result punctures the intuition that "better training criteria inevitably lead to better performance" and quietly shifts the focus of the paper's method to the decoding side. The second finding: directly swapping the decoding of a CTC-trained model to NTC yields an absolute average recall improvement of 2.7 percentage points, with gains concentrated at low SNR levels (-5 dB jumps from 41.6% to 52.0%, an increase of 10.4%; 0 dB increases by 4.7%)—obtaining core benefits without training confirms that token decoding is the bottleneck at low SNR. The third finding: applying NTC decoding to an NTC model yields another 2.2 percentage point increase, attributed by the paper to the alignment of training and inference processes, making consistency benefits explicit. Combined, NTC-KWS achieves an absolute average recall improvement of 4.9 percentage points over CTC-KWS, with absolute gains of 15 and 8.3 percentage points at -5 dB and 0 dB, respectively—robustness under extreme noise is the core selling point of the full design. Moreover, the 2x2 table allows precise accounting: of the total 4.9 gain, the decoding graph contributes about 2.7, training-decoding alignment contributes about 2.2, and the training criterion alone contributes about 0.

Table 3 performs a grid search on the two transfer coefficients during decoding, where negative numbers represent penalties and positive numbers represent rewards. The optimal configuration is a self-loop coefficient of 4 and a bypass coefficient of 2, i.e., both coefficients take positive values, with recalls of 56.6%, 83.4%, 93.2%, 97.6%, 98.2%, 98.7% at each level, average 88.0%. The information in this result is that it indicates the NTC model does not overfit to wildcard shortcuts during training; instead, during inference, positive weighting is needed for wildcard paths to compete with real phoneme arcs—this corroborates that the average posterior in Equation 5 is naturally low, and also corroborates the necessity of the annealing schedule. When coefficients are negative, the improvement is weaker than when positive but still exceeds the CTC baseline, indicating that even with penalties, the additional paths on the graph still have search value. When both coefficients are set to negative infinity, NTC-KWS behavior degrades to Model F in Table 2, i.e., the decoding strategy collapses back to CTC—completely closing off wildcard arcs is equivalent to not adding them. Regarding sensitivity, the decoding stage is far more sensitive to the bypass coefficient than the self-loop coefficient: as the self-loop coefficient drops from 4 to 2, 0, and negative infinity, the average recall slowly drops from 88.0% to 86.0%, losing 0.2 to 2.0 points per level; whereas as the bypass coefficient rises from 2 to 4, the average recall crashes directly from 88.0% to 81.5%, with the -5 dB level dropping from 56.6% to 40.0%. Why is the bypass arc so dangerous? Because self-loop arcs only stay on the same phoneme for a few extra frames, belonging to mild insertion absorption; bypass arcs directly skip keyword phonemes. If the cost is too low, the decoder will easily let go of phonemes that, although interfered with by noise, could have been recognized. This damages both alignment quality and trigger discrimination, sharply increasing false alarm pressure. The paper admits at the end that parameters were only searched under the current noise settings, and future plans include further research on these two decoding parameters across noise types.

## Main Contributions

The paper lists three contributions, each backed by experiments. First, proposing a noise-aware CTC training criterion that models noise-dominated speech segments in complex acoustic scenarios, preventing the model from overfitting to noise—mechanistically achieved through self-loop and bypass wildcard arcs plus exponential decay penalties, with Equations 5 and 6 providing the complete formulation. Second, being the first to adopt a training-free decoding strategy that incorporates noise modeling paths into WFST-based KWS decoding—improving performance in noisy environments without relying on NTC training (average +2.7% in Table 2), and further improving when stacked into the NTC-KWS framework (another +2.2%). Third, NTC-KWS comprehensively outperforms SOTA end-to-end systems and CTC-KWS baselines under both clean and noisy conditions, with significant improvements in extreme scenarios—achieving SOTA on clean Hey Snips (Table 1), an absolute average recall increase of 4.9 percentage points across all SNRs, and gains of 15 and 8.3 percentage points at -5 and 0 dB, respectively (Table 2). From a broader perspective, the methodological contribution of this paper is moving "noise robustness" from the data and model structure levels to the alignment structure and decoding graph levels: noise is no longer an object to be fought or filtered, but is explicitly modeled as a first-class citizen in the search space.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, the wildcard posterior takes the average of non-blank phonemes (Equation 5). While this design has zero parameter overhead, it flattens the discriminative information of noise segments—the model cannot distinguish between "stationary noise that can be absorbed" and "real speech that should not take the bypass." It can only rely on transfer costs to gatekeep on the decoding side. The extreme sensitivity of the bypass coefficient in Table 3 is a downstream symptom of this defect. Second, the effectiveness of the method is heavily biased toward the decoding side: Table 2 proves that the training criterion alone is ineffective (Row F is even slightly lower than Row D), and all gains must be realized through the decoding graph. This means the essence of the method is "trading expanded search space for recall," and the acoustic model itself does not become more robust; the quality of phoneme posteriors output by the encoder under extreme noise remains unchanged. Third, there are two sets of hyperparameters (initial penalty and two decay coefficients during training, two transfer coefficients during decoding), totaling five knobs. Among them, the decoding coefficients are tuned during testing, posing a risk of overfitting to the test distribution. The generalization across noise scenarios is admitted by the paper as needing verification. Fourth, the transfer cost is globally constant and does not adapt to the actual SNR or noise estimation of the current frame. Ideally, the cost of the bypass arc should decrease only when noise dominance is certain.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First, all experiments use a single English wake-word "Hey Snips," failing to cover multi-keyword, Chinese wake-word, or user-defined wake-word scenarios. The latter two are precisely the application areas where WFST-based CTC-KWS has the most structural advantages. Second, the noise source is singular (WHAM!), and training and test negative samples are mixed with noise from the same distribution (uniformly 0 to 20 dB). Strictly speaking, this belongs to in-domain evaluation. Although -5 dB is labeled as out-of-distribution, the noise type is still from the same source, lacking the test of real-collected noise, reverberation, and multi-speaker interference. Third, the negative test set is mixed with noise in a manner completely consistent with training, meaning the model has seen the exact same noise library sampling, discounting the strength of the robustness conclusions. Fourth, edge-side metrics are entirely absent: parameter count, per-frame inference latency, real-time factor, memory usage, and the additional search overhead of the expanded decoding graph relative to the standard graph are not reported by the paper. Yet the motivation stated at the beginning is low-resource platforms—the setting of a maximum of 20 tokens implies controllable search overhead, but there are no numerical supports. Fifth, there are no direct comparative experiments with the speech enhancement frontend route, making it impossible to quantify the true position of "changing the graph without adding a frontend" in this track.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

Following the paper's own outlook, the most direct is to systematically study the value patterns of the two decoding coefficients across noise types and SNR distributions. The ideal goal is to move from grid search to having theoretical guidance or adaptability. Promising directions at the method level include: upgrading the wildcard posterior from a simple average to a learnable noise discrimination function (e.g., using noise dominance probability as a gate), making "when to take the bypass" an output of the model rather than a fixed cost on the decoding side; making the transfer cost a schedule dynamically adjusted based on frame-level SNR estimation; and migrating the idea of noise modeling arcs from CTC to the Transducer family (RNN-T, TDT, etc.). Although the alignment mechanisms differ, the problems are isomorphic. At the evaluation level, supplementing multi-wake-word, multi-language, real far-field noise, and edge-side overhead measurements is necessary to fully answer the resource-constrained scenario proposition established at the beginning of the paper. From a series perspective, this paper and TDT-KWS in the library constitute two branches of the same team "performing surgery on the CTC graph for KWS"—one uses token and duration modeling to compress decoding steps for speed, while the other uses wildcard arcs to expand the search space for stability. The two naturally have a combination space at the graph operation level, which is a convergence direction worth noting for subsequent work.
