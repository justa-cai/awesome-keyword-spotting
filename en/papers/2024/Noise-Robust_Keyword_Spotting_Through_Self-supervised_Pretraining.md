## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Modern voice assistants are deployed on almost all computers and smart devices, but their core automatic speech recognition (ASR) models are computationally intensive and cannot run directly on small embedded devices. The industry-standard architecture places the ASR on a remote server, while a keyword spotting (KWS) algorithm runs locally on the device as a trigger—the device only listens for specific wake words, and only sends audio to the cloud after being awakened. KWS has significantly lower computational requirements than ASR and can run on devices with limited computing power (Introduction, Reference [1]). This architecture dictates two inherent characteristics of KWS: First, it is a 24/7 always-on frontend on the device, facing extremely diverse usage environments—kitchens, streets, buses, cafes, meeting rooms with multiple speakers—where the input signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) can be as low as −10 dB or worse; Second, it is the gatekeeper of the entire voice interaction chain. If it fails to wake up or triggers falsely, no matter how powerful the subsequent ASR is, it is meaningless. Therefore, noise robustness is not a nice-to-have for KWS, but a hard threshold for deployment.

The second pain point is data. Current state-of-the-art KWS models are based on deep neural networks (Introduction, References [1][2]). The mainstream training method is supervised learning, which requires large-scale labeled data to achieve good performance. Manual annotation is both time-consuming and expensive. The idea of self-supervised learning (SSL) directly addresses this pain point: constructing pseudo-targets from the data itself to learn good representations of the data domain without relying on labels (References [3][4]). For KWS, a task with a small vocabulary but requiring massive recordings to cover various speakers and environments, unlabeled recordings are much cheaper than labeled ones, making the leverage effect of SSL very obvious.

The third background is the research lineage of the author team. Their previous work (Reference [6], Bovbjerg & Tan, ICASSPW 2023) already used the Data2Vec framework to pretrain a transformer-based KWS model on unlabeled data, demonstrating that accuracy significantly outperforms pure supervised training when labeled data is limited. However, that work assumed clean audio inputs. In reality, KWS is deployed in diverse and potentially noisy environments. Whether the gains under clean conditions can transfer to noisy conditions is the gap left by the previous work and the direct starting point of this paper.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

Reviewing the paper, existing work has three layers of shortcomings in "noise-robust KWS":

First, research on noise robustness in KWS is almost entirely confined within the supervised paradigm. Representative methods include multi-style training (MTR): adding noise to the data during training to force the model to adapt to noise (Reference [11], Prabhavalkar et al. used this for small-scale KWS as early as 2015); there are also adversarial training and learnable filterbanks (References [1][9]). These methods share a common structural defect: the way they improve robustness is by making the model "see noise," essentially treating noise as another style to be memorized. Their gains heavily depend on the match between training noise and test noise. The experiments in this paper provide quantitative evidence: for noise types seen during training, the advantage of MTR can be maintained below 10 dB SNR; when switching to unseen noise, the advantage is overtaken by the model pretrained on clean data at 5 dB (cross-point comparison in Table 4 and Table 5). Additionally, MTR has a hidden cost: training with noisy data dilutes accuracy under clean conditions. The authors'实测 (actual measurement) shows that Baseline-MTR's clean accuracy is 0.800, lower than Baseline-Clean's 0.832 (Table 4).

Second, the gain of self-supervised learning on noise robustness has been studied in ASR-related tasks, but has not transferred to KWS. Reference [7] performed noise-robust self-supervised speech representation learning for ASR; Reference [8] (Robust Data2Vec) combined Data2Vec with contrastive loss to improve the noise robustness of large transformer ASR models. However, research in the KWS direction still focuses on supervised methods, and "using self-supervised pretraining to improve KWS noise robustness" was under-explored at the time (original text). This leaves an unresolved scientific question: Does the improvement in representation quality learned by SSL only result in accuracy gains under clean conditions, or is it a more fundamental improvement in tolerance to input degradation? No one has systematically tested this.

Third, although previous work [6] proved that Data2Vec pretraining is effective for KWS with scarce labels, its evaluation all assumed clean inputs. It neither answered performance under noise nor explored "how to inject noise information into the pretraining objective." Can the noise-adding approach of MTR be directly moved to self-supervised pretraining (feeding noisy data to both student and teacher)? Or is a more clever construction needed? These are all blanks.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, this paper aims to answer four interlinked questions. First, the scientific question: Is the robustness brought by self-supervised pretraining a real gain or an illusion—can a model pretrained on clean data (never touching any noise during training) beat a supervised MTR model trained directly with noisy data under noisy conditions? Second, the methodological question: How to inject noise into self-supervised pretraining—which is more effective for learning noise-invariant representations: direct symmetric noise addition (both student and teacher see noise) or constructing a denoising objective (teacher sees clean, student sees noise)? Third, the fairness of comparison: SSL methods must be compared with supervised SOTA methods (MTR) under the same labeling budget, and evaluated separately under conditions of seen and unseen training noise, to distinguish "generalization robustness" from "noise memorization." Fourth, the scale consistency question: Do pretraining gains vary with model size—the paper uses three parameter scales of KWT models (0.6M, 2.4M, 5.4M) (Section 2.1) to verify the universality of the conclusions. Together, these four questions constitute a complete small-scale systematic study.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire KWS system follows the classic deep KWS three-stage pipeline: speech feature extraction, deep neural network acoustic model, and posterior processing (Fig. 1). The feature end uses 40-dimensional MFCCs, with a window length of 30 ms and a frame shift of 10 ms, followed by a linear layer projecting to the input dimension of the acoustic model (Section 2.1). The choice of MFCC over more modern log-mel or learnable frontends is motivated by the need to maintain comparability with the settings of previous work [6]. The acoustic model is the Keyword Transformer (KWT, derived from Reference [2]): 12 Transformer encoder blocks, with cosine positional encoding added to the input side, and the outputs at each time step are average-pooled and fed into a multi-layer perceptron for classification (Fig. 1). This design involves two notable trade-offs: using mean pooling to compress variable-length frame-level representations into a sentence-level vector is because GSC recordings are fixed at one second, and the task is essentially whole-word classification, not requiring CTC or alignment decoding; choosing a pure encoder transformer instead of CNN/RNN is because the Data2Vec pretraining teacher-student framework naturally acts on the encoder's hidden state representations, where frame-level hidden states can directly serve as pretraining regression targets. SSL and the architecture mutually enhance each other.

To test the impact of model size, the paper follows the configuration of [6], varying the number of attention heads from 1 to 3 and the encoder dimension from 64 to 192, resulting in three models: KWT-1 (0.6M parameters), KWT-2 (2.4M), and KWT-3 (5.4M) (Section 2.1). This size span is designed conservatively—all within the acceptable range for edge devices, exposing the issue of "whether data volume matches capacity." Subsequent experiments indeed showed that KWT-2 slightly outperformed the largest KWT-3 (Table 3), indicating that with 18.9 hours of pretraining data and 4.7 hours of fine-tuning data, the 5.4M parameter model has passed its sweet spot.

The supervised baseline method is MTR: noisy copies are mixed into the training data, which the paper uses as one of the supervised baselines (Section 2.2).

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

Pretraining adopts the Data2Vec framework (Reference [5]), whose core is teacher-student self-distillation. Two branches use identical transformer encoders, but with different inputs: the teacher receives the complete (unmasked) input, while the student receives the masked input; the student's task is not to predict labels, but to predict the teacher's hidden layer representations at the masked time steps. Specifically, the target is taken as the average of the outputs of the top K blocks of the teacher network at the masked time steps. Let the network have L blocks in total, $\hat{a}_l^t$ be the normalized output of the $l$-th block at time step $t$, then the training target at step $t$ is (Equation 1):

$$y_t = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{l=L-K+1}^{L} \hat{a}_l^t$$

The student is updated via standard backpropagation, with the loss being the mean squared error (MSE) between its prediction $y_t'$ and the target $y_t$. The teacher's weights do not receive gradients but are updated as an exponential moving average (EMA) of the student's weights: $\Delta := \tau \Delta + (1-\tau)\theta$, where $\theta$ is the student weight, $\Delta$ is the teacher weight, and $\tau$ is the smoothing coefficient (Section 2.3). It is worth emphasizing the meaning of "normalization" here: instance normalization is performed on the output of each layer of the teacher across samples before averaging, to prevent the regression target from being dominated by individual abnormal activation values and to balance variance—this is a key detail that allows Data2Vec to train stably compared to early BYOL-style self-distillation (specific normalization implementation details are not reported in the paper, following Reference [5]).

The ingenuity of this objective function lies in "predicting one's own future representation": the teacher is the historical average of the student, more stable than the student, providing higher-quality targets; predicting the average of multiple top blocks rather than a single block output is equivalent to a multi-scale smoothing constraint on the representation. The masking strategy follows the temporal masking of wav2vec 2.0 (Reference [4]): randomly sample several MFCC vector start points, replace the subsequent 10 consecutive MFCC vectors with learnable mask token embeddings, with an overall masking probability of 0.65 (Section 3.2). This ratio of 0.65 is significantly higher than the BERT-style 0.15 because speech frames have high redundancy; if not masked enough, the task degenerates into interpolation.

The supervised baseline and fine-tuning share a set of training configurations (Section 3.1): 140 epochs, batch size 512, cross-entropy loss, AdamW optimizer, maximum learning rate 1e-3, weight decay 0.1, linear warmup for the first 10 epochs followed by cosine annealing, and SpecAugment applied randomly in time and feature dimensions during training. Other hyperparameters for pretraining follow [6] (mostly based on the original Data2Vec paper [5], with minor adjustments due to dataset and hardware differences; details of adjustments are not reported item by item). The only difference in fine-tuning is that the transformer encoder weights are initialized from the pretrained model (Section 3.2). In terms of engineering overhead: on a single Nvidia A40 (48 GB VRAM, 32-core CPU), the largest model takes 2 hours for pretraining and 15 minutes for fine-tuning (Section 3)—the pretraining cost is quite low, which is a dividend brought by 18.9 hours of unlabeled audio, and also shows that this method is fully reproducible at the laboratory scale.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Data2Vec-denoising Asymmetric Noise Pretraining

This is the most core modification of the paper. On top of standard Data2Vec, the input pairing is changed from "teacher clean / student masked" to "teacher clean / student masked and noisy": the input waveform of the student branch is mixed with background noise, while the teacher branch still receives the corresponding clean signal (Fig. 2, green path). Since the hidden layer representation target produced by the teacher on clean input is noise-free, the student is forced to learn a mapping from noisy input to clean latent representation—using one target to accomplish both "learning good speech representations" and "denoising" simultaneously (Abstract and Section 2.3 original text). The ingenuity of this design lies in the fact that it does not touch the loss function or add any new modules; it simply changes the data flow pairing, thereby free-loading denoising supervision into the self-supervised objective. Compared to Reference [8] introducing contrastive loss in Robust Data2Vec for combined learning, the modification in this paper is purely at the data level and almost zero cost in engineering. Another implicit premise is: the teacher's EMA weights are updated from a student that is continuously trained on noisy inputs, but the teacher's forward pass consumes clean features, so the target side remains always clean—the denoising pressure falls entirely on the student's representation learning.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Data2Vec-noisy Symmetric Noise Pretraining (Strict Control Design)

The second modification looks simple: add a data augmentation step before pretraining, adding noise to the input waveform, so that both student and teacher receive noisy inputs (Fig. 2, blue path). On its own, this is a routine augmentation technique, but in the experimental design, it plays a crucial control role: it distinguishes the two possible pathways for "injecting noise information into pretraining"—symmetric noise addition only provides exposure to the noise domain distribution (the model has seen what noise looks like), while asymmetric noise addition provides explicit denoising supervision (the model is required to restore clean representations from noise). The experimental results prove the great value of this control: the average accuracy of Data2Vec-noisy is almost identical to "clean pretraining + MTR fine-tuning" (0.692 vs 0.693 for KWT-1 seen noise, Table 3), indicating that the gains from symmetric noise addition highly overlap with those from MTR fine-tuning—both are merely noise exposure; while Data2Vec-denoising takes another step up (0.708), the extra margin is precisely the unique contribution of the denoising objective. Without this control, it would be impossible to converge the conclusion to "denoising objectives are the key."

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Evaluation Protocol with Label Scarcity and Noise Visibility Separation

The third innovation is less of an algorithm and more of an evaluation protocol, but it is crucial for the credibility of the conclusions. The protocol has three pillars: First, label scarcity setting—splitting the original training set, using 80% only for unlabeled pretraining, and only 20% of labeled data for supervised training and fine-tuning (Section 2.4). This not only fits the reality that "unlabeled data is cheap, labeled data is expensive," but also amplifies the marginal value of pretraining; Second, separation of training noise and test noise—training only uses four types of noise: BUS, PED, STR, SSN, while BBL and CAF are completely reserved as unseen noise for testing (Section 2.4), thereby splitting robustness into two independent dimensions: "performance on seen noise" and "generalization on unseen noise"; Third, full grid testing—six noises × seven SNRs (−10 to 20 dB, step 5 dB) totaling 42 test sets (Section 2.4), plus the clean condition, and running each method on three model sizes (Table 2 lists the pretraining and fine-tuning data combinations for all six training methods). This protocol allows any conclusion to be cross-validated: whether a method is consistent on seen and unseen, consistent across different sizes, and consistent across different SNR segments.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to supervised MTR (Reference [11]), the difference in this paper's method lies in the source of robustness: MTR relies on having seen noise during training, and its gains decay with the match degree of noise types, coming at the cost of clean accuracy (dropping from 0.832 to 0.800 in Table 4); SSL pretraining relies on learning representation priors closer to the essence of speech, which is effective for unseen noise, and clean accuracy actually increases (0.887). Compared to Robust Data2Vec (Reference [8]), this paper does not modify the loss function (no contrastive term added), only reconstructs the input pairing, with a smaller model (0.6M–5.4M vs large ASR transformers), and the task changes from ASR to KWS classification. Compared to previous work [6], this paper extends evaluation from clean conditions to 42 noise conditions, and for the first time answers "where does the robustness of pretraining come from and how to further enhance it." Compared to supervised side patches like adversarial training and filterbank learning (Reference [9]), this paper's approach is orthogonal to them—pretraining and these methods can theoretically be stacked.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Training and evaluation are both based on Google Speech Commands V2 (Reference [12]): a total of 105,829 relatively clean recordings, covering 35 keywords, each 1 second long, with a fairly uniform distribution of keywords (Section 2.4). The split method is: 80% of the original training data is used as the unlabeled pretraining set, and only 20% of labeled data is used for supervised training (Section 2.4). The scale of each partition is shown in Table 1: pretraining 67,874 recordings (18.9 hours), fine-tuning training 16,969 recordings (4.7 hours), validation 9,981 recordings (2.8 hours), testing 11,005 recordings (3.1 hours), totaling 105,820 recordings—a discrepancy of 9 recordings from the 105,829 mentioned in the text, likely due to rounding or differences in handling corrupted files.

There are six types of noise: bus (BUS), pedestrian (PED), street (STR), speech-shaped noise (SSN), multi-speaker babble (BBL), and cafe (CAF). Among them, BUS, PED, STR, and CAF are from the CHiME3 dataset (Reference [13]), while BBL and SSN are generated by Reference [14] (Kolbæk et al.'s LSTM speech enhancement work). During training noise addition, one type is randomly selected from BUS, PED, STR, SSN, and the SNR is uniformly drawn from seven levels: {−10, −5, 0, 5, 10, 15, 20} dB; to ensure that clean speech still appears during training, only 50% of the training data is noisy (Section 2.4). The test set is generated by combining six noises × seven SNRs, totaling 42 (Section 2.4). A point worth noting is a potential inconsistency in terminology: Section 2.4 text says the training noises are BUS, PED, STR, SSN, while the title of Table 2 writes "the noise types used are: BUS, BBL, PED, and STR"—an inconsistency between BBL and SSN, likely a typo; reproduction should rely on the open-source code.

### Definition and Rationale of Evaluation Metrics

The metric used is classification accuracy (Section 3.3). The rationale is clearly stated in the paper: the 35 keyword categories are fairly uniformly distributed, so accuracy will not be distorted by class imbalance, and macro-average is not needed. Evaluation is performed one noise type at a time, first averaging the accuracy across noise types at the same SNR to get the average accuracy for that SNR, then averaging separately for the seen and unseen noise groups; additionally, the clean condition is evaluated separately (Section 3.3). The "average accuracy" in Table 3 is the overall mean after averaging all SNRs (including the clean condition) (Table 3 title). The paper also reports the "average relative accuracy difference" between the best self-supervised method and the best supervised method (i.e., relative improvement percentage), used to compare the magnitude of gains across model sizes (Section 4). It should be pointed out that using only accuracy as a KWS paper is somewhat simplified: real wake-word systems care about the trade-off between false alarm rate and miss rate (e.g., detection rate at fixed false alarm rate); these ROC-like metrics are not reported in the paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

There are six training methods in total for comparison (Table 2): two pure supervised baselines—Baseline-Clean (trained on clean data) and Baseline-MTR (trained on mixed noisy and clean data, i.e., the strongest robustness method on the supervised side); four Data2Vec variants—Data2Vec-clean (clean pretraining + clean fine-tuning), Data2Vec-clean+noisy (clean pretraining + MTR fine-tuning), Data2Vec-noisy (symmetric noise pretraining + MTR fine-tuning), Data2Vec-denoising (teacher clean / student noisy pretraining + MTR fine-tuning). "Noisy + Clean" always refers to 50% data unchanged and 50% noisy (Table 2 title).

The overall leaderboard (Table 3, averaging all SNRs and noise types, including clean condition) presents a clear hierarchy. Taking KWT-1 as an example: Baseline-Clean 0.524 (seen)/0.532 (unseen), Baseline-MTR 0.609/0.592, Data2Vec-clean 0.606/0.601, Data2Vec-clean+noisy 0.693/0.676, Data2Vec-noisy 0.692/0.676, Data2Vec-denoising 0.708/0.688—Data2Vec-denoising ranks first in all six combinations of three model sizes, seen and unseen (0.717/0.700 for KWT-2, 0.676/0.661 for KWT-3). The paper reports the average relative improvement of the best self-supervised method over the best supervised method (Baseline-MTR): for seen noise, KWT-1 16.26%, KWT-2 16.96%, KWT-3 16.35%; for unseen noise, 16.22%, 17.45%, 18.04% (Section 4)—the gains are not only large but stable across sizes, and the relative gain on unseen noise slightly increases with model size. Another structural finding is that the total mean of KWT-2 (optimal 0.717) is slightly better than the larger KWT-3 (0.676), indicating that under the data scale of this experiment, the 5.4M parameter model has overfitted or been undertrained, and capacity does not match data volume.

Looking at it by SNR (Table 4, KWT-1, seen noise; Fig. 3 is visualization), several key numbers are worth remembering. Under clean conditions: Data2Vec-clean 0.887 is the highest, Data2Vec-denoising 0.876 is slightly lower, and Baseline-MTR 0.800 is actually lower than Baseline-Clean's 0.832. Under extreme noise at −10 dB: Data2Vec-denoising 0.310 vs Baseline-MTR 0.236, a relative improvement of about 31% (calculated from Table 4); at 5 dB, the gap is still 0.769 vs 0.648. The cross-point analysis is the most informative: Data2Vec-clean (zero noise data throughout pretraining and fine-tuning) overtakes Baseline-MTR starting from 10 dB under seen noise (0.743 vs 0.720), 0.808 vs 0.760 at 15 dB, and 0.848 vs 0.783 at 20 dB; while under unseen noise (Table 5, Fig. 4), the cross-point advances to 5 dB (0.643 vs 0.640 basically持平, and拉开 from 10 dB as 0.757 vs 0.714). The paper's interpretation is: above medium SNR, the robustness of pretraining itself exceeds MTR, and this advantage does not depend on having seen noise during training; the advantage of MTR extending to lower SNRs under seen noise precisely shows that part of MTR's robustness is memory bound to specific noise types, and this memory fails under unseen noise, revealing SSL's generalization advantage earlier (Section 4). There is also a detail in Table 5 that the paper does not discuss in depth: at unseen −10 dB, Data2Vec-noisy (0.225) and Data2Vec-clean+noisy (0.222) are slightly higher than Data2Vec-denoising (0.219)—under extreme low SNR combined with completely unfamiliar noise, pure noise exposure actually has a slight advantage over denoising mapping, but from −5 dB onwards, denoising leads comprehensively (0.446 vs 0.434/0.432).

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

This paper does not have an independent chapter named "Ablation," but the matrix of six methods in Table 2 itself is a carefully designed ablation, from which five findings can be extracted.

First, pretraining itself brings robustness. Data2Vec-clean is more accurate than Baseline-Clean under all test conditions (including all noises and clean) (Table 3), and also outperforms Baseline-MTR at medium and high SNRs (Table 4, Table 5). Its mechanism can be understood from two perspectives: First, the pretraining objective of regressing hidden layer representations forces the model to learn the latent structure of speech (phonetic content, joint distribution of temporal context), which acts as a strong speech manifold prior; noise is outside the manifold, and under moderate degradation, the model can "fill in the blanks" relying on the prior; Second, pretraining provides a better starting point for the loss landscape for fine-tuning, allowing fine-tuning on 20% labeled data to converge to a solution with better generalization. The original text of the paper's conclusion is "pretraining itself has elevated the model's robustness at lower noise levels to a point where MTR cannot catch up" (Section 5).

Second, MTR during the fine-tuning stage is highly complementary to pretraining. Changing Data2Vec-clean from clean fine-tuning to MTR fine-tuning, the KWT-1 seen average accuracy jumps from 0.606 to 0.693 (Table 3), with particularly large gains in the low SNR segment (from 0.174 to 0.298 at −10 dB in Table 4). This shows that pretraining provides generalization robustness, while MTR provides noise adaptation under low SNR; they solve problems in different segments, and only their combination is complete—which is why the paper designs the best results as the combination of "pretraining + MTR fine-tuning."

Third, symmetric noise pretraining brings almost no extra gain beyond MTR fine-tuning. Data2Vec-noisy and Data2Vec-clean+noisy basically overlap under all sizes and conditions (KWT-1 seen 0.692 vs 0.693, unseen 0.676 vs 0.676, Table 3). The reason has been analyzed in the methodology section: when both teacher and student consume noise, the regression target itself is polluted by noise, and the student learns "representations of noisy speech" rather than "mapping from noise to clean," which is homogeneous with the noise exposure provided by MTR, and their marginal gains cancel each other out.

Fourth, the asymmetric denoising objective is the source of the gain. Data2Vec-denoising, under the premise of MTR fine-tuning, stably leads Data2Vec-noisy across three sizes (Table 3: 0.708 vs 0.692, 0.717 vs 0.699, 0.676 vs 0.655), and the cost is extremely small—0.876 vs 0.872 (clean value of Data2Vec-noisy) under clean conditions, only about 1 percentage point lower than the pure clean pretraining's 0.887 (Table 4). This is a beautiful trade-off of "exchanging negligible clean loss for significant noise gain."

Fifth, the gains are consistent across model sizes, and there is a sweet spot for size. The relative improvement of 16.22%–18.04% spans from 0.6M to 5.4M parameters (Section 4), indicating that the representation improvement learned by denoising pretraining is not exclusive to large models; while KWT-2 slightly outperforming KWT-3 reminds readers that under the setting of only 4.7 hours of labeled data, blindly increasing model size is not cost-effective.

## Main Contributions

First, to the best of the paper's knowledge, this is the first work to systematically study the impact of self-supervised pretraining on KWS noise robustness, filling the gap where "SSL robustness gains are verified in ASR but missing in KWS." Second, it provides a counter-intuitive and engineering-valuable conclusion: clean data pretraining (never touching noise during training) can beat supervised MTR trained with noisy data above 5 dB SNR, indicating that the speech representation prior learned by pretraining is a source of robustness that MTR cannot replace. Third, it proposes Data2Vec-denoising asymmetric pretraining—teacher consumes clean, student consumes noisy—embedding denoising supervision into the self-supervised objective at no cost, achieving optimal results under all noise conditions and model sizes, with only marginal loss under clean conditions. Fourth, it attributes the gain cleanly through the Data2Vec-noisy control: noise exposure (replaceable by MTR) and denoising objectives (irreplaceable) are clearly distinguished. Fifth, the evaluation protocol itself is reusable: 80/20 label scarcity setting, seen/unseen noise separation, six noises × seven SNRs totaling 42 test sets, cross-validation of three model sizes, and all code is open-sourced. Sixth, engineering feasibility data: pretraining of the largest model takes only 2 hours on a single A40 (Section 3), with a very low method threshold.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, the noise model is too idealized. All noise conditions are artificial additive mixing (additive mixing at specified SNRs), without degradation factors of real embedded scenarios such as reverberation, far-field pickup, microphone channel distortion, and automatic gain control; although CAF and BBL are "unseen noises," they still belong to the additive stationary/quasi-stationary category like training noises, and generalization to non-stationary burst noises (keyboard typing, TV channel switching) is not verified. Second, absolute performance is still unusable at extremely low SNRs: the optimal method achieves only 0.310–0.310 accuracy at −10 dB (Table 4) and 0.219 under unseen noise (Table 5), far from practical wake-up, indicating that pure backend representation learning cannot fully compensate for frontend SNR loss. Third, Data2Vec-denoising has an implicit data premise: the teacher branch must receive the clean version corresponding to the student's noisy input, which the paper constructs via "clean corpus + artificial noise addition." In real scenarios, if only noisy unlabeled recordings are available (the norm for far-field collection), this asymmetric pairing does not hold, and the method degrades to symmetric noise addition. Fourth, key hyperparameters are opaque: the number of top blocks K used for the target, the EMA smoothing coefficient τ, the number of pretraining epochs and learning rate, etc., are not reported in specific values in the paper (only stating that they follow [5][6] and are fine-tuned due to hardware), making reproduction strongly dependent on open-source code. Fifth, edge-side metrics are completely missing: the paper does not report any FLOPs, latency, or memory footprint, and while KWT is a transformer structure, streaming inference and context window issues (pretraining uses global self-attention + mean pooling, inherently offline-style) are not trivial for always-on listening devices; the feasibility of real-time performance for 0.6M–5.4M parameters is not discussed in the paper.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First, the task paradigm is distant from real KWS: GSC V2 is one-second fixed-length, isolated word classification, while product-level wake-word detection needs to handle continuous speech streams, where negative samples (non-wake-word speech and silence) account for the absolute majority, and false trigger constraints; only reporting 35-class accuracy without reporting any false alarm/miss trade-off metrics (not reported in the paper) makes it difficult to infer deployment performance. Second, there is an inconsistency in the training noise list between the text of Section 2.4 (BUS, PED, STR, SSN) and the title of Table 2 (BUS, BBL, PED, STR), which is suspected to be a typo but will affect reproduction. Third, no report on statistical robustness: the paper does not report the number of random seeds, variance, or significance tests, and some key gaps (e.g., 0.225 vs 0.219 at unseen −10 dB) cannot be judged as significant without repeated experiments. Fourth, only one dataset and one labeling ratio: the scaling benefits of pretraining were not tested on larger unlabeled corpora like LibriSpeech, nor was the labeling data ratio scanned (only 20% was tested), so it is unknown whether the conclusions hold for scenarios with richer or poorer labeled data. Fifth, no comparison with other supervised robustness methods (adversarial training, filterbank learning, cascaded frontend speech enhancement); the binary comparison of "pretraining vs MTR" is clean but incomplete, and the stacking benefits of orthogonal methods were not explored.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the paper's own framework, the most direct direction is to move the asymmetric denoising idea to real data: using speech enhancement models or differentiable noise generators to create pairings, breaking the restriction of "must have clean sources"; or drawing on Robust Data2Vec (Reference [8]) to combine contrastive loss with regression denoising objectives, to test whether the two types of supervision are complementary. Second, expand pretraining corpus and task diversity—pretrain on large-scale unlabeled speech (tens to thousands of hours) to verify whether the gains observed in this paper continue to amplify with data volume, and whether they can transfer to settings closer to products, such as streaming, custom wake words, and low-resource languages. Third, complete evaluation from a deployment perspective: replace pure accuracy with detection rate at fixed false alarm rate, ROC curves under different posterior thresholds, re-evaluate on real far-field recordings (including reverberation and channel effects), and report FLOPs and latency; if compatibility with edge devices is desired, it is also necessary to study the feasibility of migrating denoising pretraining to streaming-friendly architectures (convolutional frontends, limited context attention). Fourth, rebalance model size: since KWT-3 has passed its sweet spot under 4.7 hours of labeled data, joint calibration of labeled data volume vs optimal capacity and pretraining data volume vs optimal capacity is a subsequent topic with practical value. Fifth, method stacking experiments: whether there are stacking benefits from orthogonal combinations of pretraining + MTR + adversarial training + frontend enhancement, half of the comparison matrix in this paper is already built; completing the other half can answer this.
