- **Date**: June 2024 (arXiv:2406.15112v1, submitted 2024-06-21)

This is a five-page technical report. Its value does not lie in proposing new algorithms, but in implementing a public KWS benchmark entirely on a commercial micro-power neuromorphic audio chip and refreshing the benchmark's power consumption floor with physical measurement data. The correct way to read this paper is to treat it as an "engineering acceptance test" for always-on audio intelligence on the edge.

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the voice entry point for smart speakers, wireless earbuds, wearable devices, and battery-powered sensor nodes. Its first-principle constraint is not peak compute power, but always-on power consumption: wake-word listening must run continuously 24/7, and missing a wake word at any moment equals a failed wake-up. Therefore, KWS is a typical always-on workload, where power consumption directly translates to battery volume, charging cycles, and even the viability of the product form factor. Traditional approaches run a small neural network on an MCU, DSP, or smartphone big/little core, at the cost of continuous milliwatt-level power consumption; yet, milliwatt-level power is still too expensive for devices powered by coin cells or energy harvesting—a CR2032 coin cell has a nominal capacity of about 220 mAh, and if the listening circuit consumes 10 mW continuously, it will be depleted in weeks (calculated by this note based on common battery specifications, not data from the paper).

This is where neuromorphic computing enters. The paper's abstract states clearly: the design motivation of brain-inspired processors is to achieve extreme energy efficiency relative to von Neumann architectures and tensor processors. Their design language leverages four types of sparsity: weight sparsity, parameter sparsity, state sparsity, and activity sparsity. The extreme form is the Spiking Neural Network (SNN)—a network that transmits information via discrete spike events, mimicking the sparse activity of biological neural systems: computation only occurs when events arrive, and the cost during silent periods is approximately zero. For always-on listening workloads where "wake words are absent for the vast majority of the time," activity sparsity naturally aligns with the workload characteristics.

There is another more hidden pain point in the field: the energy efficiency claims of neuromorphic chips are difficult to verify fairly. The paper's introduction explicitly points out that few benchmarks are available for unified implementation across multiple neuromorphic and non-neuromorphic platforms, and energy efficiency figures often remain in isolated simulations or estimates. The Aloha benchmark (Blouw et al. 2019 [1]) is one of the few KWS tasks truly run on multiple platforms including GPU, CPU, Jetson, Movidius, Loihi, and SpiNNaker2, making it naturally suitable for cross-architecture energy consumption comparison. This paper's work is to move this benchmark entirely onto SynSense's Xylo Audio 2 (device model SYNS61210) neuromorphic audio chip, using measured data to answer "how much can an event-driven dedicated audio chip save for always-on KWS?"

The positioning of the Xylo Audio series (Introduction): An ultra-low-power audio inference chip family for in-microphone and near-microphone real-time analysis, built around a high-efficiency integer logic processor, specifically simulating parameter-sparse and activity-sparse LIF spiking networks; the chip features a low-power audio encoding interface that can connect directly to a microphone, sparsely encoding incoming audio before handing it to the inference core. This "in-sensor" positioning is the essential difference from all comparison platforms, a point that will be revisited repeatedly in the following sections.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

Using the measured data from the paper's Table II to "verify injuries" for existing solutions one by one:

- **The power consumption order of magnitude for general processors is completely wrong.** GPU idle 14970 mW, inference 37830 mW, dynamic 22860 mW, dynamic energy efficiency 29.67 mJ/Inf; CPU three power metrics are 17010/28480/11470 mW, 6.32 mJ/Inf; Jetson is 2640/4980/2340 mW, 5.58 mJ/Inf (Table II). These devices are born for throughput, treating single-channel continuous audio as a batch processing task, rendering event sparsity unfulfilled; their idle figures are for the entire machine or card, representing an overwhelming disadvantage for always-on comparison.
- **Embedded inference accelerators do not solve the always-on idle noise.** Movidius idle 210 mW, dynamic 437 mW, 1.5 mJ/Inf (Table II). Idle power is still in the hundreds of milliwatts, whereas always-on KWS is mostly silent, burning all power during idle.
- **First-generation neuromorphic chips lowered energy consumption by two orders of magnitude, but idle remains high.** Loihi in the original benchmark had dynamic 81 mW, 0.27 mJ/Inf; subsequent re-measurement (Reference [11]) showed dynamic 11 mW, 0.037 mJ/Inf, active energy 0.13 mJ/Inf; but both reports list idle as 29 mW (Table II). 29 mW always-on idle noise translates to about 254 Wh annually (calculated by this note based on 8760 hours), which battery-powered devices still cannot bear. SpiNNaker2's dynamic energy of 0.0071 mJ/Inf is already excellent, but its dynamic power is still 7.1 mW, and its active energy was not reported in the source literature [11] (another paper [6] reports its overall power as 390 mW), raising doubts about its system-level always-on cost.
- **Preprocessing energy consumption is systematically excluded from comparisons.** The paper's Discussion explicitly states: neither this paper nor all previous benchmark figures include audio preprocessing power consumption, and most Aloha implementations require computing MFCC spectrograms, which are "computationally demanding" (original text), and are usually completed on the host machine. This means cross-platform energy consumption comparisons systematically underestimate the true cost of general platforms, and also indicates that the MFCC frontend itself is one of the major energy consumers for always-on KWS, just no one includes it in the report card.
- **The task accuracy baseline is 93%** (Discussion). Any power-saving solution must at least hold this line, otherwise the energy efficiency advantage is meaningless.

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

To completely close the loop of the Aloha benchmark on a micro-power integer logic SNN chip designed for audio: microphone signals enter the chip directly, on-chip hardware completes acoustic event encoding, an 8-bit weight and 16-bit state quantized spiking network performs inference, and the firing or not of a single readout neuron provides the decision; requiring that the accuracy after quantized deployment remains above the 93% baseline; all power figures come from real chip continuous streaming inference rather than simulation estimates; the final goal is to refresh the four lower bounds of idle, active, and dynamic power and inference energy efficiency on the Aloha leaderboard. These four things are interlocked: encoding must be sparse (otherwise activity sparsity is moot), the network must be small (otherwise dynamic power cannot be suppressed), quantization must not lose accuracy (otherwise saving power is meaningless), and measurements must be real (otherwise they are incomparable).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire chain is "analog frontend → on-chip event encoding → feedforward SNN → single readout" (Fig 2, Fig 3): single-channel audio enters a low-noise amplifier (gain selectable in three steps: 0/6/12 dB), passes through a second-order Butterworth bandpass filter bank divided into 16 frequency bands (center frequencies covering 40–16940 Hz, Q value uniformly 4), each band's output is rectified and sent to a group of LIF neurons—Leaky Integrate-and-Fire neurons, colloquial anchor: like capacitor charging/discharging, input accumulates membrane potential, leakage drags it down, only fires a spike when exceeding the threshold—completing smoothing, quantization, and conversion into events. Ultimately, one audio channel becomes 16 sparse event channels, with the firing intensity of each channel proportional to the instantaneous energy of that band (Fig 2 caption), equivalent to a real-time updated sparse time-frequency representation, which the paper analogizes to a "real-time Fourier transform" (Fig 2e). The event stream then enters the SynNet network (see below), with a readout LIF neuron at the end responsible for the "aloha" class decision: firing on target samples, remaining silent on non-target samples (Network Architecture section).

The design motivations at three levels are worth breaking down:

1. **Why use "filter bank + rectification + LIF" instead of MFCC.** MFCC requires FFT, logarithmic operations, and DCT, which are one of the main computational hotspots on MCUs; whereas the hardware implementation of second-order bandpass filters (biquad form) is extremely cheap, rectification is a point-wise nonlinearity, and LIF smoothing exactly converts continuous energy envelopes into event rate encoding. All three steps are hardware-friendly operations. More importantly, this frontend on Xylo Audio 2 is chip hardware rather than software (Fig 2 shows the design of the on-chip audio encoding module), processing input audio continuously in a streaming-mode buffer-free manner (Audio Preprocessing section); measured power consumption is below 50 µW (Discussion). The frontend is included in the same chip's measurement scope as the inference core for the first time.
2. **Why the inference core is made into an integer logic LIF array.** The inference core of Xylo Audio 2 is a synchronous digital CMOS integer logic processor, specifically simulating parameter and activity-sparse LIF networks: neuron and synapse states quantized to 16 bits, weights quantized to 8 bits (Introduction). Integer operations save the area and leakage overhead of floating-point units, and activity sparsity means no computation without events, leaving only clock and leakage during silent periods. The main clock is only 6.25 MHz during power measurement (Power and Inference Rate section), about three orders of magnitude lower than general SoCs—this is the first source of micro-power.
3. **Why positioned as "real-time streaming" rather than "accelerator".** The Introduction emphasizes that Xylo is tailored for real-time streaming operations, contrasting with the acceleration time mode of inference accelerators; the Discussion reiterates that Xylo is for continuous (non-batch) real-world signals. This positioning difference will appear repeatedly in the comparison section: it explains why Xylo has the lowest inference rate but the highest energy efficiency.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**LIF Neurons and Quantization Settings.** The paper does not write out explicit LIF dynamic equations (not reported in the paper), but provides key settings: on-chip LIF is an integer device, neuron and synapse states 16 bits, weights 8 bits (Introduction); synapse time constants take values on an exponential grid according to $\tau_n = 2^n \times 10$ ms, with the membrane time constant of all neurons and the readout neuron time constant fixed at $\tau_m = 20$ ms (Network Architecture section). The meaning of the exponential grid is that each step up doubles the time window—20/40/80/160 ms and so on—using very few hyperparameters to cover the entire time scale from millisecond-level transients to second-level envelopes. This is the mathematical foundation for the "time constant pyramid" in the following text.

**PeakLoss Training Objective.** During training, the membrane potential time series of the readout neuron is taken as the network output $x$, with target $y=1$ for target samples and $y=0$ for non-target (Training section). The loss function is:

$$
\text{PeakLoss}(x, y) = \text{MSE}\left( \frac{1}{M} \cdot \sum_{k=m}^{m+M} x_k, g \right), \text{ when } y = 1; = w_l \cdot \text{MSE}(x, 0), \text{ when } y = 0
$$

where $m = \arg\max x$ is the peak position of the membrane potential, $M$ is the observation window length after the peak, $g$ is the target value the peak neighborhood should approach, and $w_l$ is the non-target loss weight. The network in this paper takes $M = 140$ ms, $g = 1.5$, $w_l = 1.4$ (Training section).

Translated into plain language: for target samples, it does not require the entire 3s trajectory to be high, only that "it first rushes to a peak, and the average membrane potential within 140 ms after the peak reaches 1.5"; for non-target samples, it requires the entire trajectory to stick to zero throughout, and this term's weight is amplified by 1.4 times. Why designed this way: first, the appearance position of the wake word within the 3s sample is uncertain, constraining only the window after the peak makes the loss naturally insensitive to the keyword position; second, the deployment criterion is "whether the readout neuron fires an event" (see below), the longer the membrane potential stays above the threshold, the easier it is to fire, and a "high platform after the peak" is the most direct precursor to firing, so the loss directly optimizes the decision quantity at deployment; third, the ratio of target to non-target samples in the training set is 3:1 (Dataset section), non-targets are the minority class, and the 1.4 weighting is compensating for undersampling of the non-target class and suppressing false wake-ups—this motivation is not explained in the paper, and is an explanation given by this note based on the data ratio.

**Deployment Decision and ROC.** If the readout neuron produces one or more events during the sample period, it is judged as a target (Model Performance section); scanning the readout neuron's threshold allows drawing the ROC curve (Fig 4). This criterion reduces the classification problem to a detection problem, aligning naturally with the event output of SNNs, saving softmax and per-frame post-processing, and the decision itself is something that happens on the chip.

**Sample and Temporal Processing.** Training samples are cropped or padded with silence to a uniform 3s (Dataset section; dashed line in Fig 1 is the 3s line); encoded events are binned in time in 100 ms chunks (Audio Preprocessing section original text); trained for 300 epochs, using PyTorch Lightning to manage the training process, network definition and simulation based on the torch backend of the open-source Rockpool toolchain (Training section).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: On-Chip Sparse Acoustic Event Encoding Frontend

The value of this innovation must be viewed in the context of the historical baggage of the Aloha benchmark. Previously, all Aloha implementations on other platforms calculated MFCC on the host machine and fed it to the device, with preprocessing energy consumption not included in the score (Discussion), making comparisons systematically optimistic. Xylo Audio 2 puts the encoding frontend into the chip: 16 channels, center frequencies 40–16940 Hz, Q=4 second-order Butterworth bandpass group, plus rectification, plus LIF smoothing quantization (Fig 2). The "whys" of several specific choices: fixed Q=4 means high-frequency channels have large absolute bandwidth and low-frequency channels have small absolute bandwidth, a cheap approximation of logarithmic frequency resolution, consistent with human ear critical bands in direction; second-order low-order filtering is the choice with the smallest hardware area, and since only the energy envelope is looked at later without waveform reconstruction, high-order steep cutoff is not needed; gain selectable in three steps: 0/6/12 dB, adapting to different microphone sensitivities and sound pressure level scenarios; buffer-free streaming design minimizes frontend latency and does not require frame cache SRAM. Measured frontend power consumption is below 50 µW (Discussion), about one-sixth of the deployed model's dynamic inference power of 291 µW (calculated by this note), making "compressing the entire audio link within half a milliwatt, except for the microphone itself" the first verifiable system statement.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Time Constant Pyramid SynNet

The network backbone is called SynNet (cited from the author's 2022 prior work [2]), a fully connected feedforward chain of "linear weight matrices and LIF layers interleaved" (Fig 3). The paper describes the architecture with two lists: H is the list of hidden layer widths, $\tau$ is the list of time constant steps for each layer. For example, $H = [160, 60, 60, 60, 60, 60]$, $\tau = [2, 2, 4, 4, 8, 8]$ indicates six hidden layers, the first layer has 160 neurons, the remaining five layers have 60 each; the first and second layers each have 2 steps of synapse time constants (half the neurons 20 ms, half 40 ms), the third and fourth layers have 4 steps (each quarter of neurons assigned to 20/40/80 ms and the next step), the fifth and sixth layers have 8 steps, extending exponentially according to $\tau_n = 2^n \times 10$ ms (Network Architecture section).

This "pyramid" arrangement (Fig 3 caption original text: shallow layers short time constants, deep layers long time constants) is the soul of this architecture: shallow layers use short time constants to retain fast transients—burst onset, frication noise, these millisecond-level clues; deep layers use long time constants to integrate evidence to hundreds of milliseconds or even seconds, matching the time span of a complete wake word. Multiple $\tau$ uniformly distributed within each layer is equivalent to paralleling a group of exponential smoothing filters with different time windows in the same layer, allowing the next layer to pick fast or slow evidence itself—this is the lowest cost way to obtain multi-scale temporal receptive fields without introducing convolutions or recurrence. Why insist on pure feedforward: when deployed to an integer logic chip, the data dependency of the feedforward chain is deterministic, with no state feedback, mapping and scheduling are simplest, and it avoids the extra overhead and deployment complexity of training recurrent networks via Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Training-Quantization-Deployment Closed Loop and Behavioral Migration After Quantization

The entire process is strung together using the open-source Rockpool toolchain: torch backend for definition and training, quantization (8-bit weights, 16-bit states), deployed to Xylo devices via Rockpool deployment pipeline (Power and Inference Rate section). There is only one data point for quantization comparison, but it carries significant information: the 461-neuron model has floating-point accuracy 96.88%, TPR 97.92%, FPR 4.17%, and after quantized deployment 95.31%, 91.67%, 1.04% (Table I row with †). The overall drop is only 1.57 percentage points (paper Discussion wording "less than 2%"), but structurally an interesting migration occurs: TPR drops by 6.25 percentage points while FPR drops by 3.13 percentage points—quantization noise makes the readout neuron globally more conservative, harder to cross the firing threshold. For wake-word deployment, this is usually a worthwhile trade-off: the user cost of false wake-ups (speaker lights up at midnight) is higher than occasional missed wake-ups (user says it again). The paper does not report quantization comparisons for other model sizes, limiting our judgment on "how quantization robustness changes with model size."

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Compared to Blouw et al. [1] Loihi original benchmark**: Both are SNN routes, the original calculates MFCC on the host machine and Loihi only does inference; this paper moves the frontend into the chip and measures its power consumption, advancing the "algorithm benchmark" to a "system benchmark."
- **Compared to Loihi/SpiNNaker2 neuromorphic general-purpose chips**: These are programmable many-core (Loihi is a spiking core array, SpiNNaker2 is an ARM-like small core array), with design points of large data volume, high inference rate, and heavy parallelism (Discussion original text); Xylo Audio 2 is an audio-dedicated integer SNN core, with design points of low-power real-time processing of single-channel continuous signals. In Table II, Xylo's inference rate of 40–102 Inf/s is the lowest in the field, and dynamic energy efficiency of 6.6 µJ/Inf is the highest—these two numbers are two sides of the same coin, not a contradiction.
- **Compared to CPU/GPU/Jetson/Movidius traditional platforms**: Dense batch processing vs. streaming sparsity, gigahertz clock vs. 6.25 MHz main clock, milliwatts to tens of watts idle vs. 216 µW idle. The difference is not "computes faster," but "burns less."
- **Compared to the author's own prior work [2]**: The prior work already demonstrated sub-mW SNN audio applications with Rockpool plus Xylo; this paper completes the implementation on a publicly available standard benchmark for cross-comparison along the same toolchain, and pushes the numbers to a new low of 291 µW dynamic power.

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

The Aloha dataset was introduced by Blouw et al. [1]: the training set has about 2000 utterances from 96 speakers, with a target phrase "aloha" to non-target phrase ratio of 3:1 (Benchmark Dataset section); this paper uses its provided 192 test samples. Fig 1 gives the duration distribution of training and test set samples, uniformly cropped or padded with silence to 3s (dashed line in the figure), this length retains most samples in both sets (Fig 1 caption). A fact not explicitly stated in the paper but inferable from Table I: all values of TPR and FPR are integer multiples of 1/96 (91.67% is 88/96, 97.92% is 94/96, 1.04% is 1/96, 100.0% is 96/96), indicating the test set is a balanced set of 96 target and 96 non-target samples (inferred by this note, exact composition not reported in the paper). Details such as the specific content of non-target phrases, whether test speakers overlap with the training set, and sampling rate are not reported in the paper, and need to be verified in the original benchmark literature [1].

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Task Metrics**: Acc, TPR = TP/(TP+FN), FPR = FP/(FP+TN) (Table I table note), decision rule is whether the readout neuron fired any events during the sample period. Rationale: in KWS engineering, TPR corresponds to wake-up sensitivity, FPR corresponds to false wake-up rate, these are working point coordinates that must be reported separately; a single accuracy on a balanced test set would mask this trade-off. The ROC in Fig 4 (scanning readout threshold) further provides a complete family of comparable working points between models.
- **Power Metrics**: idle (measured for 5 seconds with no input after deploying the model), active (streaming encoded audio of the entire test set to the device), dynamic = active − idle (Power and Inference Rate section); measurement uses development board current monitoring, sampling rate 1280 Hz, main clock 6.25 MHz. Why split into three: the true bill for always-on devices equals all-day idle plus dynamic during voice periods, reporting only dynamic would underestimate always-on costs, which is also the reason the paper additionally advocates for the active energy metric.
- **Energy Efficiency Metrics**: Energy per inference (µJ/Inf), 1 inference is defined as the network processing 10 time steps, following Blouw et al.'s wording to maintain comparability with historical figures (Power and Inference Rate section). The paper reports both dynamic energy (Aloha standard metric) and active energy (including idle amortization, which the authors believe is closer to system reality).
- **Measurement Principle**: All power figures for devices in Table II are physically measured on physical devices (table note original text "Power measured on physical devices in all cases"), simulation or datasheet estimates are not accepted.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table II complete comparison (idle/active/dynamic power, unit mW; dynamic energy/active energy, unit mJ/Inf): GPU 14970/37830/22860, 29.67, 49.1; CPU 17010/28480/11470, 6.32, 15.7; Jetson 2640/4980/2340, 5.58, 11.9; Movidius 210/647/437, 1.5, 2.2; Loihi 29/110/81, 0.27, 0.37; Loihi re-measurement [11] 29/40/11, 0.037, 0.13; SpiNNaker2 [11] —/—/7.1, 0.0071, — (active energy source literature not reported, another paper [6] reports its overall 390 mW); Xylo Audio 2 (this paper) 0.216/0.507/0.291, 0.0066, 0.011.

Converted to multiples (using Xylo dynamic energy 6.6 µJ/Inf as baseline, calculated by this note): about 7% lower than SpiNNaker2's 7.1 µJ; about 5.6 times lower than Loihi re-measurement's 37 µJ; about 41 times lower than Loihi original's 270 µJ; about 227 times lower than Movidius's 1500 µJ; about 845 times lower than Jetson's 5580 µJ; about 958 times lower than CPU's 6320 µJ; about 4495 times lower than GPU's 29670 µJ. The second-best in active energy metric is Loihi re-measurement's 130 µJ, Xylo's 11 µJ is lower than it by about an order of magnitude (paper original text "by an order of magnitude"). Idle power of 216 µW is about 134 times lower than Loihi's two reports of 29 mW—translating to annual always-on energy consumption of about 1.9 Wh vs 254 Wh (calculated by this note based on 8760 hours), this is the most convincing gap from an always-on perspective. Fig 6 draws a logarithmic bar comparison of the two energy metrics with Xylo as the 1.0× baseline.

The overall range given in the Power and Inference Rate section: all trained models idle 216–217 µW, active 468–514 µW, dynamic 251–298 µW; inference rate between 40–102 Inf/s depending on model size; dynamic energy 2.4–7.3 µJ/Inf, active energy 4.6–12.7 µJ/Inf. Infer from Table II, the deployed 461-neuron model ($H = [160, 60, 60, 60, 60, 60]$) has an inference rate of approximately 291 µW ÷ 6.6 µJ ≈ 44 Inf/s (calculated by this note, specific inference rate for this model not reported in the paper). Also note the Discussion original text: Xylo comprehensively defeats all comparison devices in idle, active, dynamic power, and inference energy efficiency.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have a formal ablation experiment section, the closest is the model size scan in Table I: seven architectures, total neuron count $N_{tot}$ from 221 to 461, time constants uniformly $\tau = [2, 2, 4, 4, 8, 8]$ (Table I table note), plus one row for quantized deployment comparison. Four pieces of information can be read out:

1. **Accuracy and scale are basically decoupled.** The smallest 221-neuron model ($H = [120, 20, 20, 20, 20, 20]$) achieved 97.40% accuracy, 1.04% FPR (lowest false positive rate in the field); the largest 461 model only had 96.88%; the best was actually the intermediate 341-neuron model ($H = [140, 40, 40, 40, 40, 40]$): 98.44% accuracy, TPR 100.0%, FPR 3.12%. Why: Aloha is a single-keyword small task, the test set has only 192 samples, the capacity of 221 neurons is already excessive, continuing to widen does not buy accuracy.
2. **Non-monotonic jitter falls within the noise magnitude.** The 401 model's 97.40% is actually lower than the 411 model's 97.92%, and the 461 model drops back to 96.88%; the test set granularity is only 1/192 ≈ 0.52 percentage points, and each architecture was trained only once, with no variance reported in the paper, so the ordering between sizes should not be over-interpreted.
3. **The real leverage is power and inference rate.** Inference rate drops from 102 Inf/s to 40 Inf/s with size, dynamic energy rises from 2.4 to 7.3 µJ/Inf (Power and Inference Rate section)—under fully connected architecture, the amount of computation per step is approximately proportional to the number of neurons, energy rises approximately linearly with size while accuracy remains unchanged. The selection logic is therefore very clear: take the smallest model at the working point that satisfies accuracy; the actual role of the size scan is to calibrate the "power-accuracy" Pareto frontier rather than the "accuracy-capacity" curve.
4. **Quantization was only compared once.** 461 model float vs quantized: 96.88%/97.92%/4.17% vs 95.31%/91.67%/1.04% (Table I), overall drop 1.57 percentage points, TPR drops 6.25, FPR drops 3.13, readout behavior globally shifts to conservative (reason analysis in Methodology Section 3). The paper does not report quantization comparisons for the other six sizes, nor does it ablate the $\tau$ pyramid arrangement, loss hyperparameters ($M = 140$ ms, $g = 1.5$, $w_l = 1.4$), filter bank channels (16), or main clock frequency—all of these are blanks not reported in the paper.

## Main Contributions

1. **Refreshed the power and energy efficiency floor of the Aloha benchmark**: idle 216 µW, active 507 µW, dynamic 291 µW, dynamic energy 6.6 µJ/Inf, active energy 11 µJ/Inf, all best-in-class, and all are physical measurements (Table II, Fig 6, Abstract). The abstract states that Xylo set a new lowest power record for this benchmark.
2. **Included preprocessing in the measurement scope**: On-chip event encoding frontend measured below 50 µW (Discussion), making "compressing the entire audio link within half a milliwatt, except for the microphone itself" the first verifiable statement, while exposing the systematic unfairness of previous benchmarks excluding preprocessing energy consumption.
3. **Quantized deployment held the accuracy line**: 8-bit weights, 16-bit states, maximum 461-neuron network deployment accuracy 95.31%, exceeding the 93% benchmark standard (Table I, Discussion), quantization loss less than 2 percentage points.
4. **Established the orthogonal value of the "streaming in-sensor" design point**: The paper uses data to show that low inference rate does not equal low value—dedicated design for continuous single-stream signals opens up an energy efficiency dimension beyond the metrics dominated by high-throughput accelerators (Discussion), providing architectural selection basis for always-on audio intelligence.
5. **Reproducible engineering closed loop**: The entire process of training, quantization, deployment, and measurement is based on the open-source Rockpool toolchain and commercial development board (Fig 5), allowing third parties to directly follow up and verify.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

1. **Single keyword, single readout**: Each network has only one readout neuron, one target class (Network Architecture section). Extending to a multi-keyword vocabulary requires multiple readouts plus competition mechanisms, how neuron budget and power consumption change with vocabulary size is not discussed in the paper.
2. **Context upper limit of pure feedforward**: Long-term evidence integration is completely compressed into the long-time constant synapses of the deepest layer ($\tau_n = 2^n \times 10$ ms grid), network depth is limited by power budget, and there is a hard upper limit on integrable context; tasks relying on second-level or longer context (continuous commands, conversational interaction) are not suitable for this architecture.
3. **Coarse frontend frequency resolution**: 16 channels, fixed Q=4 (Audio Preprocessing section), coarser than the conventional 20–40 channel mel filter bank, fine formant structure may not be fully retained; the paper did not conduct any tests with added noise, far-field, reverberation, or cross-microphone conditions, robustness is unknown.
4. **Real-time margin is questionable**: The benchmark runs in acceleration time mode (>4× real-time acceleration, Discussion), real deployment is strict real-time single stream. If the 40–102 Inf/s inference rate is occupied by deeper networks or multi-class tasks, the margin will be eaten up; power figures under strict real-time streaming mode are not reported in the paper.
5. **Idle is still the always-on base noise**: 216 µW idle translates to about 1.9 Wh annually (calculated by this note), although about two orders of magnitude lower than competitors, it is still the main overhead for energy harvesting applications; clock gating, event-driven wake-up are clear next steps.
6. **Microphone and analog link not counted**: The paper measured the encoding block (<50 µW), but analog microphone body power consumption, ADC, and power conversion efficiency are not reported (not reported in the paper), system-level figures are still optimistic.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

1. **Test set is too small**: 192 samples, Acc granularity 0.52 percentage points, TPR/FPR granularity 1.04 percentage points (1/96), most differences between sizes in Table I fall within the noise interval; no multi-seed repeated training, no variance statistics reported in the paper.
2. **No robustness dimensions**: No stratified results for noise, far-field, multi-speaker, or accents, whereas the true failure modes of always-on KWS occur exactly under these conditions.
3. **No hyperparameter ablation**: $\tau$ pyramid arrangement, three loss hyperparameters M/g/w_l, 16-channel frontend, all single-point configurations output directly, unable to distinguish "architecture essence" from "luck of this set of hyperparameters."
4. **Cross-platform comparison conditions are not unified**: Table II figures come from measurements in different literature, clocks, batches, and whether preprocessing is included vary; SpiNNaker2 active energy is missing (if included according to another paper [6]'s 390 mW, its active energy will deteriorate significantly); "1 inference = 10 time steps" is a convention inherited from Blouw, the absolute values of energy/inference depend on this convention, cross-metric comparison requires caution.
5. **Time step metric is ambiguous**: The paper mentions events are binned in 100 ms chunks in one place (Audio Preprocessing section), and follows the metric of 10 time steps per inference in another, the conversion details between 40 Inf/s and ">4× real-time" are not expanded; the strict mapping between time steps and audio duration is not reported in the paper, affecting the precise reproduction of energy efficiency figures.
6. **Development board relies on host machine**: The HDK is USB-powered, requiring a PC to provide power and interface (Fig 5 caption), a system-level demonstration of independent battery-powered systems (including power tree efficiency) is not reported in the paper.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

1. **Multi-keyword and readout layer design**: Multiple readout neurons plus winner-take-all mechanism, calibrating the "neuron count-power-vocabulary size" scaling curve, answering the capacity boundary of this architecture.
2. **Strict real-time and duty cycle optimization**: Power curves under streaming real-time mode, event-driven wake-up and clock gating to further suppress idle, providing annual energy consumption for real always-on working points; completing the "frequency-inference rate-power" scaling curve under the 6.25 MHz main clock to support selection.
3. **Robustness benchmark and equal-accuracy comparison**: TPR-FPR working points under added noise, far-field, multi-speaker conditions, and comparing energy efficiency with MFCC frontend ANN under equal accuracy, answering "how much robustness does event encoding actually sacrifice."
4. **End-to-end metric benchmark transformation**: Pushing Aloha-like benchmarks to mandatory inclusion of preprocessing energy consumption; Xylo's <50 µW on-chip frontend would amplify its advantage under this metric.
5. **Migration to adjacent audio tasks**: VAD, acoustic event detection, industrial acoustic monitoring—the author's prior work [2] has already verified the sub-mW audio application route with Rockpool plus Xylo, the same toolchain can be directly reused.
6. **Lower bit-width and finer quantization research**: Current 8-bit weights still have compression space; quantization comparison was only done for one model, a systematic "quantization-accuracy-power" triangle deserves to be supplemented.
