## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS, detecting predefined wake words in continuous audio streams) is a core voice entry point for devices such as smart speakers, mobile phones, and televisions. Traditional fixed-vocabulary KWS can only use factory-predefined wake words; if a user wants to switch to a preferred word, they must collect new data and retrain the model, which is extremely costly. Flexible KWS has therefore emerged, allowing users to freely register any custom keyword. Registration methods fall into two categories: audio registration (the user speaks the target word a few times, and the system learns it as a query example) and text registration (the user simply types the text of the target word). Text registration requires no repeated speaking, offering the lowest barrier to entry. Consequently, the demand for Text-Enrolled Flexible KWS (TF-KWS) continues to grow, which is the track this paper addresses.

The typical system architecture for TF-KWS is a dual-tower structure: a text encoder maps keyword text to text embeddings (TE) during the registration phase, and an acoustic encoder maps test speech to acoustic embeddings (AE) during the testing phase. The two towers are jointly optimized using Deep Metric Learning (DML, a training objective that pulls embeddings of similar samples closer and pushes dissimilar samples apart, such as contrastive loss, triplet loss, or proxy loss). In this framework, TE is trained to be the "representative vector" for the keyword—the AE of the same keyword is pulled closer to TE, while AEs of different keywords are pushed away. Detection scoring is based on the similarity between AE and TE.

The pain point lies in the fact that flexible models must allocate their capacity to "general discriminative ability" to cover infinitely many possible keywords, meaning their single-keyword performance cannot catch up to keyword-specific models trained with massive data per keyword (the paper's introduction cites reference [13] pointing out this gap). For real users, however, the experience depends precisely on the accuracy of their single registered word—it is meaningless if the model supports ten thousand words but suffers from frequent false alarms or missed detections for the user's specific word. It is impractical to collect large-scale speech data for every custom keyword, creating a natural contradiction in custom keyword scenarios: the words that need data the most are precisely those for which data cannot be obtained. Thus, this problem naturally transforms into a few-shot learning problem: Can we adapt a pre-trained TF-KWS model into a high-performance keyword-specific model using only a very small number (5 examples in this paper) of target keyword speech samples?

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper outlines gaps in existing solutions from three levels, each supported by experimental or structural evidence:

- **Full-parameter fine-tuning fails in few-shot settings.** Intuitively, "fine-tuning with 15 speech samples" should help, but the paper's ablation study (Table II) provides counter-intuitive data: 15-shot full-parameter fine-tuning (FT 15-shot, 2.21M tunable parameters) achieved an average AP of only 67.04%, which is lower than the pre-trained model (PT) without any fine-tuning (72.02%). The reason is that 15 samples are far insufficient to constrain 2.21 million parameters; the fine-tuning process destroys the general representations learned during pre-training (few-shot overfitting combined with catastrophic forgetting). These numbers form the foundational argument of the paper: the core contradiction of few-shot adaptation is not "whether to modify the model," but "how much and where to modify."
- **Training only a new classification head is also ineffective.** Freezing the backbone and fine-tuning only an additional classifier (FT clf, 0.5K parameters) yielded an AP of only 50.14% (Table II). Learning classifier weights from scratch with 15 samples is insufficient to even learn a 512-dimensional classification boundary; in contrast, the pre-trained model directly using TE as classifier weights achieves 72.02%. This indicates that the TE learned via DML during pre-training is already a high-quality keyword prototype vector, and discarding it to learn from scratch is a huge waste.
- **Existing few-shot KWS research does not cover the text registration route.** Few-shot transfer learning in references [14], [16]-[19] targets audio-registered flexible KWS (most approaches add a target-keyword-specific classification layer after the pre-trained acoustic encoder, training it with frozen or unfrozen weights), with [14] combining self-supervised learning with meta-learning. The paper explicitly claims: to the best of its knowledge, this is the first work to apply few-shot transfer learning to TF-KWS. This "first" is valid and valuable because TF-KWS holds a card that audio registration does not—the TE produced by the text encoder can be injected as a conditional signal into the adaptation process, rather than being used solely as a scoring prototype.
- **Existing text-conditioned schemes are parameter-heavy.** AdaKWS (reference [22]) uses a text encoder to generate parameters for Adaptive Instance Normalization (AdaIN, a normalization method that predicts per-channel scaling and shifting parameters using a conditional vector) and inserts them into a so-called Keyword Adaptive Module (KAM) for conditioning. However, it requires large amounts of data to jointly train two encoders from scratch and is not designed for few-shot scenarios; moreover, the parameter overhead of AdaIN-style conditioning is $d \times 2 \times f$ ($d$ is the conditional vector dimension, $f$ is the number of feature channels), which is too many tunable parameters for a 5-sample scenario. The comparative data in Table IV is harsh: AdaKWS achieved an AP of only 61.67% at 15-shot, which is even lower than the 15-shot binary/trinary classification baselines (72.38%/73.98%), proving that "directly applying large-capacity conditioned modules to few-shot fine-tuning" does not work.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved

Converging the above contradictions into an engineering problem: How to design a few-shot adaptation scheme for TF-KWS that simultaneously satisfies four constraints—(1) The injected target keyword information must come from the text side (TE), because text is the only prior in the TF-KWS registration phase and requires no additional annotation; (2) Tunable parameters must be minimized to avoid overfitting under 15 samples (full-parameter fine-tuning has already proven to fail); (3) Modular and rollback-capable: after adapting to one keyword, it can seamlessly revert to the pre-trained model to quickly switch to the next keyword (this is a rigid requirement for product deployment: one device needs to serve adaptation requests for multiple keywords); (4) It must stably outperform the unadapted pre-trained model even under extreme conditions like 5-shot. The paper's answer is the TA-adapter (Text-Aware Adapter), with three components handling "what information to inject" (TCFM), "which weights to adjust" (FW-adapter), and "how to score" (TE classifier).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The TA-adapter is not a new network designed from scratch, but an adaptation layer added to a pre-trained TF-KWS model. The pre-trained part follows the complete configuration of the authors' previous work (reference [11], Relation Proxy Loss, RPL): the acoustic encoder and text encoder are jointly pre-trained using RPL. RPL promotes tight alignment between the two by leveraging the structural relationships within the AE group and the TE group. This step is crucial as it guarantees the quality of TE as a keyword prototype vector, forming the trust basis for the subsequent design of "fixing TE as classifier weights."

The acoustic encoder adopts the ECAPA-TDNN architecture (reference [28], a widely used embedding extractor in the speaker recognition domain). It uses 1D convolution as its skeleton and contains three SE-Res2Blocks (residual blocks combining multi-scale branches of Res2Net with the SE module): the Res2Net part extracts multi-scale features from different levels, and the SE module (Squeeze-and-Excitation, a lightweight structure that learns per-channel weights based on feature importance) performs channel-level feature recalibration. The outputs of the three blocks are concatenated along the channel dimension, fused through a dense layer, and fed into attentive statistics pooling (which performs weighted statistics on variable-length features to output a fixed-length embedding), yielding the final AE. The paper divides this network into seven groups, G0 to G6, in Figure 1: G0 is a 1D convolution with kernel 5 and dilation 1 plus BN; G1/G2/G3 are three SE-Res2Blocks with dilation rates 2/3/4; G4 is a 1D convolution with kernel 1 and dilation 1 plus BN; G5 is attentive statistics pooling; G6 is a fully connected layer plus BN, outputting $p(k|x)$ after sigmoid. This group numbering is the coordinate system for understanding all ablation experiments.

The ideological sources of the adaptation strategy are three lines, each corresponding to the design motivation of a component. The first line is adapter methods from the vision and NLP domains (references [20][21]): inserting small modules into intermediate layers of pre-trained large models and freezing other parameters for fine-tuning, using very few additional parameters to prevent overfitting. The authors acknowledge that the TF-KWS acoustic encoder is not as large as SSL models, but believe that the parameter efficiency and modular design of adapters exactly hit two rigid needs of few-shot KWS. The second line comes from low-resource domain adaptation research in speaker recognition (references [25][26]): SE blocks and BN layers have been proven to be lightweight and effective domain adaptation structures. Their implicit assumption is that "essential speaker features have already been learned by the pre-trained model using big data, and domain adaptation only needs to adjust how low-level features are aggregated into high-level features." The authors migrate this idea from "cross-domain" to "cross-keyword": essential keyword discriminative features are already learned by pre-training, so few-shot adaptation only needs to adjust the weights of feature aggregation—this directly gave rise to the FW-adapter (adjusting only BN and SE). The third line is Learnable Activation Function conditioning (LAF, reference [24]), originally used for personalized speech enhancement and speaker-dependent recognition conditional injection. The authors transform it into a text conditioning mechanism much lighter than AdaIN, namely TCFM.

The division of labor logic for the three components can be understood as follows: TCFM is responsible for "telling the network what the target word is" (content conditional injection, changing the shape of the activation function); FW-adapter is responsible for "recalibrating feature aggregation" (not changing content, only changing the weight distribution of per-channel features); the TE classifier is responsible for "pinning the scoring geometry to the pre-trained metric space" (classifier weights are not learned, TE is used directly). The paper's experiments prove that the first two are synergistic and complementary, and neither is dispensable (Table III: using only TCFM 74.12%, using only FW-adapter 84.33%, using both 87.22%).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Learnable Activation Function (LAF) and TCFM**. TCFM replaces the original ReLU in ECAPA-TDNN with LAF. Given an ordered set of base activation functions $\{A_1, ..., A_a\}$, LAF calculates a weighted combination of these base functions conditioned on TE (Equation 1 in the paper):

$s = \text{softmax}(\text{TE} \cdot w + b), y = \text{LAF}(h|\text{TE}) = \sum_{i=1}^{a} s_i \cdot A_i(h)$

where $\text{TE} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}$ is the text embedding of the keyword, $w \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times a}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times a}$ are the trainable parameters unique to this LAF instance, $s \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times a}$ is the activation weight vector, $h$ is the input feature, and $y$ is the activated feature. Each LAF instance has its own independent $w$ and $b$, estimating $s$ using TE, thereby transforming input features into activated features. The paper sets $d=512$, $f=256$ (number of feature channels), and $a=6$. The six base activation functions were selected from the function set in reference [24] based on validation set performance: ELU, hard sigmoid, ReLU, softplus, swish, and tanh.

The key comparison here is parameter count: AdaIN-style conditioning requires $d \times 2 \times f$ parameters (predicting two vectors, scaling $\gamma$ and shifting $\beta$, for each channel), while TCFM requires only $d \times a$ parameters. Substituting the paper's dimension settings ($d=512$, $f=256$, $a=6$), each conditional injection point requires $512 \times 2 \times 256 = 262,144$ parameters for AdaIN, whereas TCFM requires only $512 \times 6 = 3,072$ parameters, a difference of about 85 times (this multiplier is derived from the formulas and dimensions given in the paper; the main text only qualitatively describes it as "significantly fewer"). A parameter difference of two orders of magnitude is the structural reason why TCFM can survive at 5-shot while AdaKWS cannot.

**TE Classifier**. The scoring path after adaptation (Equation 2 in the paper): AE passes through a fully connected layer with final weights $\theta_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 1}$ to produce logits for the target keyword $k$, then passes through sigmoid to get $p(k|x)$. The approach of TA-adapter is not to learn $\theta_k$ from scratch, but to directly fix the frozen TE as this weight vector. The rationale is: TE has already been trained by DML during pre-training to be the representative vector for the corresponding keyword, so the inner product of tunable AE and fixed TE is naturally a reasonable logit; furthermore, since both AE and TE are L2-normalized, the inner product is equivalent to cosine similarity $S(\cdot, \cdot)$, thus $p(k|x) = \sigma(\theta_k \cdot \text{AE}) = \sigma(S(\text{TE}, \text{AE}))$—the final scoring method is exactly the same as TF-KWS, and the loss used for fine-tuning the acoustic encoder is binary cross-entropy. The ingenuity of this design lies in freezing the "classifier learning" step, which is most easily misled by 15 samples (the 50.14% of FT clf is a cautionary tale), into a verified high-quality prototype, leaving all degrees of freedom for fine-tuning to pull AE towards TE.

**Visualization Normalization of LAF**. To observe whether conditioning actually occurs, the paper defines Equation 3 to plot the trained LAF output minus the average of the base activations: $y = \text{LAF}(h|\text{TE}_k) - \frac{1}{a}\sum A_i(h)$, where $h$ ranges from $[-3, 3]$. The purpose of subtracting the mean is to eliminate interference caused by differences in the value ranges of different base activations, making the shape differences of LAF under different keyword conditions visible.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Text-Conditional Feature Modulation (TCFM)

TCFM solves the problem of "where to inject text information and with what mechanism." At the mechanism level, as described in the previous section, it replaces ReLU with a weighted combination of activation functions conditioned on TE, encoding the keyword condition as "activation shape" rather than "feature value offset." Compared to AdaIN, which directly performs per-channel affine transformation on instance-normalized features (changing features before entering the next layer), LAF only changes the non-linear shape, causing more indirect and gentle perturbation to the features—this is an advantage for few-shot scenarios: even if the condition is learned poorly, the damage to the backbone features is limited.

At the position level, the paper systematically scanned the effects of placing TCFM in positions G0 to G5 (and combinations) in Table III, with very clear conclusions: placing it in low layers (G0 to G3) severely harms performance—G0 average AP 48.92%, G1 39.04%, G2 45.58%, G3 54.77%, all significantly lower than the pre-trained model's 72.02%; placing it in high layers G4, G5 achieves 85.18% and 84.96% respectively, with the G4+G5 combination being optimal at 87.22%. The paper's explanation for the failure in low layers is: low layers produce the basic features that ultimately constitute the keyword representation, and directly modifying these basic features with limited samples damages performance; whereas FW-adapter only adjusts the feature aggregation method, so it is effective at any layer. This finding corroborates AdaKWS's practice of inserting KAM before the final classifier—high-layer conditioning is a consensus position in this field, and this paper solidifies this consensus through ablation. Reading the parameter column in Table III also reveals a structural detail: doing TCFM in G1-G3 (inside SE-Res2Blocks) involves 69.5K tunable parameters, much higher than 45.0K or 41.9K for G4/G5, because there are more LAF insertion points inside the residual blocks—low-layer conditioning not only hurts accuracy but also consumes more parameters, a double disadvantage.

Figure 3 provides direct evidence at the mechanism level: after TE conditioning for six keywords ('backward', 'happy', 'house', 'bird', 'cat', 'down'), the LAF output curves in G4 and G5 have different shapes, and the curves for the same keyword in G4 and G5 are also different. This shows that TCFM is not just for show; it really learns different activation profiles per keyword and per layer, providing visual verification for the claim that "keyword information is conditioned into the acoustic encoder."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Feature Weight Adapter (FW-adapter)

FW-adapter solves the problem of "which existing parameters in the backbone are worth re-tuning with few samples," and the answer is to adjust only BN layers and SE modules (highlighted in green in Figure 1), freezing all convolution weights. The design assumption is directly inherited from conclusions in speaker recognition domain adaptation research (references [25][26]): the pre-trained acoustic encoder has already learned essential keyword features using massive samples, so few-shot transfer only needs to adjust how low-level features are aggregated into high-level features, and SE (recalibrating channel weights based on importance) and BN (adjusting normalization statistics and affine parameters of feature distribution) happen to be the lightest structures for doing this.

The ablation in Table II provides three layers of evidence for this hypothesis. First layer: adapting BN in any single group stably exceeds the pre-trained model—average AP for BN adaptation in groups G0 to G6 ranges from 72.24% to 76.63%, all higher than PT's 72.02%, proving that "adjusting feature weights can adapt" holds at every depth. Second layer: adapting BN in all groups together (9.0K parameters) achieves 79.40%, better than any single group, indicating that the gains from BN in each group are complementary and cumulative. Third layer: joint adaptation of SE and BN further improves performance—SE G1&BN, SE G2&BN, SE G3&BN achieve 84.12%, 84.19%, 84.33% respectively (each 41.8K parameters), with the best being SE G3&BN. However, an important failure mode appears here: spreading SE adaptation across all groups (SE & BN, 107.3K parameters) drops performance to 80.18%, lower than single-group SE adaptation; the paper's hypothesis is that this adds too many parameters for few-shot KWS. In other words, BN adaptation is "the more the better" (complementary accumulation), while SE adaptation is "too much is as bad as not enough" (parameter bloat backfires)—the dose-effect directions of these two lightweight structures are opposite, which is the most engineering-valuable boundary uncovered by ablation. Another noteworthy data point is that SE & BN full-group adaptation without the TE classifier (107.8K parameters) achieves only 64.32%, even lower than PT; this shows that when tunable parameters increase, learning a classifier from scratch misleads the entire embedding space, and the TE classifier plays an anchoring role for training stability under large tunable parameter counts.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: TE Classifier (Fixing Text Embeddings as Scoring Prototypes)

As described in the mathematical principles section, the TE classifier fixes the frozen TE as the weights of the final fully connected layer, making scoring equivalent to the cosine similarity between AE and TE. Its value needs to be viewed in contrast to two failed baselines: PT (scoring with TE, no fine-tuning) 72.02% vs. FT clf (learning classifier from scratch) 50.14% (Table II), a gap of over 21 percentage points, proving that TE is a far superior scoring prototype to a "classifier learned from 15 samples"; and all adaptation rows with TE classifier (Table II) are significantly higher than PT, proving that "fixing scoring prototype + fine-tuning AE to approach it" is a more stable few-shot adaptation geometry than "fixing AE + learning new prototype" and "moving both." This also explains the modular rollback capability of TA-adapter: because TE and scoring geometry are not destroyed, removing the adapter allows lossless restoration of the pre-trained model to adapt to the next keyword.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Differences from four categories of existing methods: For AdaKWS (reference [22]), the differences lie in the conditioning mechanism (LAF's $d \times a$ parameters vs. AdaIN's $d \times 2 \times f$ parameters), injection position (high layers G4/G5 inside the encoder vs. after the encoder), and training paradigm (few-shot adaptation with frozen dual towers vs. joint training from scratch). Table IV shows a crushing gap at 15-shot: 90.38% vs. 61.67%, and the gap widens further at 5-shot: 87.63% vs. 58.32%. For binary/trinary classification baselines (references [19][16], not freezing the pre-trained model, training binary or target/non-target/noise trinary heads for the target word respectively), the difference for TA-adapter is preserving the pre-trained metric space structure rather than covering it with a new classification head, leading by about 16 to 18 percentage points at 15-shot. For few-shot methods on the audio registration route (references [14] etc.), TA-adapter is the first to introduce few-shot transfer to the text registration route and additionally utilizes TE, a text-side prior. For TF-KWS models without fine-tuning (AdaMS 74.44%, RPL 77.93%, both tested directly after training on out-of-domain data, marked with daggers in Table IV), TA-adapter raises RPL to 87.63% with the same sample budget (5 samples), verifying the independent value of the "adaptation" step.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Pre-training completely reuses the configuration of the authors' previous work (reference [11]): datasets, acoustic features, and model architecture are all consistent. The pre-training corpus is King-ASR-066 (DataOceanAI, reference [33]), which is out-of-domain data relative to the evaluation set. Evaluation is conducted on Google Speech Commands V2 (GSC V2, reference [15]), consisting of 35 keywords, of which 10 were seen in the pre-training corpus ('backward', 'follow', 'forward', 'happy', 'house', 'one', 'seven', 'sheila', 'visual', 'zero'), and 25 were unseen, forming the Seen/Unseen split in Table I. Table I also gives the scale: Seen group training samples 2,332, Unseen group 3,090 (this is the sample volume used for the full fine-tuning reference FT full-shot, also explaining the pain point magnitude of "collecting big data for a single keyword is unrealistic"); validation set positive/negative samples Seen 231/9,750, Unseen 307/9,674; test set positive/negative samples Seen 249/10,756, Unseen 341/10,664. The positive-to-negative sample ratio is approximately 1:31 to 1:43 (derived from Table I numbers), typical of an imbalanced detection task.

Few-shot settings: 5-shot, 10-shot, 15-shot three tiers, with each keyword randomly sampling the corresponding number of samples per tier to independently train a keyword-specific model. The mini-batch for fine-tuning consists of 256 samples: 128 target word samples, 96 non-target samples (randomly sampled from the remaining 34 keywords), and 32 noise samples (from GSC V2 background noise recordings); target and non-target samples undergo data augmentation consistent with pre-training. Optimizer AdamW, initial learning rate 1e-5, halved every 20 epochs, for a total of 150 epochs, weight decay 1e-5. To simulate real scenarios, the official validation and test sets are convolved with synthetic room impulse responses (reference [34]) from OpenSLR and overlaid with MUSAN noise (reference [35]), with SNR 5 to 25 dB, expanding to 4 times the original scale. To mitigate randomness, the fine-tuning dataset under each condition is randomly resampled 5 times and averaged; the entire fine-tuning process takes less than 1 hour on a single V100, and the optimal epoch is selected based on validation set AP. Ablation experiments (Table II, Table III) are done only at 15-shot with 10 keywords (5 Seen: 'follow', 'happy', 'house', 'one', 'seven'; 5 Unseen: 'cat', 'dog', 'eight', 'nine', 'off') to control variables; the main comparison (Table IV) uses all 35 keywords. Since the keyword sets for the two experiments differ, the 87.22% in Table III and the 90.38% in Table IV (both the best configuration at 15-shot) cannot be directly compared.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper uses two widely used metrics in the KWS domain (references [7][9][10][11][36]): Equal Error Rate (EER, the error rate when the false alarm rate and miss rate are equal, lower is better) and Average Precision (AP, the area under the precision-recall curve, higher is better). The rationale for selection can be understood from the task structure: keyword detection is an extremely imbalanced task (negative samples are 30 to 40+ times the positive samples in Table I). The precision-recall curve is sensitive to the retrieval quality of the minority class, and AP reflects the comprehensive detection quality under all scoring thresholds; EER provides a single scalar at the balance point of false positives and false negatives, aligning with the practical concern of system threshold tuning. Table IV reports the Seen/Unseen averages for both, while ablation tables (Table II, Table III) report only AP. It should be noted that the paper body does not elaborate on the formal definitions of the metrics; the definitions here are supplemented according to domain conventions.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table IV compares six methods across all 35 keywords and three shot tiers (AP/EER are averages of Seen and Unseen):

- AdaMS (TF-KWS model configured in reference [11], no fine-tuning): AP 74.44% / EER 8.25%;
- RPL (the paper's own pre-trained model, no fine-tuning, i.e., the counterpart of PT in Table II on the full word set): AP 77.93% / EER 7.94%;
- 2-class clf (reference [19], binary fine-tuning without freezing the pre-trained model): 5/10/15-shot APs are 50.97% / 65.53% / 72.38%, EERs are 20.45% / 15.27% / 10.49%;
- 3-class clf (reference [16], target/non-target/noise trinary fine-tuning): 52.54% / 68.67% / 73.98%, EERs 18.91% / 13.22% / 9.87%;
- AdaKWS (reference [22], replacing TA-adapter with KAM, keeping the rest of the architecture and training method consistent, the most strictly controlled baseline): 58.32% / 59.13% / 61.67%, EERs 14.02% / 13.33% / 12.59%;
- TA-adapter (best configuration in Table III, TCFM placed in G4/G5 plus FW-adapter SE G3&BN): 87.63% / 89.15% / 90.38%, EERs 5.09% / 4.66% / 4.56%.

There are three layers of comparison conclusions. First, TA-adapter leads comprehensively under all conditions; at 15-shot, it is 16.40 percentage points higher than the strongest baseline (3-class clf 73.98%, derived from Table IV) and 28.71 percentage points higher than AdaKWS. Second, the gap increases as samples decrease: at 5-shot, the lead over the strongest baseline is 35.09 percentage points (87.63% vs. 52.54%), and TA-adapter's 5-shot result (87.63%) exceeds the 15-shot results of all baselines (highest 73.98%)—the advantage of parameter efficiency under extreme scarcity is amplified, which is empirical support for the design claim that "adapter parameter count must be small." Third, relative to the un-fine-tuned pre-trained model (RPL 77.93%/7.94%), 5 samples bring an AP improvement of 9.70 percentage points and reduce EER from 7.94% to 5.09% (a relative decrease of about 36%, derived from Table IV), and EER monotonically improves with increasing shot (5.09→4.66→4.56), indicating that the method has no突变 (sudden) sensitivity to sample size. Another notable signal is that AdaKWS's AP hardly increases with shot (58.32→61.67), corroborating the diagnosis that its conditioning module is too parameter-heavy and cannot be learned in few-shot settings. Regarding fairness, the paper states that all few-shot baselines share the same dataset, model architecture, and pre-trained model, differing only in training strategy, making this comparison largely free from interference by backbone strength. (Detail reminder: The citation numbers for AdaMS and RPL in Table IV seem swapped relative to the reference list—according to the reference list, [8] is AdaMS and [11] is RPL; rely on method names when reading.)

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The ablation chain completely answers four questions:

**Why full-parameter fine-tuning was rejected**. Table II gives three rows of comparison: FT full-shot (fine-tuning 2.21M parameters using all available samples) 95.35%, which is the performance ceiling but unrealistic in data collection cost; FT 15-shot is only 67.04%, lower than PT's 72.02%; FT clf (learning only a new classifier with 0.5K parameters) 50.14%. These three rows define the problem space: too many tunable parameters (2.21M) leads to overfitting, learning a new classifier (learning weights from scratch) leads to collapse, and the only feasible direction is "very small parameter count + reusing pre-trained prototypes."

**Dose effect of FW-adapter** (Table II). Per-group BN adaptation is uniformly positive (75.50%-76.63%), full-group BN accumulates to 79.40%, single-group SE&BN best is SE G3&BN 84.33% (41.8K parameters), but full-group SE&BN drops back to 80.18%—BN gains are complementary and cumulative, while SE parameter overload backfires, meaning the correct dosage for the two structures differs. An附带 (incidental) finding: the more tunable parameters, the more critical the anchoring role of the TE classifier (same full-group SE&BN, with TE classifier 80.18%, without only 64.32%).

**Position effect of TCFM** (Table III). Low-layer injection (G0-G3) causes catastrophic drops to 39.04%-54.77%, high-layer injection (G4/G5) is effective, with G4+G5 optimal at 87.22%. Looking at the parameter column, low-layer injection is accompanied by parameter bloat (G1-G3 requires 69.5K, due to many LAF insertion points inside residual blocks). Also, the bottom two rows of Table III provide evidence of synergy: TCFM at G4+G5 without FW-adapter is only 74.12% (3.2K parameters), reaching 87.22% (45.0K parameters) with FW-adapter, while FW-adapter alone is best at 84.33% (Table II)—both components are effective on their own (74.12 and 84.33 are both higher than PT's 72.02%), and using them together increases performance by another 3 percentage points, which the paper calls synergistic complementarity: one injects keyword content, the other calibrates aggregation weights, acting on orthogonal degrees of freedom.

**Final ledger of parameter overhead**. The LAF parameters added by TCFM total 3.2K, accounting for 0.14% of the model's total parameters (cited in both the abstract and main text); FW-adapter adds no new parameters (reuses existing BN/SE weights); the optimal configuration has a total of 45.0K tunable parameters (Table III), accounting for about 2% of the full model's 2.21M trainable parameters (derived). This two-layer ledger of "adding 0.14%, utilizing 2%" is the parameter efficiency claim of TA-adapter.

The LAF visualization in Figure 3, as mechanism verification, is included in ablation findings: activation profiles in G4/G5 under six keyword conditions are different from each other and across layers, indicating that conditioning indeed varies with text content, rather than all words sharing a fixed activation.

## Main Contributions

- **Problem Definition Level**: The first few-shot transfer learning method for Text-Enrolled Flexible KWS (TF-KWS). Previous few-shot KWS research focused on the audio registration route; this paper points out and utilizes the unique asset of TF-KWS—the TE produced by the pre-trained text encoder can simultaneously serve as a conditional signal (entering TCFM) and a scoring prototype (entering the TE classifier), which is structurally impossible in the audio registration route.
- **Method Level**: Proposes the three-component design of TA-adapter—TCFM uses LAF to achieve text conditioning with only $d \times a$ parameters (compared to AdaIN's $d \times 2 \times f$, a difference of about 85 times, derived), FW-adapter migrates SE/BN domain adaptation experience from the speaker recognition domain to keyword adaptation and clarifies the dose boundaries of the two, and the TE classifier fixes text embeddings as scoring weights to eliminate the largest source of instability in few-shot classifier learning.
- **Empirical Level**: Under strict conditions on all 35 keywords in GSC V2, with reverberation and noise added (SNR 5-25 dB), 5-shot raises the pre-trained model's AP from 77.93% to 87.63% and reduces EER from 7.94% to 5.09% (Table IV); 15-shot reaches 90.38%/4.56%, comprehensively exceeding AdaKWS and classification head fine-tuning baselines, with the advantage increasing as samples decrease.
- **Engineering Level**: Adds only 0.14% new parameters (3.2K), with the optimal configuration having a total of 45.0K tunable parameters (about 2%); single-keyword fine-tuning takes less than 1 hour on a single V100; modular design supports seamless restoration of the pre-trained model after adaptation, with adapters plugged and swapped by keyword, directly corresponding to the needs of multi-keyword product deployment.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Strong dependence on pre-trained TF-KWS infrastructure**: All premises of TA-adapter rely on the existence of a dual-tower pre-trained model well-aligned with metric losses like RPL. The upper limit of TE quality determines the upper limit of conditioning and scoring—if the text representation of the target word is underfit in the text encoder (rare words, spelling variants, mixed-language spelling), both the TE classifier and TCFM will fail simultaneously; the paper does not perform stress tests on this.
- **Does not yet reach zero-shot**: Each keyword still requires 5-15 real speech samples and one fine-tuning (although less than 1 hour). Relative to the ultimate form of TF-KWS where users only type to register, this is a compromise: exchanging a small amount of recording for about 10 percentage points of AP. When deploying multiple keywords, a copy of adapter parameters and BN/SE states must be stored for each keyword; the storage and switching overhead after keyword quantity scales is not reported by the paper.
- **Absolute performance still has a gap**: The 90.38% at 15-shot (Table IV) still has a gap of about 5 percentage points from the full fine-tuning result of 95.35% (Table II), indicating that the adapter scheme is currently "high cost-performance" rather than "lossless."
- **Single evaluation domain**: All conclusions are built on 35 English words in GSC V2 (isolated command words of about 1 second). Real wake words are often multi-syllable phrases or streaming audio of continuous speech by multiple people; performance on long phrases, cross-language, and continuous streaming is not reported; end-side deployment metrics such as latency and parameter footprint are also not reported.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- Ablation experiments (Table II, Table III) are done only at 15-shot with 10 keywords (5 Seen + 5 Unseen), while the main comparison (Table IV) uses all 35 words; the numbers from the two groups cannot be directly translated; and each condition is resampled only 5 times for averaging, with no variance or confidence intervals reported, making it impossible to exclude the possibility that conclusions at the percentage point level (e.g., SE G2&BN 84.19% vs. SE G3&BN 84.33%) fall within the noise range.
- Baseline coverage has gaps: no comparison with strong audio-registration few-shot baselines like the SSL plus meta-learning route (reference [14]) in the same arena (although the task settings differ, at least discussion is possible); nor is Ta-adapter tested against other parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods (such as LoRA-style low-rank injection), so the claim that "LAF is the optimal conditioning mechanism" lacks comparison with alternative mechanisms of the same parameter magnitude.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity is not reported: the number of base activation functions $a$, the selection of the six functions (chosen based on validation set), 150 training epochs, the batch composition of 128/96/32, and the search space for the TCFM-FW combination are all single-point settings; whether the failure of full-group SE adaptation stems from the total parameter count or the SE structure itself lacks attribution experiments with per-group breakdowns.
- The reverberation and noise on the evaluation set are generated by a synthetic pipeline (RIR convolution plus MUSAN overlay), lacking verification with real far-field recordings; additionally, the citation labels for AdaMS and RPL in Table IV seem swapped (according to the reference list, [8] is AdaMS and [11] is RPL), which is a表述 (expression) flaw but does not affect the numerical conclusions.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- The direction pointed out by the paper itself: using Text-to-Speech (TTS) to synthesize training data for target keywords, advancing "few-shot adaptation with a small amount of real recording" to "zero-shot transfer with zero real recordings." The framework of TA-adapter is ready for this—the TE classifier and TCFM do not require real speech; what is missing is only the source of fine-tuning data, which TTS exactly fills.
- Extension at the mechanism level: compare and combine LAF conditioning in TCFM with other parameter-efficient methods like low-rank adaptation and prompt tuning under the same budget; explore learnable selection of conditioning positions (currently G4/G5 are discrete conclusions from grid search); make the dose effect of "BN gains are cumulative, SE gains overload" into an adaptive per-layer ratio.
- Extension at the evaluation and deployment level: expand to multi-syllable wake words, multi-language and code-mixed keywords, continuous streaming, and streaming detection; report end-side latency and memory usage and perform real-device verification with quantization; perform sensitivity analysis of TE quality on final performance, providing engineering guidelines on what scale of pre-training the text encoder needs; move from single-keyword independent adaptation to multi-keyword shared adapters or multi-head conditional architectures to amortize deployment costs.
