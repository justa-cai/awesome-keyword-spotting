## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

End-to-end (E2E) automatic speech recognition (ASR) models have become mainstream in recent years due to their unified structure and simple pipeline. Whisper is a representative example: it is based on the Transformer architecture and trained on 680,000 hours of speech data using large-scale weak supervision (Introduction, Citation [16]). However, E2E models have a structural weakness: their output distribution is dominated by the statistical patterns of the training corpus. Proper nouns—such as person names, technical terms, and product names—that appear infrequently in the training data exhibit significantly high error rates. While this may be a minor issue for general transcription, it is often critical in real-world business scenarios: if technical terms repeated in a technical presentation are transcribed incorrectly, the readability and usability of the entire transcript collapse.

The pain point scenario addressed in this paper is very specific: the technical presentation transcription task faced by the Huawei Translation Service Center (the internal test set is constructed from this source). Entity words are primarily in Chinese, interspersed with English technical terms (Chinese-English code-switching), and entities are provided ad-hoc by users, varying by scenario. This imposes two叠加 constraints: First, entities are "open-vocabulary"—it is impossible to pre-train all entities into the model or retrain for each new entity. Second, the model already exists and is expensive (Whisper); the ideal solution should inject entity priors without modifying or with minimal modification to the main model, rather than training a new model with a bias module from scratch.

Notably, Whisper natively provides a channel for injecting priors: providing a text prompt during decoding allows the model to adjust its output, unlike previous methods that required an external bias module (Introduction). However, a prerequisite question for this channel remains unsolved: what words should be placed in the prompt? While users can manually input entity lists, for a fully automated system, the system must first "know" which entities actually appeared in the audio—this is precisely an Open-Vocabulary Keyword Spotting (OV-KWS) problem (detection on any user-given vocabulary, rather than fixed wake words). The entire system of this paper revolves around the point of "using an extremely lightweight KWS frontend to automatically generate Whisper prompts."

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The Introduction systematically reviews two classic routes for hot-word bias and points out their respective defects:

- **Shallow Fusion (weighting hot-word scores during beam search decoding)**: Simple to implement, but requires tuning the optimal fusion weight for each task. This weight is a global scalar; if the language, speech rate, or number of hot words changes, it must be retuned, making it fragile in engineering. More fundamentally, it only adds scores at the decoding end; the acoustic model's representation of rare entities does not improve.
- **Deep Fusion (jointly training a context encoder from scratch with the ASR model)**: Has a higher performance ceiling, but the cost is that it must be jointly trained from scratch with the ASR model—training costs are high, and the bias module is deeply coupled with the main model. If the main model is upgraded, the bias module must be retrained, making it impossible to migrate to existing strong ASR models (such as the various open-source Whisper size versions).
- **Adapter and Pointer Network Routes**: Using adapters to modify intermediate features of the ASR model, or pointer networks to modify the output distribution, also requires structural surgery on the main model, with a high barrier to entry. Shallow and deep fusion can be combined, but complexity increases further.
- **Specific Gap for Whisper**: Whisper's prompt mechanism only solves "how to use priors," not "where priors come from." Stuffing the entire entity list into the prompt (referred to as naive prompt in the paper) works in simple scenarios but leads to hallucinations in complex conversations involving spontaneous speech and code-switching. Moreover, if the list is long but only one or two words actually appear, the signal-to-noise ratio of the prompt will be poor. The system lacks a component to automatically filter "entities that actually appeared" from the audio side.
- **Authors' Previous Work CB-whisper (LREC-COLING 2024, Citation [21])**: Also used OV-KWS for context bias, but the modules were heavier. The abstract explicitly claims that this paper's lightweight OV-KWS module and multitask training method significantly improve system adaptability, efficiency, and accuracy—i.e., the positioning of this paper relative to the previous work is "lighter, more general, and more accurate."

### Key Challenges to Be Solved

The paper actually needs to answer three progressively deepening questions. First, how to reuse the hidden states of the Whisper encoder for open-vocabulary detection, rather than attaching an independent acoustic encoding path—this determines whether the additional overhead can be compressed to the extreme (the answer given in the paper is a four-layer CNN with only 0.2M parameters). Second, the capacity of the 0.2M small classifier is limited; to achieve usable detection accuracy (F1 ~0.9), it is necessary to fine-tune the Whisper encoder itself, and fine-tuning inevitably impacts the general recognition capability of the original model—how to design the training objective so that KWS and ASR tasks complement each other rather than interfere with each other (catastrophic forgetting is a failure mode explicitly acknowledged and empirically observed in the paper). Third, when the fine-tuned model experiences a rise in error rates on the real domain due to small training data scale, how to step back and make OV-KWS a plug-and-play module that does not touch the main model at all, compatible with frozen Whisper of any size (small / medium / large-v2)—this determines the engineering deployment radius of the method.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system separates the offline database construction and online recognition stages (Figure 1). Offline stage (Figure 1 (a)): For predefined entity words, speech is first synthesized using a TTS model (edge-tts in engineering implementation), then passed through the Whisper encoder to extract hidden states, which are stored in the database as acoustic representations of the entity words. Online stage (Figure 1 (b)): After input speech passes through the Whisper encoder, the hidden representations of all encoder layers are aggregated into sentence-level features using learnable coefficients for weighted summation. A cosine similarity matrix is calculated between these features and the representations of each entity word in the database. A binary classification CNN detects diagonal patterns on the similarity matrix (Figure 1 (c)) to determine whether the entity appears; the detected entity words are sent as prompts to the Whisper decoder to guide its output to the correct transcription.

The design motivation for this architecture can be broken down into three layers. First, why perform detection before decoding and above the encoder? Because Whisper is an encoder-decoder structure, the encoder's hidden states are naturally sufficient representations of acoustic content. Attaching the detection module behind the encoder allows complete reuse of the main model's forward computation, eliminating the need for a second acoustic frontend. Second, why use "TTS synthesis + encoder feature extraction" to build the entity database? Because open-vocabulary means entities are unpredictable; it is impossible to collect real human recordings for each new word. Using TTS allows a new entity to be onboarded purely via text, which is the key engineering premise for "open-vocabulary" to hold (the cost is the acoustic gap between synthesized speech and real human pronunciation, see Limitations). Third, why is the detector a similarity matrix plus CNN? This design explicitly draws from visual keyword detection methods (Citations [18] [19], i.e., "Seeing wake words" audio-visual keyword detection and "audio-text alignment learning" lines): projecting the entities to be detected and the input speech into the same feature space, when an entity appears, a diagonal texture emerges on the similarity matrix. The detection problem becomes a local pattern recognition problem. Since it is a local pattern, choosing a CNN for the classifier is natural: convolutional kernels are naturally good at capturing local textures, and they have very few parameters. The paper emphasizes that this module has linear complexity with respect to the number of keywords, and multiple keywords can be processed in parallel batches using GPU—this is the complexity guarantee for open-vocabulary scalability.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Multi-layer Feature Weighted Aggregation**: Following practices validated on self-supervised speech models (WavLM, Citation [22]), the hidden states of each layer of the Whisper encoder are summed with learnable coefficients as downstream features. The motivation is that different encoder layers carry information at different granularities, letting the data decide which layers to use, rather than manually selecting layers. These coefficients later become important interpretable evidence (Figure 2, see Ablation section).

**Cosine Similarity Matrix and Diagonal Patterns**: Let the frame-level features of the aggregated input speech be $h_i$ (total $T_{utt}$ frames), and the frame-level features of the synthesized speech for the entity word be $e_j$ (total $T_{kw}$ frames). The similarity matrix $S \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{utt} \times T_{kw}}$, where $S_{ij} = \cos(h_i, e_j)$. If the entity indeed appears in the speech, the frames of its corresponding time segment align frame-by-frame with the frames of the entity word, resulting in a high-value band along the diagonal of the matrix. Transforming "whether the word appears" into "whether there is a diagonal texture on the matrix" is a classic technique that reduces a variable-length sequence matching problem to an image local pattern detection problem. Since it is a local pattern, choosing a CNN for the classifier is logical: convolutional kernels are naturally good at capturing local textures, and they have very few parameters. The paper emphasizes that this module has linear complexity with respect to the number of keywords, and multiple keywords can be processed in parallel batches using GPU—this is the complexity guarantee for open-vocabulary scalability.

**Multitask Loss (Equation 1)**:

$$\text{Loss} = L_{ASR} + \alpha \times L_{KWS}, \quad \alpha = 3$$

That is, the weighted sum of the ASR loss and the KWS binary classification loss, with the weight $\alpha$ fixed at 3 during training. Why is this joint objective needed: The CNN classifier has only 0.2M parameters, which is too small in capacity. If only KWS is trained, the frozen Whisper encoder features are not learned for the purpose of "frame-level entity alignment," and detection accuracy cannot be improved (F1 is only about 0.6-0.68 when the encoder is frozen, Table 4). It is necessary to unfreeze the encoder and train it to adapt to the KWS task. Once the encoder is unfrozen, if there is no $L_{ASR}$ term to hold it back, general recognition capability will be lost, so both losses must be present simultaneously. The paper does not provide ablation basis for $\alpha$ being 3 (higher weight for KWS term); it is a directly given hyperparameter (the paper does not report sensitivity experiments for $\alpha$).

**Two-Stage Training Process**: Stage 1: Freeze the Whisper encoder, train only the CNN classifier (OV-KWS) and the Whisper decoder (ASR) for 10 epochs, batch size 128, learning rate $5 \times 10^{-5}$, trainable parameters 153.8M (CNN 0.2M + Decoder 153.6M); Stage 2: Unfreeze the encoder and jointly train the full model for 50 epochs, learning rate $3 \times 10^{-5}$, trainable parameters increase to 241.9M, parameters involved in the OV-KWS task increase from 0.2M to 88.4M (Section 3.1). The strategy of freezing first and then unfreezing is a common stabilization technique: first let the small classifier and decoder converge to a reasonable state in a fixed feature space, then unfreeze the encoder for overall adaptation, avoiding the large model "breaking" the small classifier in the early stages of training. Additionally, the ASR side adopts a random concatenation strategy (Citation [25]) to lengthen training speech—the motivation is that Whisper is trained on 30-second windows, and speech that is too short does not match the training distribution. The decoding beam width is fixed at 5 (Section 3.1).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Ultra-Lightweight OV-KWS Module Based on Similarity Matrix Diagonal Detection

The detector itself is a four-layer CNN with channel sizes 128, 128, 256, 256, kernel size 3, and a total parameter count of only 0.2M (Section 3.1). Choosing a binary classification framework (one "appears / does not appear" decision for each entity word) rather than a sequence localization framework actively reduces the problem difficulty: the system only needs to know if the word is present, not which frame it is in; the alignment information is implicitly carried by the diagonal pattern of the similarity matrix. There is a detail in training data construction that is easily overlooked but specifically elaborated on in the paper: the most intuitive approach is to select positive sample words based on the transcript and randomly sample other words as negative samples, but the paper points out that this is too simple—random negative samples differ too much from positive samples, making it difficult for the classifier to learn fine discriminative boundaries, leading to overfitting. Therefore, hard negatives (Citation [23]) are adopted: deliberately selecting words that overlap with positive samples and are easily confused as negative examples, raising the difficulty of the classification task and forcing the model to learn truly discriminative features. This essentially exchanges prior knowledge of data difficulty for model generalization ability.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Multitask Joint Fine-Tuning of OV-KWS and Context ASR

Having only a lightweight detector is not enough; the second innovation is to close the loop by putting detection and "using detection results" into the same training objective: during fine-tuning, words from real transcripts are concatenated into the Whisper decoder's prompt in a naive prompt format (Table 1, i.e., directly connecting all entity words with commas: Entity1, Entity2, Entity3), and the model updates on both $L_{ASR}$ and $L_{KWS}$ (Equation 1). The key role of this step is to let the Whisper decoder "learn to use prompts": during training, the prompt contains the words actually included in the sentence, allowing the decoder to establish a conditional behavior that "prompt words should be prioritized for output." During inference, switching to entities predicted by OV-KWS allows for seamless connection. The paper verifies in Table 5 that multitask does not harm KWS performance; instead, it brings an F1 improvement on the internal set (see Ablation section), indicating that there is positive sharing between the two tasks at the encoder representation level—both can benefit from an encoder that "focuses more on fine-grained acoustic details."

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Plug-and-Play Usage for Frozen Whisper (Error Correction and Spoken-Form Prompts)

The fine-tuning route suffers from catastrophic forgetting on small-scale data (Table 6 shows a clear rise in MER on the internal set, see Experimental Results section). Therefore, the paper turns OV-KWS around into an independent module that does not touch the main model at all, providing two pathways for consuming detection results (Section 2.5):

- **Pathway 1, ASR Error Correction**. Divided into two types. Pinyin method: Tokenize the transcript; if a Chinese word has the same pinyin as a detected entity, replace it—pinyin is used as the matching condition because the majority of entity errors in Chinese ASR are homophone or near-homophone substitutions, and pinyin matching directly targets this error pattern; for English words, edit distance is calculated, and if it is below a threshold, replacement occurs. LLM method: Use ChatGLM3-6B (Citation [24]) with the prompt template in Table 2 ("Please modify the ASR text based on possible keywords. Keywords: {word1}, {word2}. Recognized text: {ASR transcript}"), providing two examples for few-shot guidance, allowing the language model to use context for smarter rewriting.
- **Pathway 2, Directly Prompt the Frozen Whisper Decoder**. The key lies in the "style" of the prompt. The naive prompt (Entity1, Entity2, Entity3) is a clean written list, while the semantic role of the prompt position in Whisper training is "historical transcription of the previous utterance"—real spontaneous speech transcripts are full of pauses, filler words, and disfluencies. Style mismatch causes two types of problems: hallucinations in complex code-switching conversations, and the model learning to omit filler words and disfluencies following the clean prompt, which反而 (instead) increases MER (Section 4.2 explicitly attributes this). Therefore, the paper designs a spoken-form prompt (Table 1): "The theme of today's speech is this uh, Entity1, Entity2, Entity3. Okay, then I will continue to speak."—wrapping entities in complete sentences with disfluency markers like "uh" and "Okay, then I will continue to speak," making the appearance closer to the historical transcription distribution seen during Whisper training. The insight of this design is: the prompt not only carries vocabulary information, but its form itself is also a signal within the distribution.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to shallow fusion, this paper does not perform global weighting at the decoding scoring end, does not require tuning fusion weights, and correction information enters through the native interface of decoder prompts, with weights automatically learned by model fine-tuning. Compared to deep fusion and adapter/pointer network routes, this paper does not require joint training of a bias encoder from scratch, nor does it perform structural surgery on the main model; entity onboarding follows the "TTS synthesis + encoder feature extraction" recording-free process, decoupling the entity database from the model, allowing the entity database to be directly reused when the main model is upgraded. Compared to the native prompt usage of Whisper, this paper solves the problem of automated generation of prompt content—not manually entered by users, but entities actually detected from the current audio by OV-KWS, with a signal-to-noise ratio far higher than the full list. Compared to the contemporaneous SeACo-Paraformer (Citation [20], non-autoregressive hot-word customized ASR), this paper achieved higher entity recall on the Aishell hot-word subset using detected entities as prompts (Section 4.2). Compared to the authors' previous work CB-whisper (Citation [21]), the detection module is lighter (0.2M parameters, four-layer CNN), and multitask training replaces the previous training method; the abstract claims improvements in adaptability, efficiency, and accuracy (the paper does not report a direct comparison table with the previous work; this comparison is self-reported by the authors).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Three training datasets (Section 3.2): Aishell-1, Chinese Mandarin ASR corpus, 150 hours; LibriSpeech train-clean subset, English, 100 hours; TALCS, Chinese-English code-switching corpus, from English teaching scenarios, 578 hours. The paper compared two training configurations: using only Chinese data (zh) and using all three mixed (zh+en). For each speech, positive sample words were randomly sampled, and entity speech was generated using edge-tts synthesis. Four test sets (Table 3): Aishell-dev 1334 sentences / 113 minutes / 371 entities, Aishell-test 808 sentences / 76 minutes / 226 entities (both are Aishell hot-word subsets, Chinese); Internal-1 99 sentences / 41 minutes / 150 entities, Internal-2 112 sentences / 47 minutes / 346 entities—the internal sets are technical presentations, with entities manually annotated, primarily in Chinese interspersed with English technical terms. The composition of the test sets implies the evaluation intent: Aishell subsets represent "in-domain hot-word scenarios with the same distribution as training data," while internal sets represent "real business, out-of-domain, code-switching scenarios." The performance gap between the two is the source of evidence for the discussion of catastrophic forgetting later in the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **F1 Score (for OV-KWS)**: Harmonic mean of precision and recall. The reason for choosing it is that the costs of both types of errors in KWS are not low—missing detection means no prompt is available for the entity, and false detection means wrong entities enter the prompt or wrong replacements enter the transcript; the harmonic mean is sensitive to短板 (shortcomings) on either side.
- **Mixed Error Rate MER (for ASR)**: A character error rate adapted for Chinese-English code-switching, counting each Chinese character and each English word as one error unit. It is equivalent to CER for pure Chinese and WER for pure English (Section 3.3). Direct CER cannot be used because in code-switching text, splitting English words by letters would severely inflate the weight of English words.
- **Entity Recall**: The proportion of entity words correctly recognized. This is the goal truly concerned by the business—the overall MER may decrease only slightly, but whether entities are written correctly determines the usability of the transcript, so it must be reported alongside MER.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

**Fine-Tuning Route (Table 6, MER% / Entity Recall%)**: Using Whisper-small as the baseline. On Aishell-dev, baseline no prompt 16.7 / 6.3, baseline with ground-truth entity prompt 13.3 / 81.6; after fine-tuning, the zh+en model using predicted entity prompts dropped to 9.2 / 88.4. On Aishell-test, the corresponding values were 20.1 / 10.1 → 15.9 / 82.1 (baseline ground-truth prompt) → 10.2 / 87.7 (zh+en predicted prompt). Three key points: First, the recall rate of the fine-tuned model using "predicted entities" as prompts (88.4%, 87.7%) surpassed that of the original Whisper-small using "manual ground-truth entities" as prompts (81.6%, 82.1%), indicating that fine-tuning allowed the decoder to truly learn to consume prompts, whereas the original model could not fully utilize even correct prompts; Second, the model trained on mixed data (zh+en) had higher recall on all test sets (e.g., 91.5% vs 87.1% for zh under ground-truth prompt on Aishell-dev), attributed by the paper to better prompt generalization and universality; Third, compared to the SOTA hot-word model SeACo-Paraformer (recall 86%, 87% on Aishell-dev/test), KWS-Whisper's 88.4%, 87.7% achieved a surpass (Section 4.2). The claimed absolute recall improvement at the abstract level ("up to 80% on Aishell hot-word subset, up to 10% on internal code-switching set") matches Table 6 data (Aishell-dev increased from 6.3% to 87.1%, an absolute increase of about 80.8 percentage points; Internal-2 increased from 60.8% to 71.4%, about 10.6 percentage points).

However, Table 6 simultaneously exposes the failure side of the fine-tuning route—catastrophic forgetting: MER rises comprehensively on the internal set. On Internal-1, baseline no prompt 5.9, zh fine-tuned no prompt soared to 14.7, and entity recall dropped from 78.5% to 49.0%; even zh+en fine-tuning with predicted prompts (7.7 / 86.3) failed to pull MER back to baseline levels (5.9). The paper explicitly attributes this: the fine-tuning data scale is small, and the training domain (audiobooks, teaching scenarios) does not match the test domain (technical presentations), weakening Whisper's universality. This complete exposure of "domestic victory, out-of-domain MER rise" is the paper's honesty, and it directly催aged (spurred) the plugin route.

**Plugin Route (Table 7, Frozen Whisper, MER% / Entity Recall%)**: Attaching an independent OV-KWS module to the original Whisper of three sizes: small / medium / large-v2. Taking Whisper-large-v2 as an example, on Internal-1: no processing 4.4 / 87.3, pinyin correction 4.3 / 88.9, LLM correction 6.0 / 89.9, naive prompt 4.7 / 97.4, spoken-form prompt 2.9 / 97.7; on Internal-2: 5.6 / 79.9 → 5.5 / 83.9 → 7.7 / 85.1 → 6.6 / 95.1 → 3.6 / 96.9. The abstract claims absolute MER decreases on the internal set of 2.4%, 1.4%, 1.8% (corresponding to small, medium, large-v2, calculated as the average difference between the optimal spoken-form prompt and baseline across the two internal sets in Table 7: small is (1.9+2.9)/2, medium is (0.8+1.9)/2, large-v2 is (1.5+2.0)/2). The pattern is very clear: pinyin correction consistently wins slightly across all sizes (utilizing the prior that "homophone substitution is the main pattern of Chinese entity errors," but can only fix homophone errors); LLM correction has higher recall but MER rises (e.g., large-v2 Internal-2 worsens from 5.6 to 7.7), attributed by the paper to LLM hallucinations—more entities are corrected correctly, but more non-entities are also incorrectly changed; Prompting methods are significantly better than correction methods overall, with the paper giving the reason that prompting allows the model to continue referencing acoustic features for joint decision-making, while correction methods blindly modify in text space; Naive prompts cause a slight rise in MER relative to the original model (medium Internal-2 rises from 6.7 to 14.6, the most severe deterioration), because clean written prompts induce Whisper to omit filler words and disfluencies; Spoken-form prompts simultaneously lower MER and increase recall, being the best in the entire table. Another horizontal observation worth emphasizing: Frozen Whisper-small with spoken-form prompts on the internal set (4.0 / 95.9, 6.8 / 91.1) comprehensively outperforms the fine-tuned KWS-Whisper-small (7.7 / 86.3, 13.2 / 71.4)—the plugin usage that does not touch the main model actually wins on both MER and recall in this out-of-domain scenario, which is the hardest evidence for the paper's conclusion that "fine-tuning is not a panacea; plugins are the correct solution for out-of-domain scenarios."

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

**Encoder Frozen vs. Unfrozen + Training Language Composition (Table 4, F1)**: When the encoder is frozen, training only on Aishell yields F1 of about 0.62, 0.60 on Aishell test, and 0.68 on the internal set; training on three mixed corpora brings consistent small improvements; After unfreezing the encoder (OV-KWS task parameters increase from 0.2M to 88.4M), F1 jumps significantly: Aishell domain zh training 0.85 / 0.89 is slightly better than zh+en's 0.83 / 0.84, while the internal set zh+en reaches 0.90 / 0.88, significantly higher than zh's 0.75 / 0.82. There are two conclusions: Encoder adaptation is the main engine for detection accuracy (the 0.2M small CNN cannot support the task; F1 is only in the 0.6 range when frozen); Mixed training brings out-of-domain generalization at the cost of slight concessions in-domain.
**Single Task vs. Multitask (Table 5, F1)**: Simultaneously optimizing ASR and OV-KWS does not harm KWS performance: On Aishell-dev/test, multitask 0.83 / 0.84 vs single task 0.86 / 0.86 (slight drop in-domain), on Internal-1 multitask 0.90 surpasses single task 0.87, on Internal-2持平 (持平 means equal/same) 0.88. For reference, the values in the multitask column of Table 5 are consistent with the zh+en unfrozen column of Table 4 (0.83 / 0.84 / 0.90 / 0.88, cross-checked by the author), indicating that this ablation was conducted under mixed data configuration; trading a few tenths of an F1 point in-domain for out-of-domain improvement is a worthwhile deal, and indirectly supports the judgment that "the two tasks share benefits at the encoder representation level."
**Encoder Layer Weights (Figure 2)**: Learnable layer weights show that layers 8 to 12 (deep layers) have a greater impact on the OV-KWS task, and deep layer weights increase further after fine-tuning, indicating that the encoder has adapted directionally to the KWS task. This is consistent with the general rule of self-supervised models that "higher layers lean towards acoustic-semantic discriminative features," and provides post-hoc validation for the design of "weighted summation rather than taking the last layer."
**Prompt Source and Format (Table 6, Table 7)**: Regarding prompt source, the gap between ground-truth prompts and predicted prompts is small (e.g., zh+en Aishell-dev 91.5% vs 88.4%), proving that OV-KWS detection quality is sufficient to support downstream tasks; Regarding prompt format, spoken-form comprehensively outperforms naive prompts (see previous subsection).
**Ablations Not Performed**: Loss weight $\alpha=3$, specific construction ratio of hard negatives, TTS voice and quantity, specific strategy of random concatenation, beam width 5, the paper does not report ablations (paper does not report).

## Main Contributions

- Proposes the KWS-Whisper framework for open-vocabulary keyword detection on Whisper encoder hidden states: using TTS to build an entity database, multi-layer weighted features, and cosine similarity matrix diagonal detection as a trio, compressing entity detection into a four-layer CNN with only 0.2M parameters, and the module is linearly scalable with respect to the number of keywords (Figure 1, Section 2.2).
- Proposes a multitask fine-tuning method for OV-KWS and context ASR (Equation 1, $\alpha=3$), combined with a two-stage unfreezing process, allowing detection F1 to reach about 0.9 on the internal set (Table 4), and allowing the decoder to learn to consume prompts—the fine-tuned model using predicted entities surpasses the original model using manual ground-truth entities in recall (Table 6).
- Entity recall on the Aishell hot-word subset exceeds the contemporaneous SOTA SeACo-Paraformer (88.4% / 87.7% vs 86% / 87%, Section 4.2).
- Systematically provides two usages of OV-KWS as a plug-and-play module (pinyin correction, LLM correction, naive prompt, spoken-form prompt), allowing frozen Whisper of any size to benefit: large-v2 with spoken-form prompt reduced MER on two internal sets from 4.4 / 5.6 to 2.9 / 3.6, and increased recall from 87.3% / 79.9% to 97.7% / 96.9% (Table 7).
- Explicitly exposes and qualitatively analyzes the catastrophic forgetting problem of small-data fine-tuning (Table 6 shows MER rise on the internal set), and based on this provides an engineering conclusion: for out-of-domain scenarios, prioritize using frozen models with plugins rather than fine-tuning.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Catastrophic forgetting of the fine-tuning route is not fundamentally solved**: After fine-tuning on small-scale, out-of-domain (audiobooks and teaching scenarios vs. technical presentations) data, MER on the internal set rose from baseline 5.9 to 14.7 (zh, Table 6); even with mixed data training and predicted prompts (7.7), it was worse than the baseline (5.9). Multitask loss only mitigates rather than solves the problem; the paper's final recommendation is to retreat to freezing plus plugins, effectively admitting that the fine-tuning route is unusable on real domains.
- **Entity database relies on TTS synthesized speech**: Onboarding open-vocabulary entities relies on edge-tts synthesis followed by encoder feature extraction; synthesized speech has systematic differences from real pronunciation in prosody, channel, and accent, which will affect the alignment quality of the similarity matrix; the paper does not report control experiments comparing synthesized speech with real human registration features (paper does not report).
- **Linear complexity and pressure from large vocabularies**: The module has linear complexity with respect to the number of keywords; after the vocabulary scales up (the paper's future work explicitly mentions large-scale keyword databases), the overhead of calculating similarity matrices word-by-word plus CNN forward pass will become a bottleneck; the paper lists "improving OV-KWS efficiency" as future work in the conclusion, indicating that the current version has not implemented indexing or sub-word level acceleration.
- **Each correction pathway has hard flaws**: The pinyin method can only fix homophone errors and is powerless against non-homophone acoustic misrecognitions; The LLM method (ChatGLM3-6B) has better recall but hallucinations directly increase MER (MER in all LLM correction rows in Table 7 is worse than the baseline), and it introduces the inference cost and latency of a 6B model; the paper does not report any latency or computation data (paper does not report).
- **Spoken-form prompt is a template-level solution**: Spoken-form prompts wrap entity words in fixed sentence structures; their transferability to different speaking styles and languages is questionable; The root cause of its effectiveness (closeness to the distribution of historical transcripts) also implies sensitivity to prompt length and number of entities; the paper does not report ablations for these variables.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Internal test set scale is very small**: Internal-1 is only 99 sentences / 41 minutes, Internal-2 is 112 sentences / 47 minutes (Table 3), with 150 and 346 entities respectively. Under such a small sample size, differences of a few percentage points in MER / recall have limited statistical significance; the paper does not report significance tests or confidence intervals (paper does not report).
- **Fine-tuning experiments only cover Whisper-small**: Multitask fine-tuning was not conducted on medium / large (Section 3.1 explicitly states only small was selected); "whether fine-tuning larger models can mitigate forgetting" cannot be answered from the paper; medium / large only appear in the frozen plugin route.
- **Baseline coverage is narrow**: No same-field comparison was made with classic bias methods such as shallow fusion, deep fusion, adapters, and pointer networks (these methods were reviewed in the introduction but not included in the experiments); The only external SOTA comparison was with SeACo-Paraformer, and only entity recall values were compared on the Aishell hot-word subset (Section 4.2); MER dimension was not compared.
- **Key hyperparameters and design choices were not ablated**: $\alpha=3$, hard negative construction, two-stage epoch numbers (10 plus 50), random concatenation strategy, beam width 5 all lack sensitivity analysis; Whether there is an interaction between spoken-form prompts and random concatenation was also not discussed.
- **Lack of direct comparison table with previous work CB-whisper**: The abstract claims superiority over the previous work in adaptability, efficiency, and accuracy, but the paper does not provide any quantitative comparison numbers; this can only be regarded as self-reported by the authors.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper itself gives two directions in the conclusion: First, improve the efficiency of the OV-KWS module to support large-scale keyword databases—corresponding to the linear complexity bottleneck mentioned above, possible directions include performing vector retrieval pre-filtering on the entity database, or transforming word-by-word binary classification into multi-label heads with a shared backbone; Second, distill large-scale speech data using the original Whisper model, perform multitask fine-tuning on the general domain, and solve the out-of-domain forgetting problem exposed in Table 6 from the data scale end. Combining experimental evidence, the author believes there are three more directions worth pursuing: Upgrading spoken-form prompts from fixed templates to adaptive generation (dynamically wrapping according to the transcription style of the target domain); Building the database using a mix of real human registration audio and TTS audio to bridge the acoustic gap between synthesis and reality; Completing multitask fine-tuning experiments on medium / large to examine the relationship between model capacity and catastrophic forgetting.
