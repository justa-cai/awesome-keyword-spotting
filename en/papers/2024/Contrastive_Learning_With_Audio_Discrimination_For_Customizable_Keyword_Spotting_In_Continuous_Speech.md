## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword spotting (KWS) is a core entry point for human-computer interaction systems and has been widely deployed in smart home devices and smart cockpits. The performance of fixed wake-word detection systems (which have a fixed phrase built-in at the factory) is now satisfactory. However, making the system "user-customizable" for wake words significantly increases the difficulty—the paper highlights this contradiction right at the beginning. The practical need is straightforward: manufacturers cannot collect data and train separate models for every user's preferred word, nor do users want to wait for a training cycle each time they change their wake word. The ideal form is that the user only submits one registration (a piece of text or an audio clip), and the system can detect any new word without updating the model.

The root cause of this increased difficulty lies in the information asymmetry between training and usage. A fixed wake-word system can collect massive positive examples for that specific word and target specific confusing negative examples to refine the decision boundary. In contrast, a customizable system does not know which words will be detected at inference time during training. It can only rely on general speech data to learn a "word-level representation space," allowing any word at any position in the speech stream to be segmented and compared for similarity with the embedded text of the registration. This representation space must hold for unseen words, effectively shifting the openness of the vocabulary from a data problem to a representation learning problem—this is the starting point for all designs in this paper.

There is an additional tightening condition in the scenario: continuous speech. Real devices receive an uninterrupted speech stream, where keywords may appear at any position within a sentence. The system must judge segments sequentially in a streaming sliding-window manner. This brings two troubles that do not exist in the isolated word scenario. First, co-articulation effects: the pronunciation of preceding and following words penetrates each other. Segments like "the tail of the previous word spliced with the head of the wake word" are acoustically highly similar to the true wake word but completely different in text. Second, the uncertainty of streaming segmentation: the position cut by the sliding window cannot exactly fall on word boundaries. A large number of "half-word" misaligned segments have never appeared as negative examples during training. The abstract succinctly points out the pain point: co-articulation and streaming segmentation easily cause different texts to correspond to similar audio patterns, thereby triggering false alarms. For wake-word products, the cost of false alarms is far higher than that of missed detections (a speaker waking up by itself in the middle of the night is much worse than not hearing a call), so this problem must be addressed directly.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper divides customizable KWS systems into two main categories and points out the problems with each.

The first category is two-stage methods: first performing acoustic modeling, then performing keyword search. The search环节 generally adopts the weighted finite-state transducer (WFST) graph search used in automatic speech recognition (ASR), which is widely accepted as the dominant graph search scheme. The approach represents any keyword as a short sequence of senones or phonemes, introduces a filler model to absorb non-keyword paths, and the key issue lies in the construction of the decoding graph and the utilization of scores for keyword paths versus filler paths. Subsequent work by Chen Guoguo et al. proposed a scheme using a deep neural network acoustic model plus a post-processing module, simplifying the training and inference process. Subsequent improvements have targeted either the acoustic model structure or the post-processing module. For customizable scenarios, there are also transformer-based keyword localization methods (no alignment required, open vocabulary) and schemes that formalize customizable KWS as a detection task. The common problem with the two-stage route, as stated plainly in the paper, is that the search process consumes significant resources, potentially limiting its use in certain application scenarios (especially low-power edge devices). In engineering terms: the decoding graph either needs to be dynamically constructed at runtime for each new keyword, or the graph itself is too large, leading to slow search speeds, making it impossible for edge devices to run a decent decoder.

The second category is end-to-end methods based on representation matching: the input is the registered keyword reference plus the speech to be detected. The basic idea is to make the representation of the keyword speech closer to the registered representation than that of non-keyword speech. No complex search is needed; a simple representation feature matching yields the decision. These are divided into two types based on the form of the registration. The classic one is query-by-example: using pre-registered audio samples as references, paired with a metric learning route. The other, currently more popular, is text registration. The paper gives two advantages for text registration: it is user-friendly (typing is more convenient than recording), and it is not affected by the acoustic environment during registration (registered audio recorded in a noisy roadside environment will have poor quality). However, pure text registration "struggles to handle speech with similar pronunciations and is prone to false wake-ups when encountering confusing keywords"—text embeddings only know how the target word should be pronounced, but do not know which other words in the audio stream sound similar to it, while false wake-ups are determined by the latter.

More critical than the system form is the deficiency in representation learning itself: previous representation learning methods almost exclusively acted on pre-segmented isolated word segments. This premise does not hold in continuous speech. The streaming working mode requires representations to be generated on continuous sliding windows, which brings huge difficulties to the effective generation of negative samples—during training, we only have aligned isolated words, but at inference, we face various misaligned cuts and cross-word spliced "dirty segments." Coupled with co-articulation effects, the difficulty of representation learning is further amplified. Furthermore, training criteria that only perform audio-text matching while ignoring audio discrimination lead to a significant increase in false alarms in continuous speech. This is the most accurate gap identified by this paper: it is not that the search is not fast enough, nor that the matching is not accurate enough, but that there is a missing supervision signal in the training criteria.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

In summary, this paper aims to simultaneously address four things. First, keywords are unknown during training, and it is impossible to collect specific word data on the scale of fixed KWS. How to extract sufficient training signals from general automatic recognition data is the prerequisite for everything. Second, the forms of negative samples in continuous speech are far richer than in isolated words (misaligned sliding windows, cross-word splicing, co-articulation penetration). Pure audio-text matching criteria cannot cover this, requiring the introduction of audio-audio discrimination. However, there is no existing answer to how to organize positive and negative sample pairs at the sliding-window level and unify both criteria into a single loss function. Third, among the family of contrastive learning losses (Triplet, SoftTriple, InfoNCE), which one is applicable and how to use it in the continuous speech sliding-window scenario has no previous conclusion. Fourth, when replacing two-stage search with representation matching, performance must not drop, and speed must see substantial improvement; otherwise, the end-to-end route has no practical value.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system (Figure 1 in the paper) is divided into three parts. The first part is the frame-level acoustic model: a five-layer deep feedforward sequence memory network with 512-dimensional hidden layers and 128-dimensional projections, with a left context of 10 frames and a right context of 1 frame for memory blocks. The modeling unit is the senone, with frame-level training targets generated by forced alignment and supervised by the cross-entropy criterion. The second part consists of two encoders of identical structure: the acoustic encoder and the text encoder, each being a three-layer 128-dimensional bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, with each layer having a 64-dimensional projection. The third part is pooling and mapping: the frame-level sequence output from the last layer of the bidirectional network is weighted-summed by a weight prediction layer (mapping the 128-dimensional vector to a scalar). The weighted-sum vectors from both the acoustic and semantic sides are then passed through two independent 128-dimensional fully connected layers, projected into the contrastive embedding space, and finally scored using cosine similarity. The total number of parameters in the entire system is approximately 2.2 million, which the paper considers suitable for edge-side keyword detection tasks.

The "whys" behind these design choices are worth expanding on one by one. First, the acoustic model is frozen during the contrastive learning phase. The authors found in preliminary experiments that the performance difference between freezing and not freezing the acoustic model during the training of the contrastive part was very small, so they chose to freeze it, letting it only provide high-level representations. This fits the logic of division of labor: frame-level phoneme classification and segment-level semantic contrast are tasks at two different granularities. The supervision signal for the former (forced alignment labels) is very abundant on general recognition data. By learning the frame-level representations well first and then freezing them, the contrastive phase can focus on adjusting the geometric structure of the embedding space without bearing the optimization burden of back-propagating through the entire acoustic model. Second, choosing a deep feedforward sequence memory network as the acoustic model and keeping only 1 frame of right context is a mature configuration in streaming speech recognition—feedforward memory blocks replace recurrent structures to model temporal dependencies, and a very small right context facilitates low latency; the paper does not further discuss the reasons for these values. Third, pooling uses learnable weighted summation rather than simple averaging: the output of the bidirectional network is a variable-length frame sequence, which must be compressed into a fixed-length vector to calculate cosine similarity with the text embedding. The weight prediction layer allows the model to learn "which frames are important for discrimination" (phoneme core segments are usually more important than transition segments), preserving more discriminative information than uniform averaging. Fourth, using two independent fully connected layers for the acoustic and text sides rather than sharing them: the original spaces of the two modalities are asymmetric. Performing a modality-specific transformation before entering the shared contrastive space is standard practice in dual-tower contrastive learning, preventing one side from dominating the optimization direction.

Training configuration: Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer, initial learning rate of 5e-5, batch size of 12288 frames; the learning rate is halved if the validation set loss does not decrease, and training stops if it does not decrease for three consecutive rounds. The learning rate magnitude is very small, consistent with the setting of a frozen backbone—only the encoders and projection layers are trainable. Small-step fine-tuning of the embedding space is sufficient; a large learning rate would instead destroy the pre-trained acoustic representations.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Sliding Window Length Estimation (Equation 1)**: The segment length equals the average phoneme duration multiplied by the number of phonemes in the keyword, plus a margin. The number of phonemes comes from the phonemization result of the registered text, and the average phoneme duration is based on statistics from the training set—estimated at 90 milliseconds per phoneme on LibriSpeech. The margin is taken as 300 milliseconds, approximately enough to accommodate three to four additional pronunciation units. The paper gives two reasons for adding the margin: first, to accommodate words with pronunciation durations exceeding the average (speakers with slower speech rates), and second, to include the context adjacent to the keyword in the segment, allowing the model to see context-bearing segments during training, thereby explicitly considering co-articulation effects and learning to discriminate confusing content. This formula serves both training and inference—the paper specifically emphasizes using the same estimation function to cut windows for any keyword during inference, avoiding segment length mismatch between training and inference. This is a detail easily overlooked in streaming systems: if training uses true alignment to cut golden lengths and inference uses estimated lengths, the embedding distribution will exhibit systematic shifts.

**Audio-Text Matching Loss (Equation 2)**: During training, in each round, any word is randomly sampled from each sentence to serve as the "keyword for this round." Let the $j$-th sampled text keyword in the $i$-th sentence be $W_{(i,j)}$, and its corresponding $N$ positive audio segments and $M$ negative audio segments be denoted as superscripts positive and negative $A_{(i,j)}$. When constructing audio-text pairs, only positive audio segments are used: a certain positive segment forms a positive pair with its corresponding text keyword; all other text keywords in the batch form negative pairs with this positive segment, and negative audio segments do not enter this loss. The loss is in the form of InfoNCE: using the audio segment as the anchor, the numerator is the exponential similarity with the correct text, and the denominator accumulates the exponential similarity with all other text keywords in the batch. The temperature parameter is set to 0.12, and the negative log-likelihood of all audio-text positive pairs in the batch is summed. Intuitively, this is a softmax cross-entropy over "all candidate words in the batch": pulling the correct pair closer and pushing away the text embeddings of all other words. Negative examples in the batch are all free, coming from other sampled words, without the need for special construction. This is a structural advantage of InfoNCE relative to triplet loss.

**Audio-Audio Discrimination Loss (Equation 3)**: Positive pairs are two different positive audio segments of the same keyword, and negative pairs are a positive segment and a negative segment. Since each sampled keyword has $N$ positive segments, the number of positive pairs grows quadratically with the combination number, and the total number of audio pairs is huge. The loss is also InfoNCE: using a certain positive segment as the anchor, the numerator is the exponential similarity with another positive segment of the same word, and the denominator accumulates the similarity indices of $M$ negative segments. The temperature is set to 0.2. The semantics of this term are: different pronunciation variants of the same word (different speakers, different cut positions, different contexts) should cluster together, while being pushed away from neighboring segments that sound similar but have different content. This is a supervision signal that pure text matching cannot provide—text embeddings only know what the target word is, but do not know which segments in the audio stream "look like" it.

**Total Loss (Equation 4)**: The audio-audio term is multiplied by a weight of 0.15 and added to the audio-text term. The weight of the discrimination term is only 15% of the matching term, indicating that the authors position audio-audio discrimination as an auxiliary regularization: the dominant goal is still cross-modal alignment, and the discrimination term is responsible for pushing apart the most confusing corners in the embedding space. The three hyperparameters (temperatures 0.12, 0.2, and weight 0.15) are empirical values. The paper's original words are "empirically found these values to work well," and no hyperparameter search process is reported. The fact that the two temperatures are different is also worth noting: audio-audio pairs belong to the same speech modality, so their baseline similarity is naturally higher and the distribution is more concentrated. Using a larger temperature (0.2 vs 0.12) prevents gradients from being overly concentrated on the hardest negative pairs. Cross-modal pairs have a more dispersed distribution, and a small temperature can compress the clusters tighter. This explanation is an inference by the author based on common practices in contrastive learning; the paper does not elaborate on it.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Audio Discrimination Contrastive Learning (CLAD) and Confusing Segment Data Augmentation

The core of CLAD is not inventing a new loss, but extending the contrastive criterion from a single channel of "audio to text" to a dual channel of "audio to text plus audio to audio," accompanied by a data augmentation scheme capable of mass-producing confusing negative examples. The segmentation of positive and negative segments is based on the overlap rate: the overlap rate between the sliding window estimated by Equation 1 and the true keyword position given by forced alignment is calculated. Segments meeting the overlap threshold are cut as positive segments, and neighboring segments that do not meet the threshold are cut as negative segments. The negative segments cut out in this way naturally possess the properties of two types of "hardest negative examples": one type is misaligned sliding windows (the window is off by half a beat, covering only part of the keyword), and the other type is cross-word splicing (the window covers the tail of the previous word plus the head of the keyword). These two types are exactly the most common sources of false alarms in streaming inference. The paper explicitly gives examples in the methodology section: certain segments may contain part of the previous word plus part of the wake word, and audio-text contrastive learning cannot distinguish such difficult negative examples on its own.

Why can audio-audio discrimination solve problems that text matching cannot? A mechanistic analysis can be performed. The supervision signal of text-audio matching only constrains "whether this audio segment resembles the text representation of this word," implicitly assuming that "resembling the target word" is the only important direction in the embedding space. However, false alarms are caused by ambiguity within the audio space itself—two segments with different texts are acoustically close. To suppress such segments at inference time, there must be direct supervision of "these two audio segments are of different classes" during training, and this can only come from audio-audio negative pairs. Ablation experiments (Figure 2) verify this judgment: removing audio discrimination leads to a performance drop described by the paper as "drastic." An附带 benefit is the construction of positive pairs: different positive segments of the same word serve as positive examples for each other, effectively clustering "multiple acoustic realizations of the same text" (variations in speaker, speech rate, context), which also benefits cross-speaker generalization—this explains why this design yields greater benefits in more difficult test scenarios (test-other).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Sliding-Window InfoNCE

The second claim is to replace the contrastive loss itself with InfoNCE and organize training in units of sliding-window segments (rather than isolated words). The reasons are clearly written in the paper: triplet loss looks at only one anchor, one positive example, and one negative example at a time. Although SoftTriple softens the requirements for cluster structure, both struggle to accommodate rich positive and negative sample usage. InfoNCE is naturally a denominator stacking form of "one anchor against multiple positives and multiple negatives," making it particularly suitable for simultaneously incorporating both audio-text and audio-audio criteria in continuous speech. The data in Table 1 supports this choice: in the isolated word scenario, the Equal Error Rate (EER) for Triplet on the easy and hard negative sets is 32.75% and 44.36%, respectively; for SoftTriple, it is 28.74% and 41.95%; while for InfoNCE, it is 8.99% and 32.51%—the gap is nearly an order of magnitude, indicating that the form of the loss function itself (rather than data scale) is one of the bottlenecks of previous representation learning methods. The authors also note that even in the context-independent isolated scenario, adding audio discrimination on top of InfoNCE still yields some gains (compare rows 4 and 5 in Table 1), meaning the contributions of the dual criteria and the loss form are orthogonal and additive.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Search-Free End-to-End Inference Protocol

Inference process (Section 2.3 of the paper): First, estimate the segment length corresponding to the keyword using Equation 1, and slice the test audio track into segments with half-overlap between adjacent segments. Then, perform forward passes segment by segment, continuously calculating the similarity between the segment embedding and the registered text embedding. The highest similarity in the sliding window sequence is identified as the keyword score. If the score exceeds a threshold, a wake-up is determined, and the model state is reset with a one-second cooldown period to prevent repeated wake-ups for the same keyword. Every detail of this protocol has engineering motivation: half-overlap is a trade-off between missed detections and computational load; a one-half overlap between adjacent segments means that any word boundary will be covered relatively completely by at least one segment. Taking the highest score within the sliding window corresponds to the task definition that "the keyword must be caught wherever it appears in the stream." The one-second cooldown is because after the keyword is spoken, the subsequent few sliding windows still highly overlap with the keyword, and the scores remain high; without cooling, a single wake-up would trigger multiple reports. Resetting the model state clears the history after the decision to avoid residual effects of the same event on subsequent windows. The essential difference from two-stage methods is the complete absence of a search module—the graph search baseline needs to perform path expansion on the decoding graph, and the posterior processing baseline needs to perform posterior smoothing and decision-making, whereas here the judgment cost for each segment is just one forward pass plus one cosine similarity calculation.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to audio-text consistency learning methods (the proposal scheme of LibriPhrase, with no contrastive learning marker in Table 1): It has only one cross-modal matching criterion. CLAD adds an intra-modal discrimination term and sliding-window sample organization on top of it, reducing the EER on the hard negative set from 32.90% to 30.30% and increasing the Area Under the Curve (AUC) from 73.58% to 76.15%. Compared to the Triplet and SoftTriple routes: It is not just changing the loss function, but moving the training unit from isolated segments to continuous speech sliding windows, completely rewriting the sample generation logic. Compared to the query-by-example route: The registration object changes from audio to text, making user experience and registration quality more stable. Compared to two-stage customizable methods (transformer localization, text-adaptive detection): The search module is removed. Table 3 shows that the relative inference speedup reaches 16.95 times (the graph search baseline is defined as 1.00x, and the posterior processing baseline is only 0.38x, i.e., slower than the graph search; the paper does not explain the reason). Compared to the group's previous text-adaptive detection work, this paper takes a pure representation matching route, simplifying "detection" to "similarity compared to a threshold."

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

- **LibriSpeech Keyword Detection Version (Continuous Speech Scenario)**: Training uses the full 960 hours of LibriSpeech. Testing follows the construction strategy existing in the literature, selecting 5 to 50 of the most common words, each containing at least six phonemes, from test-clean and test-other as keywords. All audio not containing keywords is divided into corresponding false alarm datasets, each about 3 hours long. The requirement for "at least six phonemes" ensures that keywords have sufficient length and the task cannot be cheated by one or two phonemes. The gradient design of 5 to 50 words is intended to examine two capabilities separately—when the number of words is small, results reflect more on the modeling capability for high-frequency words, and when the number of words is large (e.g., 40, 50), results reflect more on the generalization capability for any specified keyword. Section 4.2 of the paper explicitly makes this interpretation and points out that both aspects are equally important for customizable systems: good results on many keywords give users the ability to choose any word, and good results on high-frequency words give space for continued custom optimization after the keyword is determined.
- **LibriPhrase (Isolated Word Scenario)**: Also derived from LibriSpeech, constructed to evaluate isolated word detection based on audio-text representation learning. The training set comes from 460 hours of train-clean-460, and the test set comes from train-others-500. Testing is divided into two levels: easy negatives and hard negatives. Each round contains 3 positive audio-text pairs and 3 negative pairs. For fair comparison, CLAD is retrained on the 460-hour clean training set for this group of experiments.

This combination of evaluations preserves a performance lower bound (isolated scenario and direct comparability with predecessors) and verifies the main battlefield (continuous speech). This is a highlight of the paper's experimental logic: first proving that the new method has not lost the ability in the old scenario, and then proving that old methods indeed fail in the new scenario.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Equal Error Rate (EER) and Area Under the Curve (AUC) (Isolated Scenario)**: Standard metrics following the LibriPhrase protocol. EER is the error rate when the false alarm rate equals the miss rate. AUC is the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. The easy and hard negative levels examine discriminative power under ordinary negatives and phonetically similar negatives, respectively. The hard negative level directly corresponds to the core pain point of "confusing keyword false wake-ups."
- **Micro-averaged Recall (Main Metric for Continuous Scenario)**: Given the number of keywords (5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50), the number of false alarms is fixed at 2 on the corresponding false alarm dataset of about 3 hours, and the micro-averaged recall is reported. The reason for choosing this metric is that it aligns directly with product logic: real devices are extremely sensitive to false alarms, so performance must be compared within a false alarm budget, otherwise recall is meaningless.
- **Relative Speedup (Speed Metric)**: Defined as the ratio of the execution time of the baseline method to the execution time of the model under test. Higher is faster. Using relative values avoids specific hardware differences, but also brings the problem of unknown absolute speed (see Limitations).

Three conclusions. First, InfoNCE is a crushing improvement over Triplet and SoftTriple (EER for easy negatives: 8.99% vs 32.75% and 28.74%), proving that the form of the loss function is very critical in audio-text representation learning, and half of the previous bottleneck was in the loss, not the data. Third, CLAD is designed for continuous speech, and not losing to mainstream methods in the isolated scenario is itself a side-effect robustness proof.

The interpretation needs to be layered. On test-clean with a small number of keywords (5, 10, 15), the graph search baseline is strongest (0.943 vs 0.915, 0.948 vs 0.927, 0.958 vs 0.944). The paper admits this honestly and explains it as a scenario of "modeling high-frequency words on simple data"—high-frequency words appear many times in the training data, and explicit decoding paths can be fully refined. But the trend of significant degradation for graph search when conditions change is obvious: on test-other with 5 words, it is 0.696 vs CLAD's 0.754 (a difference of 5.8 points), and CLAD is first in all seven keyword levels on test-other. On test-clean, CLAD also completely surpasses after the number of keywords reaches 20 and above (0.925 vs 0.916, 0.914 vs 0.901, 0.913 vs 0.899, 0.918 vs 0.907). There is another trend not explicitly pointed out in the paper's text but directly readable from the table data: when the number of keywords increases from 15 to 50, the graph search drops by 5.1 points on test-clean (from 0.958 to 0.907), while CLAD drops only 2.6 points (from 0.944 to 0.918). The generalization decay of the representation matching route is significantly more gradual. The posterior processing baseline is at the bottom throughout both tests. The paper's summary is: except for the combination of "test-clean plus 5, 10, 15 words," the model achieves consistent gains from 5 to 50 words on both simple and difficult test sets. The introduction also mentions that CLAD has a lower standard deviation of results between different keywords (better stability), but the paper does not report specific standard deviation values.

**Inference Speed (Table 3, Relative Speedup)**: Graph search 1.00x (baseline), Posterior processing 0.38x, CLAD 16.95x. The speed advantage of CLAD comes directly from removing the search module—end-to-end representation matching requires only one forward pass plus cosine similarity per segment, resulting in a relative speedup of about 17 times compared to graph search and about 45 times compared to posterior processing, giving this route a clear reason for deployment on low-power edge devices.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The core switch in the ablation is the presence or absence of the audio discrimination term: removing the audio-audio term (leaving only audio-text contrastive learning) results in four curves presented in Figure 2—the complete version of "audio-audio plus text-text" for test-clean and test-other, respectively, versus the "text-text only" version. The x-axis is 5 to 50 keywords, and the y-axis is micro-averaged recall from 0.5 to 0.9. The text describes the performance drop as "drastic": regardless of the test set or the number of keywords, the curve with only audio-text is significantly lower than the complete CLAD curve, with the gap being especially large on test-other. The paper attributes the cause to the audio-audio pairs introducing discriminative information to distinguish "locally similar negative examples," thereby significantly improving performance. Specific values for each keyword level are not given in table form by the paper; only the trend can be read from Figure 2.

Three other small-scale ablations are scattered in the methodology and implementation details: the performance difference between freezing and not freezing the acoustic model is very small (preliminary experiment, so freezing was chosen to save compute); the three hyperparameters (temperatures 0.12 and 0.2, weight 0.15) were selected empirically, and the paper does not report sensitivity analysis; the reason for the margin of 300 milliseconds is to accommodate about three or four additional pronunciation units, and the paper does not report comparisons for different margin values. These are parameter spaces that replicators need to sweep themselves.

## Main Contributions

1. **Advanced contrastive learning for customizable keyword detection from a single criterion to a dual criterion.** All previous contrastive learning methods only performed audio-text matching. CLAD introduces audio-audio discrimination for the first time, directly targeting the root cause of false alarms in continuous speech: "different texts producing similar audio patterns." The accompanying data augmentation scheme, which cuts segments based on overlap rate, turns the two hardest negative examples—misaligned sliding windows and cross-word splicing—into trainable resources that can be mass-produced. Ablation (Figure 2) proves this is not just icing on the cake—removing it causes a drastic performance drop.
2. **Established the status of sliding-window InfoNCE in this task.** Using the data in Table 1, the gap between Triplet, SoftTriple, and InfoNCE is placed at nearly an order of magnitude (EER for easy negatives: 8.99% vs 32.75% and 28.74%), indicating that a loss form capable of "accommodating rich positive and negative samples" is a prerequisite for contrastive learning in continuous speech. This conclusion has direct reference value for subsequent work on streaming representation learning.
3. **Provided a complete, runnable end-to-end continuous speech customizable detection system.** From the segment length estimation formula, half-overlapping sliding windows, highest-score judgment, to the one-second cooldown wake-up protocol, a search-free complete chain is formed. The parameter count is about 2.2 million, suitable for edge devices. Compared to two-stage baselines (with the same acoustic model), it dominates comprehensively except in a few high-frequency word scenarios on test-clean, and achieves a relative inference speedup of 16.95 times (Table 3).
4. **Contributions in evaluation methodology.** Constructed a continuous evaluation protocol for the LibriSpeech keyword detection version (960 hours of training, gradient of 5 to 50 words, false alarm set of about 3 hours, micro-averaged recall under a two-false-alarm constraint), and used isolated plus continuous dual-scenario cross-validation. The separation interpretation in Section 4.2 of "modeling capability for high-frequency words vs. generalization capability for any word" is of reference significance for later researchers designing customizable detection evaluations.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Tension between bidirectionality and streaming nature of the encoder.** Both the acoustic and text encoders are bidirectional networks, and the segments are bidirectional. Inference proceeds forward in units of sliding-window segments. Although the segments progress in a streaming manner, each segment must wait for its right endpoint data to be complete before calculation. Since the segment length itself grows linearly with the number of phonemes in the keyword (Equation 1), the judgment latency for long keywords will increase accordingly. The paper does not discuss end-to-end latency budgets, nor does it report attempts at unidirectional or causal alternatives.
- **Half-overlapping sliding windows bring computational redundancy.** A one-half overlap between adjacent segments means that about half of the frames are encoded repeatedly. This is a significant item for optimization in edge scenarios sensitive to compute power. The overlap rate of one-half was also not validated by ablation, and the paper does not report results for other overlap rates.
- **Insufficient disclosure of details on the text side.** Segment length estimation relies on the number of phonemes, indicating that registered text must undergo phonemization. However, the specific input form of the text encoder (phoneme sequence or character sequence), the phonemization tool used, and how misspelled words are handled are not detailed in the paper. Reproducing this for cross-lingual scenarios (e.g., Chinese pinyin and tones) requires filling in these links oneself.
- **Still loses to graph search in a small number of high-frequency words on test-clean** (Table 2: 5, 10, 15 words are 0.915, 0.927, 0.944 vs 0.943, 0.948, 0.958). The representation matching route's extreme modeling capability for high-frequency words is inferior to explicit decoding paths. This implies that two-stage methods still have advantages in product forms where "keywords are fixed and high-frequency." CLAD's home ground is "any word, any position, complex acoustics."
- **Hyperparameters are empirical and bound to a single data domain.** Temperatures 0.12 and 0.2, discrimination weight 0.15, and margin 300 milliseconds were all selected empirically on LibriSpeech with no sensitivity analysis. Whether this remains the optimal combination when switched to far-field, conversational speech, or other languages is not evidenced by the paper.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Single data domain.** All experiments were conducted on the LibriSpeech series (read audiobooks). test-other is merely noisy reading, not real far-field, multi-person conversation, TV noise, echo, or other actual acoustic conditions in smart homes. There is also no validation on tonal languages like Chinese. The text registration route is sensitive to pronunciation conversion quality, and tones are an additional important variable.
- **Limited sample size for false alarm statistics.** The false alarm datasets are each about 3 hours long, constrained to two false alarms. Micro-averaged recall naturally has high variance under such a small false alarm budget. Although the introduction claims a lower standard deviation between different keywords, no specific standard deviation value table is given, and the stability claim lacks directly verifiable numbers.
- **Lack of absolute efficiency data.** The relative speedup only gives multiples (16.95x), without reporting absolute latency, real-time factor, power consumption, or actual measurements on real embedded hardware. The model parameter count is about 2.2 million, but the floating-point operation count and memory usage are not reported. The decoding graph size of the two two-stage baselines and the specific implementation of posterior processing are also not disclosed. The fairness of the speed comparison can only rely on the "same acoustic model" point for endorsement.
- **Narrow coverage of ablation.** Only the switch of the audio discrimination term was verified (Figure 2). The impact of the discrimination weight magnitude, the interaction of the two temperatures, the impact of the ratio of positive/negative segment counts to M, and the combination of margin and overlap rate were not dismantled. The attribution of gains in the isolated scenario (whether it is the contribution of the audio-audio term itself or the contribution of the sliding-window sample organization method) was also not verified separately.
- **Lack of direct comparison with strong contemporaneous baselines.** The comparison objects are the audio-text consistency method, Triplet, SoftTriple, and two-stage baselines. Near methods also doing text registration (e.g., phoneme-guided zero-shot schemes, matching latent encoding schemes) are only mentioned as citations in the motivation section and do not enter the experimental tables. Readers cannot judge the incremental value of the dual-criterion design relative to the latest similar work.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **True far-field and multi-lingual evaluation.** Adding far-field simulation or recorded augmentation to the training data and validating on real home noise and tonal languages is a necessary step from "paper-available" to "product-available." Text encoding and pronunciation conversion need to adapt to tonal information, which itself is a publishable problem.
- **Streaming and low-latency transformation.** Replacing bidirectional encoders with unidirectional recurrent or causal attention, performing incremental encoding within segments to eliminate half-overlap redundancy, and cooperating with smaller margins can suppress judgment latency while maintaining recall. These changes are orthogonal to CLAD's training criteria and can be directly superimposed.
- **Automation and curriculum of negative example mining.** Current negative segments are cut by overlap rate rules. Online hard negative example mining (using the current model to pick the most similar negative segments for retraining) can be further implemented, or the weight of the audio-audio term can be arranged in a curriculum manner by difficulty, turning the fixed 0.15 into a schedule that dynamically adjusts with the training process.
- **Cascading with two-stage methods.** Table 2 shows that graph search still has advantages on clean data with high-frequency words. A natural combination is to use end-to-end matching for the first stage of high-recall screening and lightweight decoding for the second stage of confirmation—exchanging the 16.95x speed of representation matching for a balance of missed detections and false alarms in the overall system. This is likely the most pragmatic deployment form in engineering.
- **Registration-side enhancement and interpretability.** Research the geometric structure of "phonetically similar clusters" in the embedding space, feed back a list of confusing words to the user (prompting the user during registration that the selected word is easily confused with certain high-frequency words), or use speech synthesis to generate multi-speaker audio for augmentation during registration—the latter is equivalent to mixing audio registration and text registration, taking the best of both.
- **Extension to concurrent multi-keyword and out-of-vocabulary rejection.** Actual products often hang multiple wake words simultaneously or need to distinguish "sounds like but is not" utterances. CLAD's audio-audio discrimination can naturally be transformed into intra-keyword-set mutual exclusion constraints (pushing the embeddings of each word in the set away from each other). This is a potential not yet realized by its dual-criterion design and is also the direction of competition with subsequent multi-task schemes.
