# Adversarial Detection in Speech: A Classification Approach

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Saeid Samizade, Zheng-Hua Tan, Chao Shen, Xiaohong Guan (Aalborg University, Xi'an Jiaotong University)
- **Date**: October 2019 (arXiv)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10013
- **Keywords**: Adversarial Attack Detection, Speech, DeepSpeech, Keyword Spotting, Classification, Black-box, White-box, C&W Attack, Genetic Algorithm Attack

## Problem Statement

Deep neural networks have achieved significant success in speech processing tasks, but they have been proven vulnerable to **Adversarial Attacks**—adding imperceptible perturbations to the original audio signal can cause neural networks to produce completely incorrect outputs. This security vulnerability poses a serious threat to the practical deployment of KWS and ASR systems:

1. **Security Risks of KWS Systems**: Attackers can bypass wake-word detection or trigger false awakenings using adversarial audio, potentially leading to unauthorized device access or privacy leaks.
2. **Security Risks of ASR Systems**: Adversarial audio can cause speech recognition systems to produce arbitrary transcription results specified by the attacker, such as injecting malicious voice commands into smart speakers.
3. **Diversity of Attack Types**: Adversarial attacks can be categorized into **White-box Attacks** (where the attacker has complete knowledge of the target model's architecture and parameters) and **Black-box Attacks** (where the attacker has no internal information about the target model). The characteristics of different attack types vary significantly.
4. **Insufficiency of Existing Defense Mechanisms**: Traditional defense methods, such as adversarial training, are computationally expensive and require knowledge of the attack methods. Input preprocessing (e.g., compression, quantization) is ineffective against certain attacks. Therefore, a general, attack-method-agnostic **detection** mechanism is needed.

The core challenge is: How to design a reliable method to detect adversarial examples in speech signals that can simultaneously handle multiple attack methods under both white-box and black-box threat models?

## Methodology

This paper formalizes adversarial detection in speech as a **binary classification problem**—training an independent CNN detector to distinguish between clean speech and adversarial speech.

### 1. Attack Methods

#### 1.1 White-box Attack: C&W Attack (Based on DeepSpeech ASR)

In a white-box setting, the attacker has full knowledge of the target model (DeepSpeech ASR). The Carlini & Wagner (C&W) attack method is used to generate adversarial examples:

- **Attack Objective**: Minimize perturbation magnitude to cause DeepSpeech to produce a target transcription (targeted attack)
- **Optimization Objective**: $\min_\delta \|\delta\|_2 + c \cdot \mathcal{L}_{C\&W}(f(x + \delta), t)$
- Where $\delta$ is the adversarial perturbation, $f$ is the DeepSpeech model, $t$ is the target transcription, and $\mathcal{L}_{C\&W}$ is the C&W loss function
- **Characteristics**: The perturbation magnitude is extremely small and imperceptible to the human ear, yet it fundamentally alters the output of the ASR system

#### 1.2 Black-box Attack: Alzantot Genetic Algorithm Attack (Based on KWS Model)

In a black-box setting, the attacker can only obtain output labels from the target KWS model via queries (no gradient information). The genetic algorithm-based attack proposed by Alzantot et al. is used:

- **Attack Strategy**: Model the generation of adversarial perturbations as an evolutionary optimization problem
- **Population Initialization**: Randomly generate an initial population of perturbations
- **Fitness Function**: Evaluate the attack effectiveness of perturbations based on the target model's classification probabilities
- **Evolutionary Operations**: Iteratively optimize perturbations through selection, crossover, and mutation operations
- **Characteristics**: Does not require gradient information from the target model; only a query interface is needed to execute the attack

### 2. Detector Architecture

The detector adopts a CNN architecture, specifically designed to distinguish between clean and adversarial samples from speech features:

- **Input Features**: 40-dimensional MFCC features ($f=40$), extracted from the audio signal
- **Network Structure**:
  - 3 convolutional layers with filter counts of 64, 64, and 32 respectively
  - Kernel size: 2x2
  - Activation function: ReLU
  - Pooling operations
- **Output**: Softmax probabilities for binary classification (clean/adversarial)
- **Training Objective**: Standard binary classification cross-entropy loss

### 3. Experimental Datasets

Two sets of datasets were constructed in this paper for evaluation:

- **Dataset A (White-box Attack)**:
  - 900 clean samples + 900 adversarial samples (generated by C&W attack on DeepSpeech)
  - Adversarial samples and clean samples have the same speech content but different perturbations

- **Dataset B (Black-box Attack)**:
  - 1800 clean samples + 1800 adversarial samples (generated by Alzantot genetic algorithm attack on KWS model)
  - The scale is twice that of the white-box dataset, covering more diverse adversarial samples

### 4. Evaluation Settings

- **Matched**: Training and testing sets use the same type of attack
- **Multi-condition**: Training set contains multiple attack types; testing set contains unseen attacks
- **Cross-attack Transfer**: Train with one type of attack, test on another type of attack

## Main Contributions

1. **Unified Adversarial Detection Framework**: For the first time, systematically formalizes adversarial detection in speech as a binary classification problem, providing a general detection framework that can simultaneously handle white-box and black-box attack scenarios.

2. **Comprehensive Threat Model Evaluation**: Evaluates the detection method under two fundamentally different attack paradigms—white-box C&W attack (gradient-based optimization targeting ASR systems) and black-box genetic algorithm attack (query-based optimization targeting KWS systems), covering major practical threat scenarios.

3. **Cross-attack Transferability Analysis**: Provides detailed cross-attack transfer experiments—whether a detector trained on white-box attack data can detect black-box attacks (and vice versa)—revealing whether adversarial examples share common features across different attack methods.

4. **High Detection Accuracy**: Achieves over 96-98% detection accuracy in both matched and multi-condition settings, proving that adversarial speech samples can be reliably detected.

## Experimental Results

### Matched Condition Detection Results

| Training/Testing Setup | Detection Accuracy |
|--------------|-----------|
| White-box Matched (Dataset A training and testing) | **>98%** |
| Black-box Matched (Dataset B training and testing) | **>98%** |

Under matched conditions, the detector can identify adversarial examples with high precision.

### Multi-condition Detection Results

| Training/Testing Setup | Detection Accuracy |
|--------------|-----------|
| Multi-condition Training (Mixed Attack Types) | **>96%** |

Detectors trained with mixed attack types maintain high detection rates across various attacks.

### Cross-attack Transfer Results

| Training Data | Testing Data | Detection Accuracy |
|---------|---------|-----------|
| White-box Attack A | Black-box Attack B | **82.07%** |
| Black-box Attack B | White-box Attack A | **48.76%** |

Key Findings: Transfer from white-box to black-box (82.07%) is significantly better than from black-box to white-box (48.76%). This indicates:
- Adversarial perturbations generated by C&W white-box attacks have more distinct statistical features, allowing the trained detector to possess certain generalization capabilities
- Adversarial perturbations generated by genetic algorithm black-box attacks differ significantly from white-box attacks; detectors trained on black-box attacks struggle to effectively detect white-box attacks
- Adversarial examples generated by different attack methods exhibit significant differences in feature space, indicating that general detectors require further research

### Key Observations

1. Adversarial examples leave detectable "traces" in the MFCC feature space—even imperceptible perturbations to the human ear produce systematic biases in spectral features
2. The CNN detector can effectively capture these biases from MFCC features
3. The detector's transferability across different attack types is limited, suggesting that optimal defense strategies should include training data from multiple attack types

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations
- **Vulnerability to Adaptive Attacks**: When attackers are aware of the detector's existence, they can design adaptive attacks to bypass the detector (e.g., incorporating a differentiable approximation of the detector into the optimization objective of the C&W attack). This is a fundamental challenge for all classification-based adversarial detection methods.
- **Additional System Overhead**: Deploying an independent detector model increases the system's computational complexity and inference latency. For systems like KWS that need to run continuously, the additional model may significantly increase energy consumption.
- **Limited Coverage of Attack Types**: Only C&W white-box attacks and Alzantot genetic algorithm black-box attacks were evaluated; the detection capability for other attack methods (such as PGD, FGSM, physical world attacks) was not verified.

### Experimental Design Limitations
- The cross-attack transfer results (48.76% accuracy) indicate insufficient generalization of the current method; detectors trained solely on a single attack type may have limited effectiveness in practical deployment.
- Lack of systematic analysis of the detector's robustness—how variations in adversarial perturbation strength affect detection accuracy.
- The dataset scale is relatively small (900-1800 samples); performance on large-scale data remains to be verified.

### Future Directions
- Research hybrid defense strategies combining adversarial training and detection, enhancing model robustness while maintaining detection capabilities.
- Explore statistical test-based adversarial detection methods (such as Mahalanobis distance, kernel density estimation), which may offer better cross-attack transferability.
- Investigate the possibility of performing adversarial detection in feature space (rather than raw audio space), leveraging feature representations at different levels to improve detection robustness.
- Extend detection methods to online/streaming scenarios, enabling frame-by-frame detection of adversarial examples in real-time audio streams.
- Research detection methods for physical world adversarial attacks (adversarial audio propagated through air), which are more critical for practical deployment scenarios.
