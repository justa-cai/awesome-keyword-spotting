## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Voice User Interfaces (VUIs) have been deployed on a large scale, and the Wake-Up Word (WUW) is the most frequent action users take to trigger this interface: uttering a short command activates the system, which then receives the full instruction. The paper’s argument starts from an observation long ignored from an engineering perspective—when users speak to a voice assistant, they naturally use a manner of speaking akin to "conversing with a person" (the paper cites research on voice branding [13] to support this), and when humans speak to each other, emotional information is naturally carried through the speech channel. In other words, a wake-up word is never just a "power-on command"; it simultaneously carries three types of non-lexical information: speaker identity, the speaker's emotional state, and paralinguistic cues regarding the manner of speaking. Existing wake-up word research has focused its efforts on the first two engineering goals: making the detection itself accurate and fast [9][10], and rejecting impostors and non-registered users through voiceprint verification [11][12]. The dimension of emotion has hardly been systematically studied, not because it is unimportant, but because there is no data.

The chain of motivational evidence provided by the paper is worth expanding upon, as it explains "why specifically emotion recognition for wake-up words":

First, the product appeal of VUIs is inherently tied to emotional communication. Research cited in the paper points out that the appeal of VUIs partly stems from its invisible interaction form [4], and users' expectations for emotional communication are higher than for traditional interface types [5]. A voice interface that only "understands words" but "does not understand emotions" is an incomplete product under such expectations.

Second, the in-vehicle scenario pushes this demand the hardest, and Hi,KIA is explicitly anchored to the in-vehicle context, from its naming (participation by Kia Design Center) to its scenario design (all are in-car scenarios). The paper gives three reasons: in-vehicle VUIs improve driving safety by replacing manual operations [6][7]; in-vehicle VUIs can enrich the driver's emotional experience [8]; and Nass et al.'s classic 2005 field study—pairing the car's voice emotion with the driver's emotion can improve driving safety [5]. To achieve any form of emotion-adaptive in-vehicle interaction, the first step is to be able to read emotions from the user's voice, and the first thing users say to the car system is exactly the wake-up word. If the wake-up word itself carries emotional signals, the system can complete the initialization of the emotional state before the user finishes the full instruction, allowing subsequent response strategies (tone, speed, wording) to be adjusted accordingly.

Third, and this is the fundamental reason for this paper's existence as a dataset paper: there is a hard constraint in engineering that has been long ignored—wake-up words are extremely short. The average utterance duration in Hi,KIA is only 0.64 seconds (Table I), whereas sentences in conventional speech emotion recognition (SER) datasets average two to four seconds. The acoustic carriers of emotion, including fluctuations in fundamental frequency trajectories, variations in energy envelopes, and textural changes in spectral details, require a certain time window to fully unfold; within a window of less than one second, long-term statistics degrade to near-instantaneous observations, and the cues available to the model are significantly compressed. This intersection of "ultra-short duration and emotion annotation" did not even have publicly available data for training and evaluation prior to this paper, let alone model research.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper clearly lays out the gap in data using Table I, which can be summarized into four dimensions:

**Mismatch in Duration and Text Distribution**. Mainstream Speech Emotion Recognition (SER) datasets—IEMOCAP [14], EmoDB [15], RAVDESS [1], TESS [2]—are all designed to be text-independent, meaning models should recognize emotions without relying on lexical content, so they record complete sentences: RAVDESS averages 4.09 seconds, TESS averages 2.06 seconds (Table I). Models trained on these data implicitly rely on sentence-level prosodic structures: the rise and fall of intonation, stress distribution, trailing tones at sentence ends, and breathing rhythms between sentences. These structures simply do not exist in 0.64-second wake-up words. A duration difference of three to six times is not a simple scaling problem; it is a difference in the set of available acoustic cues itself—this determines that sentence-level SER models cannot be directly transferred to the wake-up word emotion task.

**Wake-up Word Datasets Lack Emotion Annotations**. Industrial wake-up word data (signature words like Ok Google, Hey Siri) are targeted at detection and do not annotate emotions. The closest public data is OK Aura [3]: 1247 utterances, 80 speakers, Spanish wake-up words, average 1.52 seconds, with rich metadata including gender, room size, accent, and emotion, where emotion takes three classes: annoyed/friendly/neutral—but only 218 out of 1247 have emotion annotations (Table I), a coverage rate of about 17.5%. Using less than one-fifth of the annotated volume cannot support training nor credible evaluation.

**Language Gap**. The accent column in Table I is straightforward: North American English (RAVDESS, TESS), Spanish (OK Aura), and German (EmoDB), with Korean absent. The acoustic expression of emotion has language specificity (pitch systems, duration systems, linguistic etiquette constraints on emotional expression), making direct cross-lingual transfer unreliable.

**Contradiction between Small Data and Deep Methods**. Recent progress in SER relies on deep learning and larger annotation volumes [23], and the paper explicitly points out that insufficient annotated data has always limited such methods. Wake-up word emotion data is naturally difficult to scale up: it requires professional voice actors performing, manual verification for each utterance, and high costs. This technical route has never been systematically validated on WUW—on the order of 488 utterances, how much better transfer learning from pre-training is compared to hand-crafted features, and how many parameters are appropriate for fine-tuning, are all unresolved issues.

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

The paper actually aims to solve three things. First, how to induce diverse and natural short-voice emotions within controlled costs: directly asking actors to "imagine an emotion and then read the word" would limit subtle variations within the same emotion (as stated in the paper), requiring a systematic scenario design method, and this method must expose gaps in the coverage of the emotion space. Second, how to ensure annotation quality: emotion labels are highly subjective, and human perception of emotion on ultra-short speech is itself an unknown quantity, requiring human verification and filtering mechanisms, while simultaneously answering "how many emotions can humans actually recognize on 0.64 seconds"—this number will become the benchmark for interpreting all subsequent model results. Third, how to provide a credible baseline on 488 small samples: speaker-independent cross-validation, plus two technical routes of "hand-crafted features vs. pre-trained transfer," to establish a comparable benchmark for future research.

## Methodology

This is a dataset paper, and the methodology is organized along three lines: "acquisition protocol, annotation design, baseline models."

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The construction of Hi,KIA is a three-stage pipeline: scenario selection, recording and post-processing, and human verification, with the entire process completed online due to the pandemic. The design philosophy of this pipeline is to address the most unstable link—"where emotions come from"—at the front end: rather than using annotations later to distinguish the authenticity of emotions, it is better to ensure the diversity and naturalness of emotions at the acquisition end using scenario scripts. Each of the three stages corresponds to a quality control goal—scenario selection controls emotion coverage (using the circumplex model of emotion to ensure structurally distinguishable structures between categories acoustically); recording protocol controls acoustic condition consistency (online tutorials, fixed mouth-to-microphone distance, re-recording after first-round feedback); human verification controls label credibility (a conservative criterion where only samples rejected by all evaluators are removed). The design goal is a text-dependent (fixed wake-up word "Hi, KIA") four-class emotion dataset: angry, happy, sad, neutral.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Valence-Arousal Space Mapping**. 53 candidate scenarios are mapped to the valence-arousal plane (Figure 1(a)): the horizontal axis is valence (valence, emotional pleasantness from negative to positive), and the vertical axis is arousal (arousal, physiological activation level from low to high). The four final categories occupy four regions: angry is low-valence-high-arousal, happy is high-valence-high-arousal, sad is low-valence-low-arousal, and neutral is medium-valence-medium-arousal; the fourth quadrant (high-valence-low-arousal, i.e., calm, relaxed region) was entirely removed due to scarcity of scenarios. The engineering significance of this mapping is to explicitly decompose category distinction into a combination of two orthogonal dimensions. All patterns in the subsequent confusion matrix—difficulty distinguishing angry and happy within high arousal (valence dimension blurred), difficulty distinguishing sad and neutral (both low arousal)—can be traced back to this geometric structure. Acoustically, this corresponds to differences in distinguishability between arousal-related cues like energy and pitch, and valence-related cues like spectral centroid (Figure 3's energy and pitch distribution plots show that high-arousal and low-arousal groups are indeed separable).

**Mathematical Form of Hand-Crafted Feature Track**. The extended Geneva Minimal Acoustic Parameter Set eGeMAPS [22] is used, 88 dimensions, covering frequency, energy, and spectral domains. Sentence-level features are input into a logistic regression classifier after z-score standardization with fixed mean and variance. The reason for choosing eGeMAPS over open feature sets: it is a minimal standard feature set extensively validated in the affective computing field, with low dimensionality and high information density, statistically matching the sample size of 488 (88-dimensional features for 488 samples, overfitting risk is controllable), while retaining cross-study comparability.

**Mathematical Form of Pre-trained Track**. wav2vec 2.0 [29] consists of three modules: a CNN-based local encoder extracts low-level representations directly from raw waveforms; a quantization module discretizes low-level representations into pseudo-labels for contrastive learning; a transformer-based contextual network predicts future representations from past representations using a contrastive loss—i.e., identifying the true future quantized representation among several distractor candidates. Its standard contrastive objective is $\mathcal{L}_t = -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_t, q_t))}{\sum_{k} \exp(\text{sim}(z_t, \tilde{q}_k))}$, where $z_t$ is the output of the contextual network, $q_t$ is the predicted future quantized representation, and $\tilde{q}_k$ is the distractor (this formula comes from the original wav2vec 2.0 paper; this paper only describes the structure and does not provide the formula). This paper uses wav2vec2.0-base: 12 transformer blocks plus 7 convolutional blocks (512 channels per block), implemented based on HuggingFace transformers [30]. Sentence-level features are obtained by average pooling over frame-level outputs. Fine-tuning hyperparameters: AdamW optimizer [31], $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, learning rate 5e-5 (printed as 5e5 in the original, likely a typo), trained for 200 epochs, input is full audio sampled at 16kHz, batch size is 1 (the reason for batch size 1 is not reported in the paper, speculated to be related to extremely short samples and training stability strategies).

**Evaluation Metrics**. Weighted Accuracy (WA) is the overall accuracy of all samples, i.e., $\text{WA} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \mathbb{1}[\hat{y}_i = y_i]$; Unweighted Accuracy (UA) is the arithmetic mean of the accuracy of each class, i.e., $\text{UA} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{i \in c}\mathbb{1}[\hat{y}_i = y_i]$. The reason for reporting both: classes are not completely balanced (107 to 133 utterances per class, Table II), WA would be dominated by majority classes, while UA treats each class equally, exposing model collapse in individual classes. Human verification performance is calculated as the average of the performance of 8 evaluators (footnote explanation in Table III).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Scenario-Based Elicititation Protocol

**Where do scenarios come from**. The paper did not have authors write scripts on a whim, but instead hired 8 graduate students with over three years of experience in affective computing, starting from Zepf et al.'s review on driver emotion recognition [16], first establishing five types of driver emotions—anger, stress, happiness, fear, sadness—and then requiring each person to propose at least two "scenarios of using in-vehicle VUI under this emotion" for each type. After deduplication and merging, 53 scenarios were obtained. The starting point was a driver emotion review rather than a general emotion classification, ensuring the scenario set naturally fits the senior target scenario rather than a general scenario.

**From 53 to 12**. The 53 scenarios were mapped to the valence-arousal plane (Figure 1(a)), the fourth quadrant was removed due to scarcity of scenarios, and the rest were clustered into four groups (angry: low-valence-high-arousal, happy: high-valence-high-arousal, sad: low-valence-low-arousal, neutral: medium-valence-medium-arousal). Three representative scenarios were selected from each group (marked in red, orange, blue, and black in the figure), totaling 12 scenarios entering the recording phase. Why go through this process in a continuous space rather than directly defining four emotion words: first, space mapping can expose coverage gaps—the absence of the fourth quadrant was discovered this way, which directly explains why the conclusion lists calm, relax-type emotions as future work; second, it ensures that the final categories are spaced apart in 2D geometry, leaving structural space for subsequent classification tasks in terms of acoustic distinguishability.

**Design of Scenario Cards**. Each scenario is made into a card (Figure 1(b)): a recording guide sentence at the top, fixed with the wake-up word "Hi, KIA" at the beginning, followed by a scenario sentence inducing the target emotion at the end; the background is an in-car illustration, accompanied by a reference image visualizing the scenario. The recording interaction process (Figure 1 caption): participants look at a visualized scenario, imagine themselves in the given scenario and emotional state, and after being ready, click the recording button to speak dialogue starting with Hi, KIA. The paper cites scenario planning method research [17][18] to explain that visual cues are a simple and effective means to allow participants to immerse quickly. The fixed start with "Hi, KIA" on the card serves two purposes: ensuring text consistency of the wake-up word (text-dependent design), and allowing the emotion to be induced first by the scenario sentence within the speaker, and then naturally extended to the wake-up word at the beginning of the sentence—this is fundamentally different from the acquisition method of "direct imperative reading of words" in terms of ecological validity.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Recording Quality Control and Human Verification Annotation Protocol

**Recording Protocol**. 4 male actors and 4 female actors were recruited online, requiring similar recording conditions between each other; average age 31.38 years, standard deviation 3.90 years. The process has four steps: Step 1 provides an online recording tutorial, including guidance on expressing target emotions; Step 2 requires maintaining a distance of 30 cm from the microphone during recording (the original text writes "mouse position", combined with context, it should be a typo for "mouth", i.e., mouth-to-microphone distance 30 cm); Step 3 provides feedback on recording quality after the first round of recording, confirming that emotions are correctly captured; Step 4 adds 5 more recordings per person. Finally, 576 audio clips were obtained from 12 scenarios. Calculated as 8 people × 12 scenarios × 6 utterances per scenario per person (1 first round + 5 after feedback) exactly divides to 576, which the paper does not directly give, but the numbers are consistent.

**Post-processing**. The recording is of the complete scenario sentence, and post-processing crops out the wake-up word segment at the beginning of the sentence as the dataset sample—this is the source of the 0.64-second average duration: the scenario sentence completes the emotion induction task and is then discarded; the dataset is designed to retain only the wake-up word itself.

**Filtering Criteria for Human Verification**. The 8 graduate students who participated in scenario design listened to all 576 recordings in random order and performed four-class classification, allowing "unknown" for difficult judgments. The removal rule is very conservative: only deleted when all evaluators' predictions differ from the true label, totaling 88 utterances removed (removal rate approx. 15.3%), retaining 488 as the final dataset. Inferring from the initial 144 per class (8 people × 3 scenarios per class × 6 utterances), the number of deletions per class is approx. angry 37, neutral 25, happy 15, sad 11 (inferred values, not directly reported in the paper)—the anger class lost the most, corroborating the pattern in the confusion matrix where "angry is largely heard as neutral": performed anger is most easily heard as emotionless within the 0.64-second window.

**Human Verification Itself is an Experimental Result**. The confusion matrix in Figure 2 gives three findings: evaluators classified sad relatively best; angry and neutral were the most difficult, tending to judge angry as neutral and neutral as sad; participants also observed angry and happy as neutral (the paper states that high-arousal speech is perceived as the emotional baseline), indicating that humans themselves have difficulty perceiving high-arousal impressions from wake-up words. This finding shapes the baseline for interpreting all results: human average performance WA 63.17%, UA 62.31% (Table III), any model must be compared to this line.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Dual-Track Baseline and Fine-Tuning Location Ablation

**Why set two tracks**. 488 utterances is a typical scale where "deep models cannot be trained, but one is unwilling to use only hand-crafted features." The paper's two routes each represent a source of knowledge for coping with small data: Track A compresses decades of domain knowledge in speech emotion research into 88-dimensional eGeMAPS—mature, low-dimensional, interpretable, zero pre-training cost; Track B transfers general speech representations learned via self-supervision on large corpora (wav2vec 2.0 pre-trained on LibriSpeech read English corpus) here, adapting to the emotion task via fine-tuning. The question answered by the dual-track comparison is: on small-sample wake-up word emotion tasks, which is more valuable, domain knowledge or pre-trained generalization, and what is the gap?

**Fine-tuning Location Ablation Design**. Track B follows the practice of Xia et al. [28] to systematically compare four strategies: no fine-tuning directly extracting features (vanilla), only fine-tuning the encoder network (responsible for low-level representations), only fine-tuning the contextual network (responsible for high-level representations), and full network fine-tuning. The value of this ablation lies in decoupling "what to transfer" and "how much to transfer": the low-level acoustic representations of the encoder functionally approximate the scope of hand-crafted features, while the high-level compositional representations of the contextual network are where emotion mapping is most likely to occur. Under the constraint of 488 utterances, the performance ranking of the four strategies directly gives the sweet spot for small-data fine-tuning (results see Experimental Results section).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

**Compared to SER datasets like RAVDESS, TESS**: Text-dependent (fixed Hi, KIA) vs. text-independent; 0.64 seconds vs. 2.06 to 4.09 seconds (Table I); full emotion annotation vs. sentence-level annotation. Distribution differences determine that sentence-level SER models cannot be directly transferred; a baseline must be rebuilt on the new distribution.

**Compared to OK Aura**: Both are wake-up word emotion datasets, but OK Aura has only 218/1247 annotated, three classes, Spanish; Hi,KIA has full 488 annotated, four classes, Korean, and the category system comes from valence-arousal space design rather than direct emotion naming—the latter allows the acoustic distinguishability between categories to be explicitly planned before acquisition.

**Compared to general SER acquisition pipelines**: The paper documents and opens source the complete protocol for scenario induction, space mapping, recording quality control, and human verification, and the methodology itself is transferable to the acquisition of other languages and other wake-up words—this has direct reference value for teams wanting to build Chinese wake-up word emotion datasets, which is an overflow contribution of this single dataset paper beyond its data itself.

**Compared to general wav2vec 2.0 fine-tuning practices**: The paper's contribution is giving the fine-tuning strategy conclusion for the extreme case of "ultra-short speech plus extremely small data" (only fine-tuning the contextual network is optimal), and the baseline fact that "the fine-tuned model exceeds humans on specific confusion pairs."

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

The experiment is conducted entirely on Hi,KIA: 488 utterances, 8 Korean voice actors (4 male, 4 female), four emotion classes, Korean accent, wake-up word type, average duration 0.64 seconds (Table I). Table II gives the label breakdown: angry 107 utterances, average 0.610 seconds; happy 129 utterances, 0.586 seconds; sad 133 utterances, 0.776 seconds; neutral 119 utterances, 0.588 seconds; total 488 utterances, 0.644 seconds. A notable detail: the average duration of sad is significantly longer than the other three classes (0.776 vs. 0.586 to 0.610 seconds), consistent with the phonetic rule that low-arousal emotions have slower speech rates and prolonged vowels—the dataset does not artificially equalize lengths, retaining natural duration differences of emotions, so duration itself becomes a learnable arousal cue. Data split ensures speaker independence via 8-fold cross-validation: each fold uses utterances from 7 speakers for training and validation, and utterances from the remaining 1 speaker for testing, averaging approx. 61 test utterances per fold. Total account on the recording side: 576 original recordings minus 88 removed equals 488; training side sampling rate 16kHz, input full audio.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Metrics are WA and UA, defined in the Methodology section. Reasons for selection: first, classes are lightly imbalanced (107 to 133 per class), a single overall accuracy would mask inter-class differences, UA is equivalent to the macro-average of recall rates for each class, specifically exposing minority class collapse; second, reporting WA and UA together is a common convention in the SER field, ensuring comparability with existing literature on baselines like IEMOCAP; third, putting human verification performance (average of 8 evaluators, WA 63.17%, UA 62.31%, Table III) in the same table is because human perception ability on ultra-short wake-up words is itself an unknown quantity, and a "human perceivable line" must be set first as a reference for interpreting models—the meaning of models exceeding or falling below this line is completely different. The paper does not report F1 values, confidence intervals, or significance tests.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table III gives six rows of results, analyzed row by row:

**Baseline (eGeMAPS + Logistic Regression) WA 51.02%, UA 50.63%**. The four-class random line is 25%, the hand-crafted feature track achieves twice the accuracy of the random line, but the absolute level is just over half. Combined with the acoustic analysis in Figure 3, its information source is mainly the arousal dimension carried by energy and pitch, while the valence dimension is basically not captured.

**Wav2vec2.0 No Fine-tuning WA 33.61%, UA 32.16%**. Lower than the hand-crafted feature track by more than 17 percentage points, only 8 percentage points higher than the random line. The paper's interpretation is: self-supervised learning alone is difficult to extract high-level emotional features. The deep reason is worth expanding: wav2vec 2.0's pre-training objective is speech recognition-oriented contrastive learning, the training corpus is LibriSpeech read English, with no emotional supervision from start to finish; its representations are optimized for recognizing phonetic content, and on the 0.64-second window, the low-level acoustic information it can carry overlaps significantly with eGeMAPS, but lacks the high-level structure to map arousal and valence. This result itself is valuable—it falsifies the imagination that "large model features are plug-and-play."

**Only Fine-tuning Encoder Network WA 46.52%, UA 44.25%**. After fine-tuning, it is still lower than the hand-crafted track. Explanation: the general acoustic representation of the low-level convolutional encoder is itself high quality, and forcibly adjusting low-level parameters on the small data of 488 utterances, the loss of breaking general representation is greater than the cost of overfitting, while the high-level parts that truly need to adapt to the emotion task are still misaligned with the task.

**Only Fine-tuning Contextual Network WA 68.64%, UA 68.51%**—best in the table. 17.62 percentage points higher than the hand-crafted track (WA), 35.03 percentage points higher than no fine-tuning, and exceeding human average performance by approx. 5.5 percentage points. The paper thus gives the core conclusion: for small datasets, updating only parameters related to high-level representations is more efficient than updating all parameters. Explanation at the mechanism level: emotion mapping occurs on high-level compositional representations, and the contextual network is exactly the part that needs reprogramming; while the low-level acoustic features extracted by the CNN encoder are highly general, freezing them is equivalent to freely preserving the acoustic priors learned from large corpora, while compressing the number of trainable parameters to a scale matching the 488 samples.

**Full Network Fine-tuning WA 57.99%, UA 57.22%**. In the middle, significantly lower than only tuning the contextual network. Explanation: updating all parameters simultaneously overfits on 488 utterances, and the cost of misleading the low-level layers exceeds the benefit of adapting the high-level layers. The ranking of the four strategies (no fine-tuning, only tuning encoder, full tuning, only tuning contextual network) forms a clean unimodal curve, with the sweet spot falling on only tuning the contextual network.

**Human Verification WA 63.17%, UA 62.31%**. Exceeded by the model that only tunes the contextual network. The paper's attribution is the subjectivity of emotion recognition: human judgment carries individual differences and instability, while the model learns a consistent mapping within the dataset on fixed labels. Another interpretation is that this exceedance is concentrated on the confusion pairs where humans are weakest (see cross-comparison below with Figure 2), the model did not comprehensively exceed human perception, but rather filled the gap in the specific shortcoming of distinguishing high-arousal from neutral.

**Fold-by-fold Analysis (Figure 4)**. Three observations: wav2vec 2.0 features (fine-tuned contextual network) exceed human verification performance on most female folds; human verification performance is relatively stable on both male and female folds; hand-crafted features and wav2vec 2.0 fine-tuning both have significant performance gaps between male and female folds, lower than human verification on the four folds M1, M2, M4, F5. The conclusion is that speaker and gender generalization are clear shortcomings—the scale of 8 speakers determines that each speaker is a fold, the model is sensitive to the acoustic specificity of individual speakers, and the paper's conclusion also lists "robustness to speakers and gender" as future work based on this.

**Confusion Matrix Analysis (Figure 5,对照 Figure 2)**. Both models are good at distinguishing happy and sad—this pair pulls apart on both arousal and valence dimensions, with the most sufficient acoustic cues. Hand-crafted features are weak in two places: valence differences within high arousal (angry vs. happy) and sad vs. neutral. Both weaknesses correspond to the valence dimension: features like energy and pitch in eGeMAPS mainly encode arousal, while valence-related cues like spectral centroid are too weak within 0.64 seconds. Fine-tuning the contextual network significantly alleviates these two confusions. Cross-comparison with Figure 2's human confusion yields one of the most interesting findings of this paper: wav2vec 2.0 fine-tuned on the contextual network exceeds humans in distinguishing "high-arousal emotions from neutral"—this is exactly where humans find it most difficult to identify high-arousal impressions from wake-up words (humans largely judge angry and happy as neutral), and the model fills the gap in human blind spots.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The only systematic ablation in this paper is the fine-tuning location ablation (no fine-tuning, only tuning encoder, only tuning contextual network, full tuning), findings summarized into four points:

First, fine-tuning location is more important than fine-tuning itself. For the same fine-tuning, only tuning the contextual network (68.64%) differs from only tuning the encoder (46.52%) by more than 22 percentage points, exceeding any difference caused by "whether to fine-tune or not." The key decision in small-data transfer learning is not "whether to transfer," but "which layer to transfer."

Second, freezing low-level layers is equivalent to free regularization. Full parameter fine-tuning (57.99%) is more than 10 percentage points lower than only tuning high-level layers (68.64%), indicating that at the scale of 488 utterances, the overfitting cost of releasing low-level parameters is the dominant factor.

Third, there is a representation gap between self-supervised pre-trained representations and emotion tasks. Unfine-tuned wav2vec 2.0 (33.61%) is worse than 88-dimensional hand-crafted features (51.02%), indicating that when the pre-training objective (speech recognition contrastive learning) is misaligned with the emotion objective, large model representations cannot automatically cash in as emotion task performance.

Fourth, cross-analysis of fold-by-fold and confusion matrices (Figure 4, Figure 5) shows that ablation conclusions are not uniform across different speaker subgroups: the model's advantage over humans is more obvious on female folds, and the model is lower than humans on M1, M2, M4, F5 folds—the sweet spot of the fine-tuning strategy fluctuates depending on gender and speaker.

Ablation dimensions not reported by the paper: classifier comparison (SVM, Random Forest, etc., besides logistic regression), comparison with other pre-trained models (HuBERT, WavLM, which performed better in SER at the time), pooling strategy comparison (attention pooling besides average pooling), handling of class imbalance, noise and reverberation robustness tests.

## Main Contributions

**Contribution 1: Filling the blank of public datasets**. The first Korean emotion-annotated dataset for wake-up words: 488 utterances, 8 speakers, four emotion classes, average 0.64 seconds, full manual annotation (the only dataset in Table I comparison that simultaneously satisfies "wake-up word type plus full emotion annotation"), data and code are all open source.

**Contribution 2: Reusable acquisition methodology**. The four-stage complete protocol of scenario induction, valence-arousal space mapping, recording quality control, and human verification is documented, and executed entirely online—this process has direct transfer value for building emotion datasets for other languages and other wake-up words, especially the design sequence of "first checking coverage in continuous space, then landing categories."

**Contribution 3: Discovery of human baseline**. Systematically quantified human emotion perception ability on 0.64-second wake-up words (WA 63.17%), and gave its confusion structure: sad is easiest to distinguish, high-arousal emotions (angry, happy) are most easily heard as neutral. This finding is independent of any model and itself corrects the intuitive assumption that "humans can always hear emotions in short speech."

**Contribution 4: Strategy conclusion for small-data transfer learning**. Under the extreme constraint of 488 utterances, only fine-tuning the contextual network (high-level) of wav2vec 2.0 is significantly better than full-parameter fine-tuning and only tuning low-level layers, reaching WA 68.64%, UA 68.51%, and exceeding human average in the human's weakest high-arousal vs. neutral distinction—setting a benchmark for subsequent research on this dataset.

**Contribution 5: Engineering implications for in-vehicle emotional interaction**. Data and protocols were produced in collaboration between KAIST Design Department and Kia Design Center, anchoring the entire process from scenario design to emotion category selection to the in-vehicle scenario, providing the first data cornerstone for the "wake-up means knowing emotion" in-vehicle interaction paradigm.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**Scale is the biggest wall**. 488 utterances, 8 speakers. Under 8-fold cross-validation, each fold tests only approx. 61 utterances, a flip of a single sample affects approx. 1.6 percentage points of intra-fold accuracy, and the inter-fold fluctuation in Figure 4 partly stems from this; there are only 4 males and 4 females in the speaker dimension, unable to support sufficient testing of gender robustness, let alone estimating larger demographic variances like age, dialect, etc. All absolute performance numbers should be interpreted against this background of statistical noise.

**Ecological validity gap of performed emotions**. All samples come from voice actors performing emotions according to scenario scripts, which has a known gap in acoustic distribution from spontaneous emotions in real cockpits (fatigue, road rage, distraction, tension). Scenario cards plus reference images alleviate the problem of "imperative reading of words," allowing emotions to be induced by the scenario first and then extended to the wake-up word, but cannot eliminate the essential difference between acted and spontaneous—the model learns the acoustic pattern of "performed anger," and its generalization to real emotions is unverified.

**Uncontrollable recording environment**. Completed entirely online, the paper only constrained tutorials, 30 cm mouth-to-microphone distance (original text "mouse position" should be a typo for "mouth") and the requirement of "similar recording conditions," but key acoustic variables such as microphone model, room reverberation, and background noise level are not reported. This batch of data therefore cannot be used to study device and channel robustness, while real in-vehicle scenarios are exactly engine noise plus far-field multi-microphone pickup.

**Incomplete coverage of emotion space**. Among the initial five types of driver emotions (anger, stress, happiness, fear, sadness), stress and fear were merged or discarded in valence-arousal clustering; the fourth quadrant (high-valence-low-arousal calm, relaxed) is entirely missing due to scarcity of scenarios—and this is exactly one of the most common state areas in normal driving. The granularity of four classes is too coarse for in-vehicle emotion adaptive applications.

**Absolute performance level**. The best contextual network fine-tuning only reaches WA 68.64%, approx. one error in three on a four-class task, far from any practical deployment; the positioning of this paper is a baseline, not an usable system.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

**Non-blind design of verifiers**. Human verification was executed by the same 8 graduate students who participated in scenario design, who have prior memory of the scenario scripts they wrote, and their judgment strategies may be contaminated; and the average performance of 8 people itself has large variance, limiting the estimation accuracy of the "human line." The paper does not report inter-evaluator consistency coefficients (such as Fleiss kappa).

**Two-sided nature of removal criteria**. The rule that only deletes when all judges disagree ensures zero false removals, but the cost is that a large number of samples where "the majority disagrees" are retained, and label noise still exists in the final dataset; how the unknown judgment count is included in performance statistics is also not reported by the paper. More strict partial consistency rate filtering or soft label schemes are worth considering in an extended version.

**Narrow baseline coverage**. Only one classifier, logistic regression, no comparison with traditional strong baselines like SVM; only wav2vec2.0-base on the deep side, no comparison with HuBERT, WavLM which already performed better in SER at the time; no joint training or transfer experiments with cross-datasets like OK Aura, and cross-lingual generalization ability is unknown.

**No robustness and no statistical testing**. No evaluation with added noise, added reverberation, or far-field simulation; all comparisons lack significance tests or confidence intervals (not reported by the paper), and conclusions like "model exceeds human by approx. 5.5 percentage points" may not be statistically significant at the scale of 61 utterances per fold.

**Incomplete training details**. Learning rate printed as 5e5 in the original is suspected to be a typo for 5e-5; reasons for batch size 1 and epoch 200 are not explained; handling of class imbalance sampling or loss weighting is not reported by the paper; regularization and hyperparameter search for the hand-crafted feature track are not reported.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper self-reports three directions: adding more emotion types such as calm, relax (filling the fourth quadrant of valence-arousal, especially important for in-vehicle scenarios); in-depth analysis of the essential acoustic features of wake-up word emotion classification; building recognition models robust to speakers and gender.

From an engineering perspective, five directions can be extended: **Multi-task joint training**—jointly training emotion recognition with the wake-up word detection itself, outputting wake-up judgment and emotion state simultaneously in one forward pass, saving edge-side computing power for independent emotion models, which aligns with the main line of constraints for edge-side KWS (fast, accurate, low power); **Cross-lingual extension**—using multi-lingual pre-trained models to transfer wake-up word emotions from Korean to other languages, testing the language-independent components of emotional acoustic cues; **Spontaneous emotion acquisition**—collecting spontaneous emotions in real or simulated cockpits using scenario induction plus physiological signal corroboration, bridging the distribution gap between acted and spontaneous; **Far-field and noise enhancement**—adding noise, reverberation, and far-field simulation enhancement to the existing 488 utterances, or re-recording far-field versions, to approximate in-vehicle pickup conditions; **Closed-loop verification**—how the system should adjust responses (tone, speed, turn-taking) after recognizing anger or fatigue, interaction experiments of emotion adaptive strategies are the downstream problems this dataset ultimately serves, and this is where its value lies in distinguishing it from pure algorithm datasets.
