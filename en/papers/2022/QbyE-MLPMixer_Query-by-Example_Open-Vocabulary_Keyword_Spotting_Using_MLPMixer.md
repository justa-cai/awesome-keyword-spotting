- **Date**: June 23, 2022 (arXiv v1)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13231
- **Keywords**: open-vocabulary keyword spotting, query-by-example, MLPMixer, user-defined wake word, audio embedding, far-field robustness, edge deployment

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the voice interface for smart speakers, smart TVs, and various home appliances. However, the wake words in commercial systems are almost exclusively fixed at the factory—"Hey Siri," "Alexa," "Ok Google." Behind this fixed-vocabulary paradigm lies an implicit cost structure: models must be trained on large amounts of pre-defined keyword data (the introduction lists fixed-vocabulary backbones such as DNN, TDNN, CNN, RNN, and transformer in chronological order, referencing [1]-[8]). Changing a wake word implies re-collecting data, re-training, and re-releasing the software. For manufacturers, this is a week-long pipeline; for ordinary users, it is a completely closed option. Allowing users to register wake words using their own accents, phrasing, or even self-chosen names—i.e., user-defined keyword spotting—is a natural demand for personalized interaction in smart devices and constitutes the task definition of open-vocabulary KWS.

Open-vocabulary KWS raises the difficulty of the problem by an order of magnitude. The introduction explicitly points out three challenges: low latency, small memory footprint, and the fact that enrollment and query utterances naturally fall outside the training distribution. The first two are old constraints for always-on detection on edge devices—devices need to listenaround the clock, and computational and storage budgets are extremely limited; the third is a new constraint unique to open-vocabulary settings: when users enroll, they provide only a few utterances, while the speech rate, accent, speaking distance, and background noise during querying are uncontrollable. The model cannot pass by "remembering the decision boundary of a specific word"; it must learn truly generic word-level audio representations, encoding the "identity of the word" into a comparable vector space.

Early open-vocabulary solutions relied on the output of ASR systems, performing matching on word-level [9] or phoneme-level [10] transcribed text. These solutions have two fatal flaws: first, running full ASR decoding is computationally too expensive for edge devices, conflicting with the "always-on, low-power" positioning of wake-word detection; second, performance degrades immediately if the user-selected keyword falls outside the ASR vocabulary—which is precisely the most typical usage scenario for such applications. The mainstream paradigm later shifted to Query-by-Example (QbyE): mapping variable-length audio signals to fixed-length embeddings in a vector space, with the training objective being to make embeddings of the same word close and embeddings of different words far apart; inference no longer requires any decoding, only comparing the distance between the enrollment embedding and the query embedding. In this paradigm, the representational capacity and efficiency of the encoder constitute the entire upper limit of the system, which is exactly the main battlefield for the model selection in this paper.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper reviews existing choices for QbyE encoders along technical lines, implicitly outlining their respective costs:

- **RNN-based encoders**: Literature [11] first applied LSTM to QbyE open-vocabulary detection; [13][14][15] used RNNs combined with CTC loss to predict keyword sequences (e.g., DONUT, Quantized LSTM, LSTM-CTC); [16] used GRU with multi-head self-attention and softtriple loss (i.e., the direct baseline GRU-ATTN in this paper, also by the same authors in their previous work at ICASSP 2021, which reported open-vocabulary results on Hey-Snips). The inherent cost of RNNs is serial dependency in the time dimension: hidden states must be passed frame by frame, backpropagation during training requires unfolding along time, and inference latency accumulates linearly with the number of frames, making deep parallelization difficult on modern accelerators and DSPs. From Table 1, the smallest GRU-ATTN (0.55M parameters) incurs 41.23M MACs, which is double that of the proposed model.
- **CNN encoders transferred from vision**: Edge-friendly vision models like MobileNetV2 [30], MobileNetV3 [25], and EfficientNetB0 [34] have indeed had successful precedents in audio tasks (audio tagging [31], fixed-vocabulary detection [32], personalized detection [33]). However, transfer comes with a hidden tax: the input is originally single-channel time-frequency features, but they must be artificially mapped to three channels to fit the input interface of vision models, as done in [32]; furthermore, experiments found that versions with ImageNet pre-trained weights significantly outperformed those trained from scratch—despite the fact that there is "no explicit relationship" between MFCC features and ImageNet images (in the authors' words), the model essentially relies on a task-agnostic initialization to achieve competitiveness. From Table 1, the parameter count for this route ranges from 2.22M to 4.01M, and the embedding dimension is around 1000 (MobileNetV3 is 960), which is not friendly for enrollment storage and retrieval computation.
- **Attention/Transformer route**: ViT [21] has only 0.96M parameters as a baseline, but MACs are as high as 77.06M (Table 1), nearly four times that of the proposed model. The quadratic complexity of self-attention is a real power burden in always-on edge scenarios.
- **Non-QbyE sister routes**: Siamese networks with triplet hinge loss [17], RNN-T predicting phoneme or grapheme subword units [18], prototype networks with Max-Mahalanobis center loss [19], etc., solve "classification with variable class numbers" or "subword decoding," which is different from the embedding retrieval route of this paper; the paper only provides background information on these.

A noteworthy statement of motivation is that the authors favor MLPMixer [20] not because it is "faster to update," but because of its "simple and intuitive design"—replacing the attention mechanism in ViT with pure MLPs alone, while sibling models like gMLP [22], ResMLP [23], and RepMLP [24] have already proven competitiveness in vision and language tasks. In other words, the core question of this paper is: Can this minimalist structure, when moved to audio embedding learning, simultaneously maintain both accuracy and edge-side overhead?

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Transforming MLPMixer, designed for images, into an audio QbyE encoder, and answering three specific questions: First, how to adapt the input representation—is patch splitting and linear embedding in the image pipeline necessary for MFCC, or can the complete time-frequency matrix be used directly; Second, how to select structural hyperparameters—under what depth and width does pure MLP stacking achieve FRR lower than the SOTA RNN/CNN baselines on Hey-Snips and internal datasets under 10dB, 6dB noise, and far-field conditions; Third, can the overhead be made the lowest overall—the goal is to have fewer parameters than the smallest GRU-ATTN (0.55M), fewer MACs than the smallest MobileNetV3 (22.24M), ultimately achieving 0.25M parameters and 20.16M MACs (Table 1), while the embedding dimension is only 81.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The system follows an encoder-decoder skeleton (Figure 1). During training, the encoder compresses high-dimensional input into low-dimensional embeddings, and the decoder ties the encoder to the loss function (cross-entropy at the word level in this paper), causing embeddings of the same word to cluster together and embeddings of different words to separate; during inference, the decoder is completely discarded, and the trigger decision degrades to "distance comparison between enrollment embedding and query embedding." The value of this design lies in decoupling "representation learning" from "trigger decision": in the training phase, the embedding space is shaped using a large-scale general vocabulary; in the deployment phase, registering a new keyword only requires performing a forward encoding for a few enrollment audio clips of that word, with zero training and zero software release, naturally supporting open-vocabulary.

The encoder itself is an audio-adapted version of MLPMixer, with two key changes forming the technical主线 (main thread) of the paper. The first is no patching at the input: since ViT, many models (including the original MLPMixer) first split the input into non-overlapping patches and then perform per-patch linear embedding, while this paper keeps the feature dimension and time dimension intact, feeding the matrix of 81-dimensional features multiplied by 81 time steps directly into the model. The authors give two reasons: first, to study the impact of input representation on the model's ability to extract information from the entire feature space; second, direct input reduces computational and memory footprint. Experiments prove that this approach significantly outperforms patching (see Section 4.3.1 and Table 4). The second is the redefinition of mixing dimensions: the original MLPMixer alternates mixing on the flattened image patch embedding token dimension (token-mixing) and channel dimension (channel-mixing), while this paper changes to alternating mixing on the feature dimension (feature-mixing) and time dimension (time-mixing)—for single-channel MFCC input, these are the two axes with true physical significance.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The input is 81-dimensional MFCC features extracted from 1-second audio, with a frame length of 25 ms and a frame shift of 12.5 ms, yielding exactly 81 frames, forming an 81×81 square matrix (feature dimension f = time dimension t = 81; the square structure makes the shapes of the two mixing blocks symmetric, allowing hyperparameters to share intuition). CMVN (Cepstral Mean and Variance Normalization) is performed on the time dimension before entering the encoder.

The feature mixing block acts on the feature dimension, formula (1):

$$U = X + W_2\, \sigma\left(W_1\, \text{LayerNorm}(X)\right)$$

where $X \in \mathbb{R}^{f \times t}$ is the input feature, and $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times f}$ and $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{f \times h}$ are the weights of the two fully connected layers within the block. The features after LayerNorm are first projected to the hidden dimension $h$, passed through the activation function $\sigma$, then projected back to $f$, and finally added to the input via residual connection. The time mixing block does the same thing with the transpose of $U$ as the object, formula (2):

$$Y = \left(U + W_4\, \sigma\left(W_3\, \text{LayerNorm}(U^T)\right)\right)^T$$

where $W_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{g \times t}$ and $W_4 \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times g}$. The structures of the two formulas are completely isomorphic, differing only in the axis of operation—feature mixing recombines the 81-dimensional spectrum within each frame, while time mixing recombines the 81-frame history of each frequency bin. Alternating stacking of the two achieves layer-by-layer coupling of frequency-domain patterns and temporal dynamics. The activation function chosen is Hardswish (selection basis see ablation 4.3.2), with LayerNorm and residual connections within each block.

This design has a very engineering-friendly property: regardless of the values set for hidden dimensions $h$ and $g$, the output shape of each mixing block is always equal to the input shape $f \times t$. This means blocks can be freely stacked, and the entire model truly only needs to tune four hyperparameters—feature hidden dimension $h$, time hidden dimension $g$, number of stacked blocks $n$, and activation function $\sigma$, making hyperparameter optimization straightforward. The paper ran hundreds of sampling experiments using Asynchronous Successive Halving Algorithm (ASHA) for search, and the final selected configuration is: 12 mixing blocks, hidden layer width of 64 for both time and frequency mixing blocks (the original text writes "64 hidden layers," but contextually it means hidden dimension 64), no dropout, corresponding to 250K parameters and 20M MACs (precise values in Table 1: 0.25M / 20.16M).

The output of the last mixing block $O \in \mathbb{R}^{f \times t}$ undergoes average pooling on the time dimension to obtain the embedding, formula (3):

$$z = \frac{1}{t} \sum_{i=1}^{t} O_{:,i}$$

The embedding $z \in \mathbb{R}^{f} = \mathbb{R}^{81}$. Why average pooling is sufficient here: the training data is uniformly cropped or padded to 1 second, and the position of the word within the window is random; averaging over the time dimension naturally provides translation invariance, directly eliminating the irrelevant variable of "where the word is in the window," leaving only the word identity information. The 81-dimensional embedding size is the same as the MFCC feature dimension, an order of magnitude smaller than the embeddings of baseline models which are often around 1000 dimensions (MobileNetV3 is 960)—the authors interpret this as evidence that MLPMixer can effectively project useful information into a very small hidden space.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Native MFCC Input without Patching and Feature/Time Dual-Dimension Mixing

This is the most transferable experience in the paper for audio tasks. The original motivation for image patching is to reduce the burden on self-attention: cutting a 224×224 image into 16×16 patches reduces the number of tokens from tens of thousands to 196, making the quadratic cost of attention affordable. However, MFCC is already a highly compressed time-frequency feature; the "resolution" of 81×81 is the entire amount of information the model needs to access, and there is no issue of "downsampling to reduce burden." Forcing a 9×9 patch split (the "with PE" variant constructed by the paper for fair comparison, where each patch undergoes 81-dimensional linear embedding, resulting in an input shape identical to the proposed model) forces the model to perform a linear recombination within local 9×9 sub-blocks first, delaying cross-patch frequency-time structural interactions to deeper layers, and additionally incurring the overhead of a patch embedding layer with 7K parameters and 500K MACs.

Ablation data (Table 4, average FRR across six scenarios) supports this judgment: with PE is 13.22% (Hey-Snips near-field), 5.98% (Internal near-field), 20.37% (Hey-Snips far-field), 13.07% (Internal far-field); the proposed direct input corresponds to 7.86%, 3.57%, 12.26%, 8.63%—according to the original text, the error rate is relatively reduced by 40% (Hey-Snips near-field and far-field) and 34% (Internal far-field). More interestingly, the third variant "w/o PE" (cut 9×9 patches but only do reshape, no linear embedding): it performs worse than with PE on Hey-Snips (near-field 17.02% vs 13.22%), but slightly better on the internal dataset (near-field 5.74% vs 5.98%, far-field 12.40% vs 13.07%), indicating that the effect of patch linear embedding fluctuates with the dataset, while direct mixing of complete dimensions is the robust global optimum among them.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Replacing GELU with Hardswish Activation Function

The original MLPMixer uses GELU, but the ASHA hyperparameter search in this paper found Hardswish [25] to be more effective for this task. Table 5 gives the average FRR across six scenarios for four activation functions under the same pipeline: GELU is 8.69% (Hey-Snips near-field), 3.95% (Internal near-field), 13.13% (Hey-Snips far-field), 10.02% (Internal far-field); ReLU is 9.76%, 4.03%, 14.90%, 9.29%; SiLU is 8.33%, 4.29%, 11.90%, 9.91%; Hardswish is comprehensively optimal at 7.86%, 3.57%, 12.26%, 8.63%. Besides accuracy, the authors particularly emphasize an edge-side reason: Hardswish is a piecewise linear function, containing no exponential or sigmoid operations, making it easier to implement in hardware than GELU and SiLU. For always-on low-power devices, the instruction overhead of the activation function is amplified by the number of inferences per second; this "convenient saving" choice has practical significance.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: 81-Dimensional Small Embedding and Convolutional Cosine Retrieval Inference

The inference process (Section 2.3) is: for any input audio, perform sliding inference with a 1-second window and 100-millisecond step (i.e., 10 forward passes per second), calculating the cosine distance between the query embedding and all n enrollment embeddings each time. Distance calculation needs to handle the issue of inconsistent lengths between enrollment and query embeddings—because enrollment audio clips of different keywords may still be shorter than the 1-second query window after cropping. The rule in this paper is: if the enrollment embedding is shorter than the query embedding, perform convolution of the query embedding with the enrollment embedding and take the minimum distance, which is equivalent to letting the enrollment template slide within the query window and taking the best local alignment; if the enrollment embedding is longer, zero-pad the left side of the query embedding to match. The authors further point out that in streaming scenarios, even padding is unnecessary—one can pre-allocate a buffer of the same length as the longest enrollment, allowing the query to always match the length of the longest enrollment. After calculating the cosine distance for all enrollments, take the minimum value and compare it with a preset threshold; if less than the threshold, trigger a positive response. The reason for using cosine distance instead of Euclidean distance is straightforward: the magnitude of embeddings under different speakers and gains will drift; cosine only looks at direction, being naturally immune to loudness changes. The entire inference chain involves no decoding, no attention, no recursion, and the embedding is only 81 dimensions, so enrollment storage and retrieval overhead both scale linearly with the embedding dimension.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to GRU-ATTN [16]: Removed recurrence and attention, pure feedforward MLP, temporal modeling is completed by the implicit fully connected layer in the time mixing block; parameters 0.25M vs 0.55M, MACs 20.16M vs 41.23M (Table 1), both less than half of the opponent, embedding dimension 81 vs ~1000 magnitude. Compared to visual CNN baselines: No need for interface modifications like three-channel input mapping, nor reliance on ImageNet pre-training (although baselines with pre-trained weights could not catch up in difficult conditions), it is a structure designed natively for single-channel time-frequency features. Compared to the original MLPMixer [20]: No patch splitting and linear embedding, mixing axes changed from token/channel to feature/time, activation changed from GELU to Hardswish, time-dimension average pooling added at the output, time-dimension CMVN added at the input—among these five changes, the first two are backed by ablation data (Table 4, Table 5), and the latter three are conventional but necessary audio adaptations.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

**Training**: Follows the protocol of [16]. LibriSpeech (1000 hours, 16 kHz sampling, English read audiobooks with text annotations); since the training objective is "classify words given audio," word-level aligned splits are generated using Montreal Forced Aligner; after splitting, uniformly standardized to 1 second—random bilateral cropping for those longer than 1 second, bilateral zero-padding for those shorter than 1 second. Data augmentation runs in parallel on two fronts: first, randomly adding 4 to 12 dB background noise from the noise-train folder of the MS-SNSD dataset; second, simulating far-field effects with 50% probability according to [29], and stacking 4 to 15 dB point-source noise on top of the far-field effect. The objective function is cross-entropy. Notably, the training vocabulary comes from general vocabulary in LibriSpeech, completely non-overlapping with the keywords used in evaluation—this is the true meaning of the open-vocabulary setting: the embedding space is shaped by massive irrelevant words, and the evaluation words are pure zero-shot.

**Evaluation Positives**: Two datasets. The public Hey-Snips test set contains 2,588 positive utterances from 520 speakers (up to 10 per person); since the evaluation protocol requires the same speaker to provide both enrollment and query, the paper filtered out all speakers who had exactly 10 keyword utterances, reducing the number of speakers to 40, of which 3 were randomly selected for enrollment and the remaining 7 for query. The internal dataset contains 8 real appliance keywords—"LG Styler," "LG Washer," "LG Dryer," "Hey LG," "LG Fridge," "LG Puricare," "LG Oven," "Hey Cloi"—each spoken 10 times by 50 speakers (8 keywords × 50 speakers, i.e., the 400-speaker scale mentioned in the abstract), also with 3 for enrollment and 7 for query. Both datasets were recorded in clean environments, then distorted to simulate real scenarios: adding 10 dB and 6 dB noise from the noise-test folder of MS-SNSD, and stacking far-field effects before adding noise, forming a total of six scenarios (clean/10 dB/6 dB × near-field/far-field).

**Evaluation Negatives**: Take the negative samples from the Hey-Snips test set, approximately 20,000 general utterances, 1,469 speakers (up to 30 per person), split in half to randomly add 10 dB and 6 dB noise respectively; for far-field scenarios, add far-field effects first, then noise, pairing each negative subset with the corresponding positive subset for evaluation.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The main metric is the False Rejection Rate (FRR) at the fixed 0.3 False Acceptance per hour operating point (Tables 2, 3), with overall trade-offs presented via ROC curves (Figure 2). The reason for choosing this metric combination is the asymmetric cost of KWS productization: a false acceptance means the device suddenly records or responds when the user has not called it, directly harming privacy trust and user experience, the cost of which is far higher than the mild inconvenience of a missed wake-up. Therefore, the industry convention is to pin the false acceptance rate at a very low level (0.3 per hour approaches product-level strictness), and then compare who misses fewer under this constraint—the lower the FRR, the more usable the model is under the strict constraint of "almost no false triggers."

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Two ablations were run across all six scenarios on Hey-Snips and the internal dataset, with average FRR reported separately for near-field and far-field.

**Input Representation (Table 4)**: As described in the methodology, with PE (9×9 patch + 81-dimensional linear embedding) is 13.22 / 5.98 / 20.37 / 13.07, w/o PE (only block cutting reshape) is 17.02 / 5.74 / 21.67 / 12.40, the proposed direct input is 7.86 / 3.57 / 12.26 / 8.63. Relative to with PE, the proposed representation reduces error rates by 40% in Hey-Snips near-field and far-field, and by 34% in Internal far-field; simultaneously saving 7K parameters and 500K MACs. The deep implication of this ablation is: the input convention of vision architectures (patching) is not a neutral operation but a negative asset for audio tasks; removing it saves overhead and improves accuracy.

**Activation Function (Table 5)**: Hardswish 7.86 / 3.57 / 12.26 / 8.63 comprehensively outperforms GELU (8.69 / 3.95 / 13.13 / 10.02), ReLU (9.76 / 4.03 / 14.90 / 9.29), and SiLU (8.33 / 4.29 / 11.90 / 9.91), and is the simplest to implement. This ablation shows that components brought over intact from vision (GELU) are not optimal under the dual constraints of audio and edge-side, and the gains from small changes can be of the same magnitude as structural changes.

Synthesizing the two ablations reveals a common thread: the paper's two "de-visionization" decisions (no patching, changing activation) both yield positive returns for audio tasks, and neither adds any overhead—this supports the authors' stance that "the role of basic components in specific tasks deserves continuous reflection."

## Main Contributions

1. To the authors' knowledge, this is the first study of MLPMixer on the QbyE open-vocabulary keyword spotting task, formally introducing this pure MLP backbone from vision into the field of audio embedding learning, and proving it can serve as a strong alternative model for audio applications.
2. Proposes a simple and effective audio adaptation scheme (native direct input of MFCC without patching, feature/time dual-dimension mixing, Hardswish activation, time-dimension average pooling), achieving significant performance improvements relative to the original MLPMixer—among which the input representation and activation function are backed by complete six-scenario ablation data (Tables 4, 5).
3. Under the condition of having the fewest parameters (0.25M) and MACs (20.16M) overall (Table 1), it surpasses SOTA CNN and RNN baselines on Hey-Snips and the 400-speaker internal dataset, with significant advantages especially under difficult conditions of 10 dB, 6 dB noise, and far-field叠加 (stacking) (Hey-Snips absolute FRR reduction of 2.15 to 8.57 percentage points relative to the optimal baseline, Tables 2, 3).
4. Establishes a public evaluation benchmark for near-field and far-field (synthetic) six-scenario evaluations on the public Hey-Snips dataset, which can serve as a public foundation for subsequent open-vocabulary keyword spotting research.
5. Additional finding: The model can effectively compress useful information into an ultra-small 81-dimensional embedding space (compared to the ~1000-dimensional magnitude of baselines); this capability has positive spillover potential for other QbyE-related applications such as speaker verification and novelty detection.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Clean near-field is not comprehensively optimal**: Under the clean near-field condition of the internal dataset, the FRR is 2.68%, significantly worse than MobileNetV3's 0.62% and GRU-ATTN's 0.81% (Table 3); 10 dB near-field (3.71% vs 3.69%) is only tied. The model's advantageous scenarios are clearly limited to noise and far-field; the territory of "high-fidelity enrollment keyword detection in quiet rooms" still belongs to CNN/RNN.
- **Word length assumption of 1-second fixed window**: Training and inference are based on a 1-second benchmark window; how to handle longer custom wake phrases (e.g., multi-word slogans) is not reported by the paper; the embedding-level convolutional alignment is a coarse-grained solution, only allowing the enrollment template to translate within the query window, without comparison with finer alignment mechanisms such as DTW or local attention.
- **Time-dimension average pooling erases temporal structure**: Formula (3) directly averages 81 frames; the temporal dynamics within the word (phoneme order, rhythm) do not enter the embedding, potentially limiting the distinction upper bound for phonetically similar word pairs (words with reversed order or similar phonemes); the paper does not report specific word pair analysis of false acceptance samples.
- **Single global threshold**: The trigger decision uses a fixed cosine distance threshold; no mechanism for adaptively adjusting the threshold based on speaker, channel, or enrollment sample quality is discussed; threshold transfer when reusing across devices is not reported.
- **Embedding dimension not ablated**: 81 dimensions happens to equal the feature dimension, looking more like a design coincidence than a search result; the sensitivity of the embedding dimension and the capacity upper limit of 81 dimensions are not reported.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Evaluation scale is small and filtered**: The Hey-Snips evaluation subset was filtered to 40 speakers to meet the "same speaker enrollment plus query" protocol, with 7 queries per person, i.e., about 280 positive examples per scenario; an error on one utterance is worth about 0.36 percentage points, and a difference of 2 percentage points corresponds to only a few utterances; the paper does not report confidence intervals or significance tests.
- **Distortion is entirely simulated**: Far-field conditions are generated by convolution simulation according to [29], noise is taken from synthetic mixing of MS-SNSD, and the internal dataset is also clean recording with post-added noise; there is no verification in real far-field rooms or real echo devices; the gap between simulated far-field and real far-field is not discussed.
- **Single language, read-style training**: Training data consists only of English read audiobooks from LibriSpeech; generalization to spontaneous speech, heavy accents, and non-English scenarios is not reported.
- **Lack of edge-side actual measurement**: Only two proxy metrics, parameters and MACs, are reported; real-time latency, real-time factor, peak memory, and power consumption are not reported; the detection latency distribution corresponding to the 100-millisecond sliding window step is also not given.
- **Baseline tuning budget is unclear**: The proposed model underwent hundreds of ASHA hyperparameter searches; whether each baseline received a search budget of the same magnitude is not reported, and fairness cannot be completely ruled out; visual baselines rely on ImageNet pre-training as an extra resource, introducing resource inequality when compared with the proposed model trained from scratch.
- **Single enrollment protocol**: Fixed 3 enrollments, 7 queries; performance curves under 1 enrollment (one-shot) or more enrollment samples are not reported; enrollment and query always come from the same speaker, and cross-speaker enrollment scenarios for family shared devices are not evaluated.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The authors give two directions in the conclusion: first, to spill over the extraction capability of the small embedding space to other QbyE-related applications such as speaker verification and novelty detection; second, to continue reflecting on the idea of MLP and "mixing" itself, understanding the role each plays in achieving competitive performance. Combining the aforementioned limitations, the author believes there are several routes worth following: introducing differentiable DTW or lightweight local attention to perform fine-grained temporal alignment, replacing the current convolutional plus zero-padding scheme; re-verifying the six-scenario protocol on real far-field and multi-lingual data; supplementing structural ablations of embedding dimension, number of blocks, and hidden width, drawing the accuracy-overhead Pareto frontier; combining with metric learning losses (triplet, arcface) to test whether the embedding space shaped by cross-entropy still has free accuracy space; finally, the edge-side landing closed-loop—measuring latency and power consumption on real DSP after INT8 quantization, turning "minimum parameters and MACs" into perceptible product metrics.
