## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS, also known as spoken word detection) is the task of detecting specific words or multi-word phrases within continuous speech. It is widely used in speech data mining, audio indexing, and telephony routing. In recent years, the largest application driver has been voice assistants: wake-word mechanisms ("Okay Google", "Hey Siri", "Alexa") serve as the entry point for user interaction, and the accuracy of wake-word detection directly determines the upper limit of product experience. Thespecificity of the wake-up scenario lies in the fact that it is a persistent, all-day detection problem: devices need to continuously listen to audio streams and catch fleeting keywords under extremely low false alarm rates. This is far less tolerant of errors than general classification tasks.

KWS is closely related to Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), with the core difference being the scope of the target: KWS only cares about the detection accuracy of a small set of phrases, whereas ASR aims to transcribe all spoken words in the recording. This difference has led to a long-standing divergence in technical approaches—traditional KWS follows the path of small models that directly output detection scores, while ASR follows the path of large models that produce high-quality transcriptions. However, in recent years, sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) end-to-end (E2E) trained ASR models, particularly those in the RNN-T paradigm (Transformer-Transducer, Conformer-Transducer), have achieved State-of-the-Art (SOTA) levels in Word Error Rate (WER). This naturally raises a question: Can the quality dividends accumulated on the ASR path be transferred to the KWS task? This paper is Google's systematic answer to this question.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper reviews the deficiencies of existing solutions from two directions.

**First Route: Conventional Non-ASR KWS Systems**. Traditional methods use Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) as the backbone, with Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) characterizing acoustic features. This evolved into DNN frame-wise classification with posterior processing to generate confidence scores, CNNs modeling time-frequency relationships, RNNs capturing longer temporal dependencies, and finally E2E methods (streaming KWS with SVDF low-rank decomposition layers) that directly output detection scores using compact network topologies. The main deficiencies of this route are twofold: first, it requires forced alignment to produce frame-level training labels, and the alignment process itself introduces errors that propagate into the model; second, the temporal modeling capability of these models is limited by their network topology, making them less capable of capturing long-range contexts compared to seq2seq architectures.

**Second Route: Directly Applying General ASR to KWS**. The most straightforward approach is to train a T-T model to output both keywords and other spoken tokens simultaneously, and then check if the keyword string appears in the ASR output. The paper points out that this solution is suboptimal for three reasons:

1.  **Detection accuracy is easily skewed by minor ASR errors**. For example, recognizing "Okay Google" as "Okay GOOGL" causes string matching to fail. This problem is particularly severe for multi-word, multi-syllabic key phrases—the longer the phrase, the higher the probability of character-level errors accumulating; this is equally severe for ASR models using grapheme modeling, as grapheme-level outputs have no inherent tolerance mechanism for spelling errors.
2.  **Data sparsity**. The occurrence frequency of key phrases in training data is usually much lower than that of other spoken words (this shares the same root as the sparse word problem in E2E ASR), leading to insufficient modeling of keywords by the model and low detection accuracy. Intuitively: dozens of common words in a sentence share the vast majority of training signals, while keywords appear in only a few sentences, leading to a natural imbalance in gradient allocation.
3.  **Lack of flexible operating point adjustment**. String matching is a hard decision, unable to output continuous confidence scores, and thus cannot work by tuning thresholds to balance false alarms and misses.

Furthermore, previous seq2seq KWS explorations also had their own costs: methods using LSTM encoders with CTC decoders to build phoneme lattices require lattice search; methods based on N-best result indexing search (attention models) have expensive inference computation.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

How to transform a general T-T ASR system into a model truly adapted to the KWS task, while solving three sub-problems: (1) Make keyword detection robust to ASR character-level errors, no longer relying on word-for-word correct transcriptions; (2) Mitigate data sparsity by directly optimizing the keyword detection error rate at the training objective level; (3) Enable the model to output continuous confidence scores, supporting threshold adjustment according to application scenarios, and allowing fusion with conventional KWS systems to complement each other's strengths. An additional engineering requirement is that changes to the training pipeline when switching keywords should be minimal, and forced alignment should be avoided.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The skeleton of TT-KWS is the standard Transformer-Transducer: an audio encoder converts input features into acoustic embedding vectors, a label encoder converts text tokens into language embedding vectors, and a joint network receives both embeddings and outputs a probability distribution over a predefined set of text tokens. The specific configuration in this paper is: the audio encoder consists of Transformer blocks, the label encoder is an LSTM layer, the joint network is a fully connected layer, and the output is graphemes. The motivation for choosing T-T over attention-based seq2seq or CTC is streamability: the transducer structure natively supports frame-by-frame streaming decoding, which is a hard requirement for the persistent listening scenario of wake words; and the advantage of T-T over RNN-T is that the encoder replaces the unidirectional LSTM with self-attention, providing stronger long-range modeling capabilities, and has already been verified to achieve SOTA on ASR.

For two resource configurations (large and small), the hyperparameters of the Transformer blocks are shown in the original Table 1: the input feature projection for both configurations is 160 dimensions, the number of attention heads is 8, the head dimension is 64, and dropout is 0.1; for the large model, the two fully connected layers are 1024 and 256 dimensions, respectively, while for the small model, they are 128 and 32 dimensions. Note that the number of heads and head dimensions remain consistent across both scales, with differences concentrated in the width of the feed-forward layers—this indicates that model capacity scaling is mainly achieved through position-wise feed-forward sub-layers, while the topology of the attention sub-layers is treated as a structural constant.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**The main training objective is the RNN-T loss**. The transducer loss is the negative log-likelihood of the probability of all possible monotonic alignment paths between the input feature sequence $X$ and the output token sequence $Y$. The key property of this mechanism is that the model implicitly learns the alignment between acoustic and text, without requiring external forced alignment to provide frame-level labels—this directly eliminates the source of alignment errors in conventional KWS training.

**Keyword tokenization is the core modeling idea**. During training, every occurrence of the keyword in the transcription text is replaced with a special token `<kw>`, which becomes a member of the 75-output grapheme vocabulary (the vocabulary includes English letters, punctuation, `<kw>`, "$", and other special characters). In this way, the model treats the keyword audio segment as a coherent acoustic event as a whole during decoding, outputting a single `<kw>` instead of spelling out "Okay Google" character by character.

**MBR Training Loss** is adapted from Minimum Bayes-Risk (MBR) techniques. Let $H_{ij}$ be the $j$-th hypothesis for the $i$-th training sample, with probability score $P_{ij}$, and let $R_i$ be the reference transcription for all hypotheses. Count the number of `<kw>` tokens in $H_{ij}$ and $R_i$, denoted as $KH_{ij}$ and $KR_i$ respectively. Then, the insertion error (false positive) and deletion error (false negative) for the keyword token are calculated according to formula (1):

$$FP_{ij} = \max(0, KH_{ij} - KR_i), \quad FN_{ij} = \max(0, KR_i - KH_{ij})$$

The single-sample loss is calculated according to formula (2):

$$L_{ij} = P_{ij} \cdot (\alpha \cdot FP_{ij} + \beta \cdot FN_{ij}) / (KR_i + \epsilon)$$

where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ control the relative strength of the two components, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant to avoid numerical errors. The loss for the entire batch is calculated according to formula (3):

$$L = \sum_i \sum_j L_{ij} - \lambda \log P(Y|X)$$

where $-\log P(Y|X)$ is the negative log-likelihood of the reference transcription (referred to as RNN-T loss in the paper), and $\lambda$ is the regularization coefficient. The mathematical essence of this design is to rewrite the training objective from "maximizing transcription likelihood" to "minimizing expected detection error": using the probability $P_{ij}$ of each N-best hypothesis as a weight, it calculates the weighted sum of keyword-level false positives/negatives for that hypothesis. Beam search is used during training to generate N-best hypotheses, and the expected risk is calculated over the hypothesis space. It is worth noting that the paper specifically points out: for any single training sentence, at most one of $FP_{ij}$ and $FN_{ij}$ is non-zero (the number of keywords in the reference is fixed, so a hypothesis either over-detects or misses), but both have distributions across the entire training set, so the adjustment of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ acts at the global level. The division by $KR_i + \epsilon$ for normalization aims to make sentences with multiple keywords contribute a comparable loss magnitude to sentences with only one keyword. $\lambda \log P(Y|X)$ is retained as a regularization term to prevent the collapse of the main task's likelihood when optimizing the MBR objective alone.

**Inference Scoring**: The value of the `<kw>` token in the softmax output of the joint network is taken as the KWS score for that frame—this is a direct measure of the network's confidence in the keyword for the current frame. The score for the entire sentence is taken as the maximum value among all frame outputs in the sentence. The rationale for taking the max comes from the task setting: a keyword appears at most once in a sentence, and the maximum value corresponds to the moment the model is most confident in its detection. This is consistent with the posterior processing ideas of conventional KWS, but saves manual steps such as posterior smoothing.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: `<kw>` Special Token — Treating Keywords as a Coherent Acoustic Event

This is the most core "knife cut" in the entire paper. The motivation comes from observing the propagation of ASR errors: in string matching schemes, if any character inside the keyword is wrong, the entire word matching fails, and errors accumulate in an "AND" relationship; after tokenization, the detection problem becomes "does this special token appear", and the model is constrained to predict the acoustic contour of the keyword as a whole, absorbing character-level uncertainties inside the keyword into the acoustic modeling of the token. At the same time, this design alleviates data sparsity: all sentences containing keywords contribute training signals to the same `<kw>` token, regardless of what the specific keyword is; when switching keywords, one only needs to modify the transcription text and retrain, and even a model trained for one keyword can be used as a pre-trained model for another keyword. This idea draws inspiration from speaker turn detection work (using T-T to detect speaker changes to assist speaker diarization); the essence is "stuffing an event-level detection problem into a token sequence generation framework". There is also an engineering benefit: no forced alignment is needed to create frame-level labels, which not only saves alignment errors but also clears the way for federated learning—because running forced alignment on user data in a federated scenario is both expensive and has privacy costs.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: MBR Training Loss for KWS

The standard RNN-T loss optimizes the likelihood of the entire sentence transcription, where the keyword token is treated equally with ordinary characters in the loss—this is exactly how data sparsity manifests at the loss function level. The MBR loss directly targets the detection error rate: it calculates the expected false positive/negative rate on the N-best hypothesis space and minimizes it. The training process adopts a two-stage approach: first, train TT-KWS with RNN-T loss, then use this as a warm-start, and fine-tune with the combined RNN-T + MBR loss from formula (3), fixing the learning rate at $10^{-5}$. The empirical setting for hyperparameters is $\{\alpha, \beta, \lambda\} = \{1.0, 1.0, 0.01\}$, and the MBR loss is calculated on 4-best hypotheses generated by beam search (beam size 8). The reason for training with RNN-T first and then fine-tuning is practical: the MBR loss depends on the quality of N-best hypotheses, and hypotheses generated by an unconverged model are meaningless; cold-starting directly with MBR would optimize a garbage hypothesis space.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Softmax Confidence Scoring Replacing ASR Result Parsing

Unlike parsing ASR decoding results for string matching, this method directly takes the softmax value of `<kw>` as the score. This brings three benefits: the score is a continuous confidence measure of the model for the keyword, natively supporting threshold tuning to switch operating points; it does not require N-best or lattice search, and the inference computation is far lower than previous methods based on lattice/N-best indexing; the score is defined at the model output end, making the MBR optimization during training aligned with the scoring logic during inference (what is optimized during training is what is measured during inference).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to conventional E2E KWS (SVDF baseline): TT-KWS uses a seq2seq architecture to capture longer temporal dependencies, does not require forced alignment, switching keywords only requires changing the transcription text, and the model can reuse pre-training for new keywords. Compared to methods based on LSTM-CTC phoneme lattices: there is no need to build and search lattices. Compared to N-best indexing search methods: this paper uses softmax probabilities for direct scoring (originating from the same approach as E2E KWS), avoiding the computationally expensive N-best inference, and because this score is defined at the model output end, it allows for sequence discriminative optimization (MBR) of keyword tokens directly during training—this is a coupled design of "scoring method" and "training objective", which previous methods could not achieve. Compared to keyword bias methods (biasing general ASR towards specific keywords): this paper folds keywords into a single token rather than biasing word probabilities during decoding, resulting in a more thorough processing path. Compared to pure ASR baselines: for fair comparison, the paper also designed a bigram edit distance scoring for the ASR baseline—for each bigram in the hypothesis text, calculate its grapheme-level edit distance (ignoring spaces and case) with the two target keywords ("Hey Google", "Okay Google"), take the minimum distance $GED_{min}$, and use $e^{-GED_{min}}$ as the score (e.g., "Okay GOOGL" contains one deletion error, so the score is $e^{-1} \approx 0.37$), thereby extending the ASR system, which can only do exact matching hard decisions, into a system with scores capable of drawing DET curves. This scoring itself is also an engineering contribution, allowing ASR route evaluation to no longer be limited to a single operating point.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

All experiments use the same internal data, feature frontend, and data augmentation. The data consists of English speech collected by suppliers, covering various accents (American, Indian, British, Australian), various acoustic conditions (in-car, telephone voice, background noise), balanced gender, and balanced near-field and far-field audio distribution. The data is divided into positive and negative classes: positive sentences contain either the keyword "Hey Google" or "Okay Google" followed by a voice query; negative sentences do not contain any keywords, including about 15,000 so-called confusable sentences whose wording is phonetically similar to the target keywords. In terms of scale: the training set has 4300 hours of positive data (4 million sentences) plus 4000 hours of negative data (3.6 million sentences), and clean sentences are augmented with noise and reverberation using a room simulator, generating 25 augmented versions per sentence; the validation set has 700 hours of positive data plus 500 hours of negative data, used for training monitoring and checkpoint selection; the test set has 3800 hours of positive data plus 7000 hours of negative data, also including confusable sentences. Feature extraction uses 40-dimensional log-Mel filter bank energies with a 25ms window, concatenated every 4 frames, and downsampled every 3 frames, resulting in 160-dimensional feature vectors with a stride of 30ms input to the audio encoder. The total magnitude of training data is close to 8300 hours of original positive and negative data multiplied by 26 times augmentation, indicating that this system targets large resource configurations with sufficient data—this point is important for understanding the scope of applicability of the conclusions.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The main metric is the Detection Error Tradeoff (DET) curve, showing the false negative rate (FN) and false positive rate (FP) operating points as the detection threshold varies; the Equal Error Rate (EER, i.e., the point where FP equals FN) is also reported; and additionally, the FN at two low FP interest points is reported: the false negative rate when FP is 1% and 0.5%. The reason for choosing low FP points is clearly stated in the paper: in practical applications, it is better to accept a higher miss rate to suppress the false alarm rate, not the other way around—high false alarm rates will cause frequent false triggers of the wake-up, directly harming user experience. This metric design captures the asymmetric cost structure of the wake-word scenario, being closer to real product constraints than a single-point EER. Model configuration parameters are shown in Table 1 and the main text: the large configuration (ASR baseline large, TT-KWS large, TT-KWS+MBR large) has an audio encoder of 15 Transformer blocks, a label encoder of 128-dimensional LSTM, and a joint network that projects both sides to the same dimension embeddings, sums them, and then projects to a distribution of 75 output graphemes, with approximately 13 million parameters; the small configuration has 7 smaller Transformer blocks plus a 32-dimensional LSTM label encoder, with a total of 350,000 trainable parameters. For E2E baselines, the small configuration has 330,000 parameters (7-layer SVDF, first 4 layers encoding, last 3 layers decoding, encoding SVDF has 576 nodes per layer, memory 6, followed by a 64-dimensional projection layer, last encoding layer followed by a 32-dimensional projection; decoding SVDF has 32 nodes per layer, memory 24, followed by a 32-dimensional projection layer, last layer followed by a binary classification projection), and the large configuration upgrades the encoding SVDF to 4096 nodes and 128-dimensional projection (last encoding layer 16-dimensional projection), with the decoding side unchanged, totaling 3.9 million parameters.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table 2 summarizes the performance of each system at different operating points (EER / FN@1%FP / FN@0.5%FP): Baseline KWS small is 3.78% / 5.07% / 5.78%; ASR baseline small is 8.24%, with the last two columns being unreachable (the ASR baseline does not have an operating point that can reach 1% or 0.5% FP); TT-KWS small is 5.68% / 11.68% / 17.49%; TT-KWS+MBR small is 5.24% / 8.70% / 12.18%; Baseline KWS large is 3.38% / 4.34% / 4.91%; ASR baseline large is 3.55%; TT-KWS large is 3.37% / 4.35% / 5.01%; TT-KWS+MBR large is 3.27% / 4.17% / 4.78%; Fusion of the two systems (Fusion 2-best, score summation, combining Baseline KWS large and TT-KWS+MBR large) is 3.09% / 3.75% / 4.25%.

Conclusions at three levels:

**Under small model (low resource) conditions**, the conventional E2E SVDF KWS baseline is still the best (EER 3.78%, significantly better than all T-T small models), indicating that under a 350k parameter budget, compact architectures customized for detection tasks still have the advantage. However, within the T-T system, `<kw>` tokenization brings a 31% relative EER improvement (8.24% vs 5.68%), which is the direct benefit of changing the ASR model to tokenized output.

**Under large model conditions**, TT-KWS+MBR large (3.27%) is comprehensively optimal, with a 3.3% relative EER improvement over Baseline KWS large (3.38%), and both are significantly better than ASR baseline large (3.55%) at all operating points. The DET curve in Fig. 1 shows that TT-KWS+MBR large and Baseline KWS large perform similarly at all operating points; the point on the ASR baseline large curve in the figure marks the performance without bigram edit distance scoring (i.e., doing exact matching), intuitively demonstrating the value of the scoring method in extending ASR route evaluation.

**System Fusion**: Judging after adding the KWS scores of the two large models, the EER reaches 3.09%, an 8.6% improvement relative to Baseline KWS large, and a 5.5% improvement relative to TT-KWS+MBR large; the gains in the low FP region are larger, with FN dropping from 4.34% to 3.75% at 1% FP, a relative improvement of 13.6%. Fig. 1 shows that the fusion system consistently outperforms the two single systems at all operating points. This result proves that the errors of the two routes are complementary: conventional KWS and T-T models do not make the same batch of errors, providing space for fusion in high-precision requirement scenarios.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have an independently named ablation chapter, but the entire experimental design constitutes a clear step-by-step additive ablation chain, where each added component has a corresponding increment in Table 2:

1.  **Contribution of `<kw>` tokenization** (ASR baseline → TT-KWS, both T-T architecture, both using RNN-T loss, the only difference is whether the transcription replaces keywords): Small model EER drops from 8.24% to 5.68%, a relative improvement of 31%; large model EER drops from 3.55% to 3.37%. This verifies the core hypothesis of "modeling keywords as a coherent acoustic event as a whole"—with the same amount of parameters and the same data, simply changing the output representation brings such a large gain, indicating that the failure of the string matching scheme is mainly not a model capability issue but an output representation issue.
2.  **Contribution of MBR Loss** (TT-KWS → TT-KWS+MBR, the only difference is the fine-tuning loss): Small model EER goes from 5.68% to 5.24%, a relative improvement of 7.7%; more importantly, the gains in the low FP region are amplified—the FN at 1% FP is relatively reduced by 25.5% (11.68% vs 8.70%), and at 0.5% FP it is relatively reduced by 30.4% (17.49% vs 12.18%). Large model EER goes from 3.37% to 3.27%. The paper's explanation for this is: MBR minimizes the expected FN/FP rate during training, and this effect can transfer to unseen data; the DET curve in Fig. 2 further shows that MBR improves at every operating point, and the smaller the parameters, the more significant the gain, especially in the low FP operating region—the judgment drawn from this is that MBR training can concentrate modeling capacity on the main KWS task, and this "focusing" effect is greater when resources are limited. This is completely consistent with the numerical performance that "the relative gain of MBR under small models (7.7% EER, 25-30% low-region FN) is much larger than under large models (3.0% EER)": large models have excess capacity, so the marginal benefit of focusing is naturally small.
3.  **Contribution of Architecture Scale**: Going from small to large (350k → 13M parameters) reduces the EER of TT-KWS+MBR from 5.24% to 3.27%, an幅度 (magnitude) far exceeding the sum of the contributions of the two loss tricks, indicating that capacity is still primary on this route. And the conventional KWS baseline goes from small to large (330k → 3.9M) only from 3.78% to 3.38%, with much faster diminishing marginal returns—the difference in sensitivity to scale between the two architectures is an important basis for choosing the deployment route.

## Main Contributions

1.  **Proposed TT-KWS: A complete scheme for adapting T-T ASR systems to the KWS task**. The core is to replace keywords in the transcription with a special token `<kw>` during training, allowing the model to detect keyword audio segments as a single coherent acoustic event as a whole. This single change alone brings a 31% relative EER improvement for small models (Table 2), and fundamentally removes the dependency on ASR character-level correctness.
2.  **Proposed MBR Training Loss for KWS**. Transformed sequence discriminative training technology to directly minimize the expected false positive/negative rate on N-best hypotheses (formulas 1-3), reconfiguring training signals at the loss function level to address the data sparsity problem. It brings up to a 30.4% relative FN improvement in the low FP operating region (Table 2).
3.  **Established the inference scheme of softmax confidence scoring**: Taking the softmax value of the `<kw>` token, and taking the max for the entire sentence as the score, enabling the T-T route to support threshold-adjustable multi-operating point work, and aligning the training objective (MBR) with the inference metric.
4.  **Provided systematic comparison and fusion evidence for two technical routes**: Proved that TT-KWS and conventional E2E KWS errors are complementary, and score summation fusion consistently dominates at all operating points (EER 3.09%, up to 13.6% relative improvement in the low FP region, Table 2, Fig. 1), and provided engineering judgments for deployment分流 (diversion) according to device power supply conditions—battery-constrained devices use conventional small models, plugged-in devices (smart speakers, smart screens, car systems) use T-T large models, and high-precision requirement scenarios perform fusion.
5.  **Methodological spillover**: The design of switching keywords only requires changing the transcription text and no forced alignment, which also opens up the path for using old keyword models for new keyword pre-training, and federated learning training for KWS (mentioned as a potential in the paper, but not experimented).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

-   **Large model and decoding overhead**. The large configuration has approximately 13 million parameters (Section 5.3.2 of the main text), which is more than three times that of the conventional KWS large configuration of 3.9 million parameters; the paper's conclusion section admits itself that KWS adopting ASR technology usually requires large model sizes and computationally intensive decoding processes, making it difficult to deploy on mobile devices with limited battery and computing power. Under small model conditions, the performance is inferior to conventional KWS (EER 5.24% vs 3.78%, Table 2), so this route can currently only fall into the "plugged-in device" category, leaving the middle tier (persistent detection on high-end phones) blank.
-   **MBR training cost and hyperparameter sensitivity**. The MBR loss requires running beam search (beam 8) during training to take 4-best hypotheses, resulting in significantly higher training overhead than pure RNN-T; and it requires training RNN-T first and then warm-starting fine-tuning, lengthening the training process. Hyperparameters only report one set of empirical values $\{\alpha, \beta, \lambda\} = \{1.0, 1.0, 0.01\}$, and the paper does not report how this set of values was selected, nor does it report a sensitivity analysis for hyperparameters.
-   **Implicit assumptions of the scoring logic**. The sentence-level max scoring assumes that a keyword appears at most once in a sentence; in scenarios where keywords appear repeatedly in speech or the user wakes up twice in a row, the max scoring will lose count information. In addition, the score is the maximum value of frame-by-frame softmax, without any duration smoothing or posterior accumulation, and the paper does not discuss the robustness to instantaneous peak confidence.
-   **Only verified two fixed keywords**. "Hey Google" and "Okay Google" are both two-word, multi-syllabic, vowel-rich English phrases; the paper does not report any analysis on whether the benefit of `<kw>` tokenization varies with keyword length for shorter single-syllable keywords (where acoustic events are shorter and more easily confused). The 31% tokenization benefit was measured on a specific keyword set, and its extrapolation is questionable.
-   **The label encoder is still LSTM**. In the context of full-line Transformerization, using a 128-dimensional (or 32-dimensional) LSTM for the label encoder is a conservative choice; the paper did not try a Transformer label encoder or explain the reason for retaining LSTM (streamability and parameter count may be considerations, but this is speculation).

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

-   **All data is internally collected by suppliers**, with no public datasets used (such as Google Speech Commands), and the test set (3800 hours positive, 7000 hours negative) is not accessible, making the experiments completely irreproducible or horizontally comparable by others. The specific proportions of accents, acoustic conditions, and near/far-field in the data description are not reported.
-   **No efficiency metrics are reported**: There are no numbers for inference latency, real-time rate, memory usage, or energy consumption, while the conclusions revolve around deployment suggestions based on "whether resources are limited"—using "parameter count" as a proxy for "resources" is rough, especially since the training overhead of MBR is not quantified.
-   **No statistical significance analysis**. The gaps in all key comparisons (e.g., large model 3.27% vs 3.38%) are small, and the paper does not report confidence intervals or variance from multiple runs, making it impossible to judge whether the 3.3% relative improvement exceeds random fluctuation.
-   **Confusable negative samples are only about 15,000**, accounting for a very small proportion of the 3.6 million negative training sentences, while such samples are exactly the determining factor for performance in the low FP region; the paper does not report the separate error rate of confusable sentences in the test set, nor does it analyze how much of the MBR gain comes from the improvement of confusable samples.
-   **Incomplete comparison systems**. There is only textual comparison, no experimental comparison, with the keyword bias method of [22] and the recognizer-free E2E keyword search method of [21]; the fusion experiment only did the simplest way of score summation for two components, without exploring weighted fusion or ROVER-like methods.
-   **`<kw>` tokenization and MBR are evaluated in an additive manner**: The paper does not report the combination of "using only MBR without tokenization" (which is architecturally infeasible, but the contributions of tokenization and bigram edit distance scoring are not completely orthogonally decomposed—the edit distance scoring of the ASR baseline itself also absorbs some tolerance for character errors).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

-   **Compress the T-T route to fill the middle tier**: Distill, quantize, or prune the 13M parameter large model, with the goal of bringing the accuracy advantage of TT-KWS+MBR into the power budget of persistent detection on phones; the paper's conclusion explicitly points out that conventional small models are still the optimal option for battery-constrained devices, and this blank is the most direct follow-up work.
-   **Joint optimization of MBR and streaming training**: Currently, MBR uses beam search offline to generate hypotheses during training, which creates a training-inference mismatch with greedy decoding in streaming inference; defining MBR loss on streaming hypotheses (prefix beam) may be more consistent.
-   **Multi-keyword and open vocabulary expansion**: The conclusion section points out that plugged-in device scenarios can "increase the diversity of keyword phrases", naturally extending to expanding a single `<kw>` to multiple keyword tokens or a keyword embedding table, achieving multi-keyword detection with one training; this also aligns with product requirements for custom wake words.
-   **Federated learning implementation verification**: The paper proposes "simplifying the federated training path" as a side benefit of not requiring forced alignment, but there is no experimental support, making it a clear verification point to be done.
-   **Encoder upgrade**: Replace the audio encoder from Transformer blocks to Conformer-Transducer (the paper cites [4] but does not use it); convolution enhancement may be beneficial for modeling local patterns of short acoustic events like keywords.
-   **Refinement of scoring mechanism**: Replace the frame-by-frame max scoring with state-machine-style smoothing considering duration constraints (similar to posterior processing in conventional KWS), or output segment-by-segment scores for scenarios with multiple keyword occurrences, to eliminate the implicit assumption limitations of the current scoring.

---

**One-sentence summary**: This paper proves that "folding keywords into a special token + directly optimizing detection error rates with MBR loss" can transform a general Transformer-Transducer ASR system into a wake-up detector comparable to or even better than a dedicated KWS system (large model EER 3.27% vs 3.38%, 3.09% after fusion, Table 2), at the cost of more than three times the parameters and heavier decoding; its greatest value is not just the numbers themselves, but providing a feasible recipe for "importing ASR quality dividends into KWS", and deployment judgments based on device power supply conditions—leave small models for battery devices, let T-T take over for plugged-in devices, and rely on fusion of the two routes for highest precision.
