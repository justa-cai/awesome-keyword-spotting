## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Wake word detection (also known as keyword spotting, KWS) is the always-on voice entry point for devices such as smart speakers, mobile phones, and televisions: the device listens to the audio stream around the clock and only activates the full voice assistant upon detecting the wake word (Section 1). Unlike academic benchmarks, this is a scenario where "the model must run resident on edge chipsets," and the engineering constraints are triple-fold—model size, runtime memory footprint, and power consumption must all be minimized, while accuracy must not degrade (Section 1). The paper uses CPU cycles as a proxy for power consumption (Footnote 4), which is standard practice in edge engineering: in the absence of whole-system power bench data, cycles are approximately proportional to dynamic power.

More critically, the authors highlight three implicit constraints often ignored by the research community for commercially viable KWS (Section 1, items a/b/c). First, sub-8-bit quantization requires genuine hardware instruction set support—it is not enough to store weights as 4-bit values in software; the chip must have corresponding efficient matrix-vector multiplication instructions. Second, the model must run in streaming mode: under low-latency requirements, large buffers cannot be used, and the "accumulate a batch of frames for offline inference" mode is unavailable; every frame must yield posterior probabilities immediately upon arrival. Third, the model cannot use corruptible memory: the model runs continuously, and if its internal state enters a bad state (e.g., persistent contamination caused by numerical overflow), the wake-up function fails entirely, forcing the user to restart the device. The third constraint directly explains the paper's architectural choice—the baseline is a state-free, 250K-parameter feedforward network (Abstract, Section 2), where each decision depends only on the current input window, without carrying recursive state across frames. State-free models are naturally self-healing: if one frame is anomalous, the next starts from scratch; whereas in recursive structures, once the state is contaminated, it persists, directly violating the third red line.

The hardware-side reality is more specific (Section 3). In 2021, over 90% of mobile phones used ARM architecture chips, the vast majority of which include NEON SIMD extensions (available by default on armv8a/aarch64). NEON uses 128-bit registers for vector parallelism: one register can hold 4 32-bit floats, 4 32-bit integers, 8 16-bit integers, or 16 8-bit integers, performing an operation on all scalars within the register simultaneously per clock cycle. The hard limit is that NEON's minimum container for MAC (multiply-accumulate) operations is 8-bit. This means compressing weights from 8-bit to 4-bit or 5-bit does not provide faster multiplication instructions for NEON, nor does it save runtime memory—the benefits of sub-8-bit quantization on general-purpose chips are indirect (elaborated in Section 6.2: leaving overflow headroom for accumulators, allowing lower-precision accumulators, and reducing OTA distribution size). To directly reap the memory benefits of sub-8-bit weights, one must rely on existing neural network accelerators that support low-bit weights; the paper cites datasheets from Maxim MAX78000 and Syntiant NDP120 for benefit projections (Section 3, Footnotes 7/8). Thus, the contribution of this paper spans two hardware tiers: empirical measurements on general-purpose ARM NEON (CPU, memory, model size) and projections on dedicated accelerators (memory).

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper approaches from the spectrum of compression techniques (Section 1): knowledge distillation, low-rank approximation, and efficient architecture design are three well-explored routes, while quantization is the fourth orthogonal approach—converting various model components from 32-bit floats to lower-bitwidth representations, theoretically stackable with the other three. The paper clearly outlines the chain of insufficiencies in quantization itself:

- **Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) causes significant accuracy drop** (Citation [9]). Full precision during training, followed by direct truncation during deployment, leaves quantization errors uncompensated, which is particularly fatal for small models.
- **Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) has pushed bit-widths very low, but 1-bit precision remains an open challenge.** In the speech domain, 4-6 bit QAT has precedents in event detection (Citation [10]), speech recognition (Citation [16]), and KWS (Citation [11]); binarization (XNOR-Net, BinaryConnect lineage, Citations [12][13]) is mature in vision but causes severe accuracy drops when directly applied to KWS. Since quantization functions are discrete with gradients almost everywhere zero, QAT relies on pseudo-quantization with Straight-Through Estimator (STE, Citation [14]) or Gumbel-Softmax (Citation [15]) to propagate gradients—this is the foundation for all low-bitwidth training.
- **Research focuses on "model size vs. accuracy," bypassing the commercial triple constraints** (hardware support, streaming, non-corruptible memory). Many low-bitwidth paper conclusions cannot be realized on real chips.
- **Incomplete component coverage:** Most quantization works only handle weights (plus activations), leaving the four sets of BatchNorm parameters, gain, and bias in float. As a result, edge inference remains mixed-precision computation, discounting the advantages of integer compute power—Table 4 in this paper specifically ablates the cost of per-component quantization.
- **The fundamental mismatch between weight distribution and linear quantizers is not addressed head-on:** Network weights approximately follow a Gaussian distribution, concentrated around zero in a bell shape, while hardware MACs require linear (uniform step) quantizers that spread resolution evenly across the entire value range, wasting a large number of codewords in the Gaussian tails (Section 4.2). Strom et al.'s "Squashed Weight Distribution" (Citation [17]) addresses this problem directly; this paper adapts it to streaming KWS.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved

In one sentence: For a 250K-parameter, streaming, state-free commercial-grade KWS feedforward model, compress all components—weights, gain, bias, BN four sets of parameters, input features, activations—to 8 bits and below (weights down to 4 bits or even 1 bit), while achieving FDR@FRR parity with the full-precision model at the operating point on the DET curve; and empirically measure CPU and memory benefits on ARM NEON (Abstract promises: up to 3x CPU improvement, over 4x memory improvement), and project 4-7x memory/model size reduction on existing accelerators (Section 7). The difficulty lies in the simultaneous validity of "all components" and "parity": quantizing weights can rely on distribution shaping, but quantizing parameters with large dynamic ranges and continuous drift during training, such as BN, lacks ready-made good solutions; activation values are unbounded and their distribution drifts with input, collapsing below 8 bits (Figure 2); binary weight models are sensitive to learning rates, dropping 14% in accuracy without special handling (Table 5).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The baseline model (Section 2) is a feedforward network with 250K learnable parameters, adopting a bottleneck architecture from Gao et al.'s frontend gain-invariant wake word modeling work (Citation [18]). This full-precision model has already been optimized for four goals: low power consumption, small footprint, streaming, and state-free; it is the production-grade form of Amazon Alexa—all comparisons in this paper use it as the baseline, rather than creating an academic baseline from scratch. The input features are 20-dimensional Log Mel Filter Bank Energies (LFBE), with a 25 ms analysis window and 10 ms frame shift; the model takes 81 frames as input and downsamples in time by 3x (equivalent time step approx. 30 ms). The network body consists of five fully connected layers, all hidden layers equipped with BatchNorm and ReLU; the output is a binary classification layer (wake word vs. non-wake word), trained with cross-entropy loss. During training, the wake word is always centered-aligned within the input window (Citation [21]), ensuring a fixed posterior peak position for positive samples, providing a stable alignment premise for the smoothing filter at inference. The optimizer is Adam. At inference, the wake word posterior first undergoes Windowed Moving Average (WMA) filtering, then passes through a threshold to obtain detection hypotheses. Architecture details are recorded as in Table 1: layer dimensions {87, 400, 87, 400, 87, 400}, 250K parameters (Table 1 lists six layer dimensions while the text mentions five fully connected layers, a slight discrepancy in口径, recorded as per original text).

Why choose FFN over CNN/RNN? The paper explicitly states that QAT algorithms can be applied to convolutional models, but the motivation for choosing FFN lies in the three commercial constraints in Section 1: state-free (no corruptible recursive state), streaming low latency (81-frame sliding window suffices for decision, no large buffer needed), and feedforward structure is most friendly to integer inference (the entire network is just a string of matrix-vector multiplications, allowing NEON SIMD to run at full capacity). Later, when exploring lower bitwidths, two larger models were extended with the same architecture specifications (changing layer dimensions, keeping the rest of the architecture unchanged, end of Section 2).

A key design in the overall computation graph of the quantized version: the gain $\alpha$ is placed outside the MAC. Formally, the quantized layer is written as $y_q \leftarrow \alpha_q(W_q \cdot x_q + b_q)$—the dot product $W_q \cdot x_q$ is completed in the pure low-bit integer domain, with the scalar gain multiplied outside. Why is this necessary? If each weight element carries its own scaling (a classic approach for non-uniform quantization), vectorized integer multiply-accumulate is broken down into element-wise scaling multiplications, reducing SIMD throughput to zero. Folding the scaling into a single scalar per layer and placing it outside the MAC allows the dot product to run at full density using integer instructions. This is engineering wisdom inherited from the XNOR-Net lineage, extended here from binary to 4/5/8 bits.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The form of a standard feedforward layer (Equation 1):

$$y = \psi_{bn}(\phi_{relu}(Wx + b))$$

where $x$ is the dense layer input, $W$ is the weight matrix, $b$ is the bias, and $\phi_{relu}$ and $\psi_{bn}$ are the ReLU and BatchNorm transformations, respectively. Introducing gain $\alpha$ and a linear quantizer $q_z(\cdot)$ that discretizes values into $z$ levels ($\log_2(z)$ bits), the quantized inference of the entire layer proceeds in three steps (Section 4.1):

$$y_q \leftarrow \alpha_q(W_q \cdot x_q + b_q)$$
$$y_q^{(\phi)} \leftarrow q_z(\phi_{relu}(y_q))$$
$$y_q^{(\phi,\psi_q)} \leftarrow q_z(\psi_{bn}(y_q^{(\phi)}))$$

Note that the order of these three steps exactly corresponds to the calculation order in Equation 1: the dense layer (with external gain) outputs first in the integer domain, then the quantized ReLU output enters the quantized BN. There is no floating-point element-wise operation anywhere along this chain, which is the mathematical meaning of "full-component quantization."

Training is divided into two stages (Section 4.1). In the first stage, a tanh non-linear quantization method is used to handle weights (adapted from Citation [17]); in the second stage, linear quantization is applied to all other components—gain ($\alpha_q$), bias ($b_q$), BN ($q_z(\psi_{bn}(\cdot))$), activation ($q_z(\phi_{relu}(\cdot))$), input ($x_q$)—followed by a short training round to re-estimate parameters (Section 4.3 begins by saying one epoch, Section 6.2 says 35K updates). Since the quantization function is discrete with gradients almost everywhere zero, gradients are propagated using the STE straight-through estimator (methodological premise stated in Section 1).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Weight Distribution Shaping via tanh Non-linear Transformation (Stage 1)

This is the largest source of accuracy gain in the paper. Problem statement (Section 4.2): Linear quantization is widely used in model compression, but network weights typically follow a Gaussian distribution, making linear quantization inefficient for Gaussian distributions. Why? A linear quantizer uniformly divides $[-1,1]$ into $z$ parts, each with one codeword. Under a Gaussian distribution, over 90% of weights are squeezed into a narrow band around zero, yet they only receive a few central codewords; the long tails on both sides have dense codewords but almost no weight points. This is equivalent to using most of the precision to represent values that never appear.

The solution is not to make the quantizer non-uniform (which would break the uniform grid assumption of hardware integer MACs), but rather to **shape the weight distribution into a shape the quantizer likes**: first apply tanh to $W$, then linearly quantize, i.e., $W_q \leftarrow q_z(\tanh(W))$ (Section 4.2). tanh compresses the long tails and lifts the wings of the distribution, "flattening" the near-Gaussian distribution into a distribution close to $U(-1,1)$ (Figure 1(a) shows the original near-Gaussian weight distribution, Figure 1(b) shows the flattened distribution after tanh). This way, the weight mass carried by each codeword is approximately equal, minimizing quantization error.

However, simply applying a layer of tanh at the quantization moment is not enough: applying tanh to an already trained Gaussian distribution will squeeze a large number of weights into a few codewords in the steep region around zero, and the saturation region will reuse the same codeword for tail weights,打折ting the shaping effect. Therefore, the approach is to **train the weights to "grow" into a shape that is uniform after tanh** (Section 4.2): weights are initialized with a target distribution $N(0, \sigma_t^2)$, and a regularization term is added during training:

$$\lambda_\sigma(\sigma_w - \sigma_t)^2 + \lambda_\mu \mu_w^2$$

penalizing deviations of the actual weight standard deviation $\sigma_w$ and mean $\mu_w$ from the target. The mathematical intuition: if $W \sim N(0,\sigma_t^2)$ and $\sigma_t$ is chosen appropriately, the distribution of $\tanh(W)$ approximates $U(-1,1)$—the probability integral of a Gaussian passing through tanh exactly flattens the bell shape. The regularization term ensures that weights do not drift out of this sweet spot throughout training. The values of the regularization coefficients $\lambda_\sigma$ and $\lambda_\mu$ are not reported in the paper.

The effect has a separate ablation (Section 6.1, Table 2): under the setting of 5-bit dense layer weights, other components not quantized, and 100K training updates, the model with tanh shows a relative FDR degradation of 7.4%, while the linear quantization without tanh shows 12.3% degradation (VAL set, baseline is 0.0)—with the same quantizer, relying solely on distribution shaping cuts the degradation by nearly 40%.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Full Parameter Quantization of BatchNorm with Scaling Factor

BN has four sets of parameters: $\gamma$, $\beta$, $\mu_B$, $\sigma_B^2$ (Section 4.3). $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are trainable parameters, while $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ are sliding estimates of batch statistics during training and are replaced by global estimates during inference. This paper quantizes all four sets—why emphasize "all"? Because BN on the inference end must be calculated in the integer domain; leaving any set in float means the chip must have floating-point units participating in this hottest computation path, and mixed precision will leak the integer compute power advantages accumulated by previous weight quantization.

The difficulty lies in $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$: the paper cites Banner et al.'s 8-bit training work (Citation [23]) pointing out that these two types of parameters have large dynamic ranges and observes that they drift continuously during training. For values with large dynamic ranges, fixed-bit linear quantization results in a large absolute span per codeword, inevitably leading to poor resolution. The solution is to introduce a scaling factor $C_{BN}$, dividing $x$, $\mu_B$, and $\sigma_B^2$ by it before entering the quantizer (Equation 2):

$$x_{norm} = \frac{x - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2}} = \frac{x/C_{BN} - \mu_B/C_{BN}}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2/C_{BN}^2}}$$

$$x_{norm_q} \leftarrow \frac{q_z(x/C_{BN}) - q_z(\mu_B/C_{BN})}{\sqrt{q_z(\sigma_B^2/C_{BN}^2)}}$$

The brilliance lies in algebraic identity: dividing numerator and denominator by $C_{BN}$ (dividing by $C_{BN}^2$ inside the square root in the denominator) leaves the mathematical result of normalization unchanged, but each number entering the quantizer is compressed into a narrow dynamic range, thereby improving quantization resolution. This is a standard technique of "moving the numerical range into the quantizer's sweet spot without changing the network function," analogous to the tanh in the first stage—both modify the numerical distribution to adapt to hardware, rather than modifying hardware to accommodate the distribution. Afterwards, $\gamma_q \leftarrow q_z(\gamma)$, $\beta_q \leftarrow q_z(\beta)$, and the quantized BN output is $q_z(\psi_{bn}(x_{norm_q})) \leftarrow \gamma_q \cdot x_{norm_q} + \beta_q$.

The benefit is quantified in the Table 4 ablation: under the full 8-bit setting, "weights + BN (with dynamic scaling, denoted BN1 in Table 4)" shows a relative FDR degradation of 5.3%, while "weights + BN (without dynamic scaling, denoted BN2 in Table 4)" shows 9.0% degradation (TEST set)—one scaling factor recovers nearly 4 percentage points of relative degradation.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Activation Function Bounding and BN/Activation Operator Reordering

The trouble with activation quantization (Section 4.3): ReLU is unbounded on the positive half-axis, and the linear quantizer must cover a theoretically infinite value range; in practice, this can only be done by truncating a large range, resulting in extremely poor unit resolution. The authors' initial experiments with direct linear quantization of ReLU caused significant accuracy drops. They systematically tried alternatives: bounded clipped ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, and smoother GeLU, SiLU (Citations [24][25]). The conclusion: clipped ReLU is the most promising; GeLU and SiLU did not yield definitive conclusions; sigmoid and tanh performed worse than directly quantizing ReLU—the authors speculate this is due to gradient saturation requiring more training updates. After selecting clipped ReLU, its output passes through the linear quantizer to get $q_z(\phi_{relu}(\cdot))$. Why is bounded activation so important for quantization? An 8-bit unsigned number has only 256 levels; a bounded value range means these 256 levels are effectively utilized; an unbounded activation is forced to reserve a large number of codewords for rare large values. The specific value of the clip threshold is not reported in the paper.

The second finding is operator order: in the quantization scenario, the order of BN and activation mattered. The original order was dense→ReLU→BN (Equation 1); the paper explicitly states they swapped the order of BN and clipped ReLU to become dense→BN→clipped ReLU (end of Section 4.3). The paper itself does not elaborate on the mechanism, but combining context allows for a reasonable inference: after swapping, the input to clipped ReLU is the BN-normalized quantity—a stable distribution with zero mean and variance controlled by $\gamma$, making threshold selection robust; the block output becomes the bounded output of clipped ReLU, which aligns perfectly with the 8-bit unsigned quantization bucket. If ReLU is kept first, clip must act on the unnormalized, scale-drifting dense output during training, making the threshold fragile. This is consistent with the philosophy of Innovation 2: letting each quantizer face a disciplined distribution.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Input Quantization and Two-Stage Training Process

Input side (end of Section 4.3): LFBE features first undergo global mean-variance normalization, then using the same technique as processing BN parameters, $x_{input_q} \leftarrow q_z(x_{input}/C_{input})$—again, "shrink dynamic range first, then quantize." The second stage of training runs only one round (one epoch, 35K updates, Section 4.3, Section 6.2) to re-estimate parameters.

Why is the entire process divided into two stages rather than done all at once? The paper does not explicitly state the reason, but two layers of motivation can be read from the experimental design. First, error attribution: the first stage only quantizes weights (the experiments in Section 6.1 explicitly state "other components not quantized"), allowing the effect of tanh shaping to be measured separately (7.4% vs 12.3% in Table 2); the second stage then adds quantization component by component (row-by-row ablation in Table 4). The two-stage structure makes the cost of each step traceable, which is a necessary chain of evidence for commercial model release decisions (how many bits to compress, which components to quantize). Second, training stability: weight shaping relies on target distribution initialization plus regularization constraints, and this modification perturbs training dynamics, requiring a stable backbone composed of full-precision components to support it; only after the weight distribution is定型 (solidified) are the other components subjected to gentle linear quantization and short training re-estimation, minimizing the disruption surface.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

The difference with PTQ is self-evident: quantization error is perceived during training, and parameters are re-estimated using 26K hours of data. The difference with existing low-bit QAT (event detection [10], ASR [16], KWS [11]) lies in coverage and validation metrics: this paper quantizes "all components"—including the most difficult BN four sets of parameters and gain, bias, not just weights plus activations; validation is done on production-scale data (26K hours training, 4K hours evaluation) plus real instruction sets (ARM NEON empirical CPU/memory), not just accuracy curves. The relationship with binarization methods (XNOR-Net [12], BinaryConnect [13]) is inheriting the integerization idea of external gain, but with a wider bitwidth spectrum (1/4/5/8), and providing complete data on "compensating accuracy by expanding parameters" at the binary tier (Table 5). The relationship with Strom et al. [17] is adaptation and extension: the tanh weight shaping technique originates from that paper (same group of authors); this paper moves it from general models to streaming state-free KWS, and配套 (supports) the quantization of BN, activation, and input—the first stage is just the foundation, the second stage is the incremental main body of this paper. It is completely orthogonal to the three routes of knowledge distillation, low-rank, and efficient architecture, and can be stacked (Section 1).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

All experiments are done on de-identified production datasets (emphasized at the beginning of Section 5). The training set consists of 26K hours of fully annotated audio, mixing far-field device audio and near-field mobile phone audio (Abstract, Section 5). Evaluation uses two sets: a validation set VAL of approx. 4K hours and an independent test set TEST of approx. 4K hours, both containing far-field and near-field mobile phone audio; TEST was collected from a wider range of commercial devices (Section 5). Why cut it this way? Far-field reverberation, noise, and multi-microphone effects are the real killers of wake words; production-distribution data can withstand commercial judgment; TEST uses a wider device range to test cross-device generalization—microphones and channels not seen during training. Specific wake word content, positive/negative sample ratios, and language are not reported in the paper (commercially sensitive).

### Definition and Rationale of Evaluation Metrics

The WMA smoothing parameter at inference is tuned on held-out data (not tuned on the test set to prevent leakage). Performance is measured using the DET curve: the x-axis is the False Rejection Rate (FRR) (wake word spoken but not awakened), and the y-axis is the False Detection Rate (FDR) (not spoken but awakened) (Section 5). Following the practice of Citations [18][26][21], DET coordinates are normalized, and relative FDR is reported. For brevity, most experiments only report "relative FDR degradation at the baseline model operating point FRR," where the baseline operating point corresponds to a relative FRR/FDR of 1.0 (Section 5).

Why choose this metric instead of accuracy? Wake word is a streaming binary classification detection task, naturally producing an FRR-FDR trade-off curve rather than a single-point accuracy; the costs of the two errors are extremely asymmetric (false rejection harms usability, false alarm harms trust and cloud costs), so the entire curve must be viewed. "Fixing FRR and comparing FDR" decouples accuracy from threshold selection—the threshold constraint in commerce is usually "false rejection rate must not exceed a certain value," making comparison of false alarms at this anchor point fair. Relative degradation normalization allows direct horizontal comparison of numbers across different model scales. Figure 2 provides a pair of complete DET curves (8-bit input activation baseline blue line vs. 5-bit orange line, TEST set), showing that the 5-bit tier shows significant degradation across the entire curve rather than a single-point drift.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Model Matrix (Table 1): M0 full precision (32-bit weights/parameters/activations/inputs, 250K parameters); M1 weights 8-bit, other parameters (gain/bias/BN) 8-bit, activations/inputs 16-bit; M2 all 8-bit; M3 weights 5-bit, others 8-bit; M4 weights 4-bit, others 8-bit. All models have 250K parameters, layer dimensions {87, 400, 87, 400, 87, 400}. Training volume: baseline 500K updates; two-stage QAT is 500K for stage 1 plus 35K for stage 2 (Section 6.2).

Accuracy (Table 3, TEST set, relative FDR degradation, baseline M0 is 0.0): M1 is 3.1%, M2 is 3.5%, M3 is 7.4%, M4 is 8.7%. Reading: from full precision to "full 8-bit + 16-bit activation input" (M0→M1) costs only 3.1% relative degradation, which simultaneously includes triple compression of weights 32→8, other parameters 32→8, and activations/inputs 32→16; compressing activations/inputs from 16 to 8 (M1→M2) costs only an additional 0.4 percentage points (3.1%→3.5%); compressing weights from 8 to 5 or 4 (M2→M3/M4) degrades by 7.4%/8.7%, still flat. There are no cliffs on the entire compression curve, which is the direct result of two-stage QAT plus distribution shaping.

CPU (Table 3, MCPS, million cycles per second): M0 is 37.6; M1 is 18.8 (saving 50% relative to baseline, from 32-bit to 8/16-bit parameters); M2 is 10.3—a further 45% reduction relative to M1, because the 128-bit NEON register holds 8 8-bit numbers for multiplication, with throughput 2x that of 8 16-bit numbers (Section 6.2). Note that the CPU for M3 and M4 is still 10.3: NEON's minimum container for MAC is 8-bit, so reducing weights further in bits does not yield cycle savings (Section 6.2). Calculating directly from Table 3 numbers, M0→M2 is 37.6/10.3 ≈ 3.65x CPU improvement (the口径 in the Abstract and Conclusion is written as "up to 3x").

Memory and Model Size (Table 3): M0 runtime memory 912 KB, model 912 KB; M1/M2/M3/M4 memory is uniformly 228 KB—a 75% reduction relative to baseline, and runtime memory equals model size, which is the dividend of state-free streaming FFN: no recursive state, no large buffer, a clean memory story. But M3/M4 weights are already 5/4 bits, yet memory remains unchanged, for the same reason as above: NEON's minimum data type is 8-bit, sub-8-bit weights do not save runtime memory (Section 6.2). Model size decreases linearly with bits: M3 142 KB, M4 114 KB (compared to M2's 228 KB). So why do sub-8-bit weights on NEON? The paper gives two reasons (Section 6.2): first, to leave headroom for the accumulation of weights, inputs, and activations, reducing the probability of overflow and allowing lower-precision accumulators—when performing 16-way 8-bit multiply-accumulate, full 8-bit operands easily hit the accumulator's representation range, and sub-8-bit weights leave a safety margin from the source; second, smaller model size facilitates over-the-network deployment distribution.

Binary Weight Models (Section 6.3, Table 5, TEST set): Other parameters, inputs, and activations remain 8-bit, only weights are 1-bit. Two models: M5 has 500K parameters (2x baseline), layer dimensions {123, 566, 123, 566, 123, 566}, first, second, and last layer weights are 5-bit, remaining layer weights are 1-bit; M6 has 1.5M parameters (6x baseline), layer dimensions {1080, 566, 123, 566, 123, 566}, all layer weights are 1-bit. Why expand parameters? 1-bit weights drop information content per parameter drastically, using width to exchange for capacity, and mixed precision (5-bit for key layers) adds another boost. Results: at the end with large learning rate, M5 degrades 14.0%, M6 degrades 6.2%; after manually reducing the learning rate in the last few updates of the first stage, M5 degrades 0.4%, M6 is −0.4% (better than baseline, i.e., parity), with learning rate adjustments recovering 13.6 and 6.6 percentage points of degradation respectively (Section 6.3). Regarding memory, model sizes are M5 128 KB, M6 198 KB; projected memory usage on existing neural network accelerators equals model size, corresponding to 86.0% and 78.3% memory reduction (Table 5)—the conclusion口径 is the "projected 4 to 7x memory and model size reduction" in Section 7 (912/198≈4.6, 912/128≈7.1).

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The four groups of ablations are interlinked and constitute the most engineering-valuable part of the paper's evidence chain.

**With/Without tanh (Table 2, VAL, 5-bit weights, 100K updates, other components not quantized)**: With tanh degradation 7.4%, without 12.3%. Distribution shaping is worth nearly 5 percentage points, which is the entire reason for the existence of Stage 1.

**Component-by-component addition of quantization to 8 bits (Table 4, TEST, 100K updates, all components fixed at 8 bits)**: Quantizing only weights 3.8%; adding bias and gain 4.7%; adding input activations 5.2%; adding BN (with dynamic scaling BN1) 5.3%. The marginal cost of each layer of superposition is within 1 percentage point—"full component 8-bit" is almost free. The most striking is the BN2 row (BN quantization without dynamic scaling) with 9.0% degradation: compared to BN1's 5.3%, the scaling factor $C_{BN}$ alone recovers 3.7 percentage points, which is the highest leverage among all single-point tricks. This also explains in reverse why many works leave BN in float—quantizing directly without scaling does indeed cause accuracy drops, but this paper proves that if done correctly, it does not drop.

**Activation/Input 8→5 bits (Figure 2, TEST)**: 16→8 bits is almost free (M1→M2 only 3.1%→3.5%), but 8→5 bits shows significant accuracy drop, with the entire DET curve degrading. **Parameters 8→5 bits (Section 6.2 Ablation 3)**: Compressing bias, gain, and BN parameters from 8 to 5 bits increases relative FDR degradation by only 3.0 percentage points. Together, these reveal an asymmetry: **weights and static parameters can be compressed further from 8 bits, but activations and inputs hit a wall at 8 bits**. Why the asymmetry? Weights are static and can be shaped (tanh plus regularization) during training into the sweet spot shape of the quantizer; the distribution of activations drifts frame-by-frame with input data, and can only be indirectly disciplined during training via bounding (clipped ReLU); the 32 levels of 5 bits cannot hold its dynamics, and information is truncated in the pipeline (this mechanism is the author's inference based on paper observations, not elaborated in the paper itself).

**Learning Rate Sensitivity of Binary Models (Table 5)**: Under constant Adam strategy, M5/M6 degrade 14.0%/6.2% respectively; only manually reducing the learning rate in the last few updates of Stage 1 brings it back to 0.4%/−0.4%. Why is it so effective? Binarization retains only the sign; if there are still large step updates at the end of training, weight signs flip repeatedly near critical points, causing the quantized network to disconnect from the shadow weights; small learning rate allows the sign pattern to settle first before entering Stage 2 (mechanism is inference). This ablation is a specific counterexample to the domain consensus that "binary model accuracy is still a challenge": the challenge may partly lie in the training schedule, not entirely in the method itself.

## Main Contributions

- **Proposed and verified a two-stage full-component sub-8-bit QAT algorithm**: Stage 1 tanh non-linear weight distribution shaping (adapted from Citation [17]), Stage 2 linear quantization of gain, bias, BN four sets of parameters, activations, and inputs, covering components that previous works generally left in float.
- **Achieved operating point parity with the full-precision model on 26K hours of production data**: Full 8-bit tier relative FDR degradation is only 3.5%, 4-bit weight tier 8.7% (Table 3); binary weights plus expanded parameter models reach 0.4%/−0.4% after learning rate adjustment (Table 5).
- **Provided a system-level benefit ledger on real instruction sets**: Empirical CPU 37.6→10.3 MCPS, memory 912→228 KB, model minimum 114 KB on ARM NEON (Table 3), and clarified the counter-intuitive conclusion that "sub-8-bit weights on NEON do not yield CPU/memory benefits, but yield overflow headroom and distribution volume."
- **Provided quantization comparison for BN dynamic range scaling quantization ($C_{BN}$) (9.0%→5.3%, Table 4) and activation function selection conclusions (clipped ReLU wins, operator order must be swapped)**, which are engineering recipes that can be directly copied.
- **Bridged the landing projection of binary weight models on existing accelerators**: M5/M6 maintain accuracy under 86.0%/78.3% memory reduction (Table 5), verifying that 1-bit weight KWS is usable after compensating with expanded parameters.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Sub-8-bit weights have no CPU/memory benefits on general NEON platforms** (Table 3: M2/M3/M4 are all 10.3 MCPS, 228 KB); benefits exist only in three indirect dimensions: preventing overflow, low-precision accumulators, and OTA volume. To truly reap memory dividends, one must switch to dedicated accelerators, for which the paper only did projections.
- **The quantization lower bound for activations and inputs is 8 bits**: Figure 2 shows significant accuracy drop when compressed to 5 bits. The method only applies bounded clipping to activations, lacking distribution shaping techniques, asymmetric to the tanh on the weight side—the bit lower bound of the full model is stuck by activations.
- **Binary tier relies on parameter expansion**: M5/M6 require 2x and 6x parameters (500K/1.5M) to catch up in accuracy (Table 5); the 1.5M parameter 1-bit model has higher training costs and Stage 2 re-estimation costs than the 250K 8-bit model, and benefits depend on the accelerator actually storing weights by bits.
- **The two-stage process introduces multiple manual hyperparameters**: Target distribution $\sigma_t$, regularization coefficients $\lambda_\sigma$/$\lambda_\mu$, clip threshold, values of $C_{BN}$/$C_{input}$, timing and magnitude of learning rate adjustment for binary models—the paper does not report specific values or selection methods, requiring independent parameter search for reproduction.
- **The method is only verified on FFN architecture**: The paper states that the QAT algorithm can be applied to convolutional models but did not experiment (beginning of Section 2); it is questionable whether it is equally effective for current mainstream TC-ResNet/BC-ResNet style temporal convolution KWS architectures—weight distribution characteristics and activation statistics of convolutional layers differ from dense layers.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **No horizontal comparison with external methods**: All tables only compare with their own full-precision baseline M0, without battling against low-bit KWS QAT from Mishchenko et al. [11] or other 4-6 bit QAT works on the same or comparable data, making "superiority" impossible to judge.
- **Accelerator benefits are paper projections**: Memory is calculated based on MAX78000/NDP120 datasheets (Section 3, Section 6.3), without testing latency, power, and accuracy on real accelerators—whether sub-8-bit has accuracy drift on dedicated hardware (due to different rounding behaviors) is unverified.
- **Power is proxied by CPU cycles** (Footnote 4), with no empirical energy data; memory benefits do not account for runtime miscellany such as feature buffers and post-processing.
- **Data and task details are closed**: Wake word content, positive/negative sample ratios, data language, and CL values are not publicly disclosed; production data is not reproducible; Figure 2 only gives curves without numerical tables, making "significant accuracy drop" impossible to quantify and cite.
- **No variance or confidence interval reported**: The statistical fluctuation of single-training relative FDR numbers (e.g., M6's −0.4%) on the 4K hour evaluation set is unknown, and the "parity" conclusion lacks significance support.
- **M5's mixed-precision layer selection (first, second, last layers 5-bit) lacks ablation support** (Table 5); the paper does not explain or verify why these three layers are most sensitive.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Distribution shaping on the activation side**: Transplant the tanh idea from the weight side to activations—for example, learnable per-layer activation ranges (LSQ-style methods), automatic search for activation quantile clipping, pushing the 8-bit lower bound of activations down to align with weight bitwidths.
- **Automated mixed precision**: M5's layer selection in this paper is manual; it can be extended to a bit allocation search based on per-layer sensitivity (which layers are 4-bit, which are 8-bit, which are 1-bit), minimizing weighted memory objectives under accuracy constraints.
- **Real-machine verification loop**: Empirically test end-to-end latency, energy, and accuracy on NDP120/MAX78000, replacing datasheet projections; simultaneously verify the consistency of dedicated hardware rounding semantics with QAT simulation.
- **Architecture extrapolation**: Move the two-stage QAT to temporal convolution and streaming attention KWS architectures, testing whether tanh shaping is equally effective for convolutional kernel weight distributions.
- **Stacking with orthogonal compression routes**: The paper explicitly states that quantization is orthogonal to distillation and low-rank (Section 1); binary weight models plus distillation teachers (higher-precision large models) may further suppress binarization errors, reducing dependence on parameter expansion.
- **Automation of regularization and learning rate schedules**: Annealing strategies for $\lambda_\sigma$/$\lambda_\mu$, and timing of learning rate decay at the end of binary models, can all be made into adaptive schedules linked to quantization error monitoring, reducing manual tuning.
