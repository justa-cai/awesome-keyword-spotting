## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The task of keyword spotting is defined as identifying pre-registered keywords within a continuous speech stream. It simultaneously involves two opposing sub-tasks: accurately detecting speech segments containing the keyword (avoiding misses) and reliably rejecting speech segments that do not contain the keyword (avoiding false alarms). The errors of these two sub-tasks are inversely related; in practical deployment, an acceptable operating point must be found between the miss rate and the false alarm rate. This is fundamentally why the field universally reports performance using Equal Error Rate (EER) and Detection Error Tradeoff (DET) curves. Deep learning methods have significantly improved speech analysis and detection performance. The mainstream technical routes are twofold: one is the keyword filler strategy, which detects predefined phoneme sequences on the input stream; the other is the exemplar-based query method, which performs similarity matching between the input query speech and pre-registered speech exemplars.

With the increasing demand for user-friendliness in smart speakers and voice assistants, the need for user-defined keywords is growing stronger: users may wish to wake devices using their children's names, foreign phrases, or coined passwords. However, custom keyword detection is much more difficult than fixed keyword detection because the registered phrases are arbitrary and may never have appeared in the training data. The model cannot rely on prior memory of specific words. This transforms the problem from closed-set classification to a matching problem in the sense of open-set or open-vocabulary matching: what the model learns should not be "what a specific word looks like," but rather "whether two inputs express the same linguistic content."

A deeper engineering pain point lies in the reliability of the registration process itself. The vast majority of existing user-defined keyword detection schemes adopt the exemplar-based query route, where the user first speaks the registration speech, and then new input queries are compared against the registration speech. The performance of this approach heavily depends on the consistency of audio recording sessions, which is difficult to maintain in real-world scenarios: each user has different vocal characteristics, and the registration environment may differ from the usage environment. Background noise and room reverberation can easily distort input speech. In other words, exemplar-based query schemes bake the "speaker timbre" and "recording channel"—variables that users do not want—directly into the registration template, creating an inherent speaker and environment mismatch between the template and the query.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

- **Registration Mismatch in Exemplar-Based Query Routes**: These methods use only audio as input, so the comparison between registration reference speech and query speech naturally carries speaker and environmental differences. Typical works include methods using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to map variable-length audio to fixed-length embeddings, and multi-head attention methods that suffer performance degradation on short phrases and non-word inputs due to word-level training criteria. The latter's flaw is particularly noteworthy: a word-level criterion implies that the model learns a similarity metric at the whole-word level. When a keyword consists of only one word or is a coined term outside the training vocabulary, the metric quality degrades sharply. This is precisely the most common input form in user-defined scenarios.
- **Pipeline Fragmentation in Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) Based Schemes**: These approaches first train an acoustic network using the CTC criterion to estimate phoneme sequences, and then use a post-hoc detector to determine whether the input speech contains the keyword. This two-stage design is not end-to-end; errors in phoneme estimation propagate backward to the detection decision. Furthermore, the mapping from phoneme sequences to keyword decisions relies on manually designed matching rules, meaning the overall optimization objective is inconsistent with the final detection metrics.
- **Granularity Issues in Cross-Modal Word-Level Embedding Methods**: Existing cross-modal works independently compute audio embeddings and phoneme embeddings, then map them to word-level representations using triplet loss into a shared latent space. Other works multiply word-level embeddings of registration text queries with frame-level audio embeddings to obtain probabilities. These methods explicitly operate on word-level embeddings, which require prior alignment or pooling. The temporal structural information within the phrase is compressed away at this step, yet phrase-level temporal structure is precisely the key evidence for distinguishing "saying the keyword completely" from "saying only part of it."
- **Model Scale Issues in Audio-Visual Cross-Modal Schemes**: An audio-visual keyword detection scheme first pre-trains audio-text and visual-text models separately, then fuses them into an audio-visual system during fine-tuning, performing detection via similarity graphs between video and audio embeddings. This scheme shares similar ideas with this paper (both use similarity graph patterns for decisions), but the paper explicitly notes its large model scale, making it unsuitable for embedded systems—where keyword detection is primarily deployed on low-power edge devices.
- **Mismatch Between Training Data Form and Task Form**: General speech corpora consist mainly of long sentences, whereas keywords are relatively short word sequences. Directly training open-vocabulary keyword detection on long speech corpora results in most training content being irrelevant to keyword detection, leading to low training efficiency. Additionally, there is a lack of specifically constructed, phonetically highly confusable hard negative samples, making it difficult for the model to learn true discriminative boundaries.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

The first challenge is how to map variable-length speech sequences and variable-length text phoneme sequences into a common latent space such that their similarity reflects only linguistic consistency, unaffected by speaker timbre, recording channels, and background noise. The text modality naturally contains only linguistic representations, devoid of user voice and recording environment information. Using text registration instead of speech registration theoretically eliminates registration session mismatch, provided that the audio encoder can map speech into a space comparable to text embeddings. This mapping itself is difficult to learn.

The second challenge is designing a sequence-level matching criterion that can characterize the temporal monotonic correspondence between audio and text, distinguish between complete matches, complete mismatches, and partial matches (especially the difference between prefix matches and prefix errors), and enable end-to-end training without manual alignment annotations.

The third challenge is training data engineering: transforming general long-speech corpora into phrase-level corpora that match the keyword form, and systematically constructing easy and hard negative samples to ensure the model faces sufficient tests on phonetically confusable pairs.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The model proposed in the paper is called the Cross-Modal Correspondence Detector, consisting of three sub-modules: an audio and text encoder, a pattern extraction module, and a pattern discriminator (the overall structure is shown in Figure 1, with red arrows indicating the positions of various training losses). The overall data flow is as follows: audio input is encoded to obtain an acoustic embedding sequence, text input is encoded to obtain a text embedding sequence, both are fed into a cross-attention-based pattern extraction module to obtain an affinity matrix and attention output, and finally, the pattern discriminator reads out the binary classification probability of whether the audio and text contain the same keyword.

The audio encoder consists of two one-dimensional convolutional layers with a kernel size of five and two Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs). The input is a 40-dimensional Mel-filterbank coefficient extracted every 10 milliseconds with a frame length of 25 milliseconds. For computational efficiency, the first convolutional layer uses a stride of 2 to halve the frame rate—this is a common compute-saving technique for edge keyword detection, where halving the number of frames approximately halves the computational load of subsequent recurrent units. The text encoder consists of a pre-trained Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) model followed by a fully connected layer: it first queries the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary to cover a large number of English words, and for out-of-vocabulary words, it uses a neural network to estimate the phoneme sequence. This design motivation is straightforward: user-defined keywords contain many proper nouns and coined terms, so the dictionary cannot cover everything. The pronunciation prediction capability for out-of-vocabulary words determines the ceiling of open-vocabulary capability. Audio embeddings and text embeddings are denoted as matrices with dimensions of (number of frames $\times$ embedding dimension) and (number of phonemes $\times$ embedding dimension), respectively, with the embedding dimension set to 128 in this paper.

The core motivation of this dual-stream architecture is: the text stream provides a pure linguistic template completely unrelated to the speaker and channel, while the audio stream must learn to extract information carrying linguistic content from speech into a space comparable to this template. The outputs of the two encoders are not single vectors but complete sequences, because the paper's matching criterion does not occur at the level of globally pooled vectors, but at the level of frame-level and phoneme-level affinity matrices—sequence-level temporal structure is preserved intact until the matching stage.

The pattern extraction module obtains time-correlated patterns using cross-attention between audio and phoneme representations: acoustic embeddings serve as keys and values, while text embeddings serve as queries. This key-value assignment direction is deliberate: the number of queries determines the sequence length of the attention output. Using the shorter text phoneme sequence as the query allows each phoneme position to "retrieve" its evidence from the audio frames. The resulting attention sequence is expanded along the phoneme axis, compactly summarizing "what each phoneme found in the audio." The pattern discriminator then uses a GRU layer with dimension equal to the embedding dimension to read the attention matrix, taking the output of the last frame, passing it through a fully connected layer and a sigmoid function to provide the decision probability. Using a recurrent unit instead of direct pooling to read the attention matrix allows the network to learn the sequential dependency of integrating evidence along the phoneme axis. For example, "the first few phonemes found strong alignment, but the last phoneme did not align" should output a low probability corresponding to a prefix error.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The core of pattern extraction is standard scaled dot-product attention (Equation 1):

$$Attn = softmax\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) \times V = A(Q,K) \times V$$

where the affinity matrix $A$ is obtained by the inner product of the query (text embedding) and the key (acoustic embedding), normalized by row-wise softmax. Geometrically, this is the temporal correlation matrix between the text phoneme axis and the acoustic frame axis. The key insight is: if the query and key express the same linguistic content, the affinity matrix exhibits a monotonic pattern—speech and its phonetic transcription unfold in the same temporal order. Audio cannot produce later phonemes before earlier ones (coarticulation causes only local blurring, not large-scale disorder). Thus, correctly paired matrices show energy concentration along the diagonal on a large scale. If the two contents differ, the matrix appears as an unstructured diffuse pattern. In other words, the alignment pattern itself carries discriminative information regarding "whether it is the same keyword." The discriminator must learn to read the pattern, not a single scalar similarity. Note that softmax is applied row-wise, so each row sums to one. The value range constraint dictates that the matching target constructed later must also undergo the same normalization.

The total training objective is composed of three weighted losses (Equation 2):

$$L_{total} = \lambda_1 L_{DN} + \lambda_2 L_{MM} + L_D$$

where the de-noising loss weight is 0.5, the monotonic matching loss weight is 0.3, and the detection loss is unweighted. The three terms address three different problems: de-noising loss addresses environmental robustness, monotonic matching loss addresses the structure of the embedding space, and detection loss addresses the final decision. The reason for分项 design rather than a single end-to-end loss is that under pure detection loss training, the pattern of the attention matrix emerges freely from the model, leading to slow and unstable convergence (this can be directly seen in the visualization of the first row of the ablation experiment in Figure 4). The monotonic matching loss provides explicit pattern supervision for intermediate representations, effectively paving the way for the optimization process.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Monotonic Matching Loss

The monotonic matching loss acts on the affinity matrix, reinforcing the alignment structure within the matrix by pulling the matrix closer to artificially constructed target patterns. The paper provides three target matrices for different matching scenarios (Figure 2 illustrates the targets for four scenarios when the audio keyword is "Hello world"): complete match uses a Gaussian diagonal pattern, mismatch uses a normalized random noise pattern, and prefix partial match uses a concatenated pattern of diagonal in the prefix and noise in the suffix (Equations 4 to 7):

$$L_{MM} = \|A - M_i\|^2, \quad i \in \{f, n, pf\}$$

The complete match target is a Gaussian band along the normalized time diagonal (Equation 5): on normalized time coordinates, the target value decreases with the Gaussian decay of the relative difference between frame position and phoneme position, with a bandwidth parameter of 0.2. This design draws inspiration from guided attention in Text-to-Speech (TTS)—the monotonic alignment prior between speech and its transcription is so strong that it can be directly written as a soft target injected into training. The mismatch target (Equation 6) is normalized Gaussian random noise, i.e., each element is a random number from a normal distribution, then normalized. There is a necessary mathematical reason for using noise instead of all zeros: the affinity matrix undergoes row-wise softmax, so each row sums to one and is non-negative everywhere. An all-zero target is unreachable. The reachable "no alignment" pattern must be one where each query position has no preferential concentration on audio frames, i.e., a low-peak, dispersed distribution. Normalized noise is exactly the target expression for this pattern. All three target matrices are sum-normalized to match the value range of the affinity matrix.

The prefix partial match target (Equation 7) is segmented based on whether the phoneme position falls within the matching boundary: phonemes before the boundary use the diagonal pattern, and those after use the noise pattern. The boundary is the boundary of the matching text, which is a hyperparameter. The most noteworthy design decision is: if only the suffix matches (the fourth scenario in Figure 2), the paper treats it as a mismatch. Why must a prefix error be judged as a whole negative? Because the semantic requirement of keyword detection is that the entire phrase appears completely from start to finish—in the wake-word usage scenario, if the beginning of the phrase spoken by the user is wrong, even if the latter half happens to be identical to the keyword, this utterance does not constitute a valid wake-up. Conversely, if suffix matches were scored as partially correct, phrases like "we mean to" vs. "i mean to," which differ by only one initial phoneme, would become high-risk false alarm sources. The visualization in Figure 4 specifically includes pairs like "i mean you" and "we mean to," which differ by one phoneme, to examine this boundary. This design directly encodes the task semantics of "complete phrase presence" into the supervision signal, leaving no room for the network to guess.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: De-noising Loss

To maintain robust performance on noisy speech queries, the paper constructs the acoustic encoder as a Siamese network, minimizing the mean squared error between the clean embedding and the noisy embedding of the same speech segment (Equation 3):

$$L_{DN} = \|E_A^{clean} - E_A^{noisy}\|^2$$

The design motivation comes from two existing routes: robust speech recognition via multi-task joint learning and discriminative de-noising autoencoders for robust speaker identification. Why is the constraint applied at the embedding layer rather than the signal or spectrogram layer? Because there is no corresponding noise in the text branch. The audio embedding must extract linguistic information into a noise-invariant form on its own to remain comparable with the text embedding. Enforcing consistency constraints at the embedding layer forces the encoder to discard additive noise components unrelated to linguistic content and retain alignable content components—this is precisely the attribute needed for cross-modal comparison. If de-noising reconstruction were performed only at the signal layer, the network might learn a de-noiser unrelated to text comparison, providing no help for downstream matching.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Curriculum Switching of Detection Loss (from Binary Cross-Entropy to Focal Loss)

The detection loss teaches the model to make keyword decisions based on the cross-attention matrix. The conventional approach is binary cross-entropy. The paper uses binary cross-entropy in the early stages of training and replaces it with focal loss in the later stages. The reason is that the training data comes from general corpora, where the number of positive sample pairs is far smaller than the number of negative sample pairs. A severely imbalanced dataset causes cross-entropy to be dominated by easily classified negative samples, wasting gradients on already correctly classified samples. Focal loss down-weights well-classified samples, concentrating gradients on hard samples, providing clear benefits for imbalanced data and mis-calibration issues. Why not use focal loss from the beginning? In the early stages of training, the decision boundary has not yet formed, and almost all samples are "hard samples." At this point, the modulation factor of the focal term cannot act as a filter, and the optimization signal is less stable than that of cross-entropy. Switching to focal loss only after the boundary has roughly formed allows it to truly play the role of "attacking hard samples," constituting a form of curriculum learning at the loss function level. The paper does not report the specific hyperparameter details of the switching time point.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: LibriPhrase Phrase Dataset

The mismatch between training data and task form is addressed by a new dataset. The paper constructs LibriPhrase from LibriSpeech: since LibriSpeech does not provide alignment annotations between audio and text, the Montreal Forced Aligner is first used to obtain word-level timestamps within the audio, and then complete long sentences are cut into phrases of one to four words. The training set is taken from the train-clean-100 and train-clean-360 subsets, with 200,000 phrases for each word length, totaling 800,000 phrases for four word lengths. The motivation for balancing by word length is twofold: first, natural sentence lengths in LibriSpeech tend to be longer; without balancing, the model would become biased. Second, the actual forms of keywords concentrate on one to four words (single-word wake words, two-to-three word greetings plus name structures). Balancing ensures the model's balanced capability across word lengths and makes the evaluation decomposed by word length (Figure 5) meaningful. For environmental robustness, noisy human speech from the MS-SNSD dataset is superimposed on the audio during training, with signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) randomly sampled between 5 and 15 dB. Choosing noisy human speech rather than white noise is deliberate: noisy human speech itself contains speech content and is the most difficult type of noise for keyword detection to handle. The 5 to 15 dB range covers typical household interference intensity.

The evaluation set is generated from the train-others-500 subset, consisting of 4,391, 2,605, 467, and 56 rounds for four word lengths, respectively. Each round targets a registration keyword anchor and contains three pairs of positive and negative audio-text pairs. Negative samples are divided into two levels: easy and hard, with the difficulty level determined by the edit distance between the anchor and the negative sample. Table 1 provides examples: hard negative samples are minimal edit variants that are phonetically highly similar, such as "friend" vs. "frind," "the river" vs. "the liver," and "i mean to" vs. "i mean you"—all differing by one or two phonemes. Easy negative samples, such as "comfort," "town with," and "be a banner," are phonetically quite distinct. The significance of automatically dividing levels using edit distance lies in systematically creating a difficulty curriculum: easy negative pairs teach the model the basic "different means mismatch," while hard negative pairs specifically hone phoneme-level discrimination, which is the main source of real-world false alarms.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to exemplar-based query routes (CTC schemes, attention schemes), the essential difference lies in the registration modality: the former registers speech exemplars and compares two audio segments, while the latter registers text and compares audio with text. Registration information changes from "a product of a specific recording session" to a "deterministic linguistic template," theoretically eliminating speaker and channel mismatch. Compared to cross-modal word-level embedding triplet methods, this paper does not explicitly construct word-level embeddings but considers the similarity between the entire text sequence and the entire audio sequence, leaving word-level alignment to be implicitly completed by the affinity matrix. This preserves the internal temporal structure of the phrase, which also explains why this method is more stable for short keywords (Figure 5). Compared to schemes that multiply word-level embeddings frame-by-frame, this paper's cross-attention makes alignment data-driven rather than implicitly assumed. Compared to audio-visual cross-modal schemes, both follow the "similarity graph pattern discrimination" route, but this paper retains only the audio and text streams, keeping the parameter scale controllable for embedded deployment. Additionally, the text side uses a three-tier combination of a pre-trained G2P model, CMU dictionary, and neural network out-of-vocabulary prediction, ensuring open-vocabulary coverage capability.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Training uses only the LibriPhrase training set: train-clean-100 and train-clean-360 from LibriSpeech, with 200,000 phrases for each of the one-to-four word lengths, totaling 800,000 phrases. During training, MS-SNSD noisy human speech noise with an SNR of 5 to 15 dB is superimposed. Three datasets are used for evaluation: First, the LibriPhrase evaluation set (from train-others-500, with speakers non-overlapping with the training set), divided into four subsets by word length, with 4,391, 2,605, 467, and 56 rounds respectively. Each round contains three positive and three negative pairs, with negative samples divided into easy and hard sets. Second, the Google Speech Commands v1 dataset, where ten short words are selected according to existing evaluation settings. This dataset contains environmental distortions and is used to examine noise robustness. Third, the Qualcomm Keyword Speech Dataset, which contains four command phrases ("Hey android," "Hey snapdragon," "Hi galaxy," "Hi lumina"). Its characteristic is that it contains proper nouns at the end of the phrase, making phoneme information difficult to estimate, serving as a hard test for open-vocabulary capability. The paper does not report the total hours for each dataset.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper reports three metrics: Equal Error Rate (EER), Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), and Detection Error Tradeoff (DET) curve. The rationale for selection is directly related to the nature of the task: keyword detection is a dual-error-rate detection problem with a threshold. Reporting the error rate at a single operating point is meaningless. EER is the error rate at the operating point where the miss rate equals the false alarm rate, compressing the entire tradeoff curve into a single value independent of threshold selection, facilitating horizontal comparison. The AUC metric is a threshold-free aggregated measure of detection sensitivity across the entire threshold range, sensitive to the shape of the entire curve, and able to capture consistency differences that the EER point cannot reveal. The DET curve plots both error rates on a logarithmic scale, directly showing the range of deployable operating points, and is used in engineering to select the working threshold. This combination is also the standard configuration in exemplar-based keyword detection literature, and its adoption ensures comparability.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

The paper reproduces three baselines on the same corpus: a single-modal CTC scheme, a single-modal multi-head attention scheme with soft triplet loss, and a cross-modal word-level embedding triplet scheme. All three baselines require three audio pronunciations to complete keyword registration during inference, whereas this paper's method requires only text registration. Table 2 summarizes all results.

Table 2 (EER is percentage, AUC is percentage; G is Google Commands set, Q is Qualcomm set, Easy/Hard are the two levels of LibriPhrase):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CTC Scheme | Single-modal | 31.65 | 18.23 | 14.67 | 35.22 | 66.36 | 89.69 | 92.29 | 69.58 |

The structure of the results is clear. This paper leads comprehensively on the Hard set and the Qualcomm set: the Hard set EER is 32.90, better than the strongest baseline, the CTC scheme's 35.22 (a relative decrease of approximately 6.6% calculated by this paper), and the AUC increases from 69.58 to 73.58. On the Qualcomm set, the EER is 12.15 compared to the CTC scheme's 18.23 (a relative decrease of approximately 33.4%), and the AUC is 94.51 compared to 89.69. The common characteristic of these two datasets is that the negative samples contain words with pronunciations similar to the registered keywords. The Qualcomm set is particularly difficult because the phrases end with proper nouns like "snapdragon" and "lumina," where phoneme information itself is difficult to estimate. However, the text encoder relied upon by this paper uses neural network out-of-vocabulary prediction to cover the pronunciation of such words, and the cross-attention alignment relies only on phoneme-level evidence rather than whole-word priors, allowing it to still win. The advantage on the Easy set is even larger: EER is 8.42 compared to 14.67 (a relative decrease of approximately 42.6%), and AUC is 96.70 compared to 92.29.

The only failure is on the Google Commands set: the Attention scheme achieves an EER of 14.75 and an AUC of 92.09, while this paper's method achieves 27.25 and 81.06, a significant gap. The paper explains that the keywords in the Google set are high-frequency common words like "on," "off," "one," and "two," which are likely included in the training data. The Attention scheme, relying on its similarity scoring mechanism, can learn the characteristics of these words into the classifier during the training phase. This is equivalent to answering open-vocabulary questions on a closed-vocabulary exam, taking advantage of in-vocabulary priors. However, once the keywords are uncommon (Qualcomm set and LibriPhrase), the Attention scheme collapses to a high Qualcomm set EER of 49.13. This paper's method does not build priors for any specific word, paying the price for in-vocabulary scenarios in exchange for consistent dominance in out-of-vocabulary scenarios: except for the Google set, this paper outperforms all baselines in both EER and AUC on all datasets. The DET curves in Figure 3 provide the same conclusion across the four datasets. This trade-off structure is the most noteworthy judgment in the entire paper: there is tension between open-vocabulary capability and closed-set ultimate precision within the same architecture, and this paper explicitly chooses the former.

## Main Contributions

First, an end-to-end user-defined keyword detection scheme is proposed that uses text registration instead of speech registration. The text modality contains only linguistic representations and is naturally immune to speaker timbre and recording environment differences, theoretically eliminating the registration session mismatch problem of the exemplar-based query route. Moreover, the interaction cost of typing to register is lower than speaking the registration speech three times.

Second, a cross-modal matching paradigm is proposed where the affinity matrix pattern serves as the discriminative carrier. Cross-attention is used to place audio frames and text phonemes into the same time-correlated matrix, using "diagonal monotonic pattern vs. unstructured diffuse pattern" as the discriminative signature for positive and negative samples. Sequence-level temporal structure is preserved intact until the decision stage, rather than being pooled away in advance as in word-level embedding methods.

Third, two targeted training criteria are proposed: the monotonic matching loss provides explicit pattern supervision for the affinity matrix (Gaussian diagonal target, normalized noise target, prefix segmented target, and explicitly judging suffix matches as negative to encode the task semantics of "complete phrase presence"); the de-noising loss constrains the consistency between clean and noisy representations at the embedding layer, complementing this by switching the detection loss from binary cross-entropy to focal loss in the later stages of training to handle positive-negative sample imbalance.

Fourth, the LibriPhrase phrase dataset is released: based on LibriSpeech and forced alignment, cut into one-to-four word phrases, with 200,000 phrases per word length totaling 800,000, superimposed with noisy human speech, and using edit distance to automatically divide the evaluation set into easy and hard negative levels. This provides a training and evaluation benchmark matched to the task form and difficulty layering for open-vocabulary keyword detection.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Performance Cost in Closed-Set Scenarios**: On the Google Commands set, this paper's method achieves an EER of 27.25, significantly worse than the in-vocabulary keyword scheme's 14.75 (Table 2). The generality of open-vocabulary and the availability of in-vocabulary priors are mutually exclusive in this architecture: the model has no mechanism to switch to a stronger word-specific discriminator upon detecting "I have seen this word," and the ceiling of in-vocabulary performance is suppressed by the open-vocabulary constraint.
- **Absolute Performance on Phonetically Confusable Pairs is Still Poor**: The Hard set EER is 32.9 (Table 2), meaning that among ten hard negative queries, approximately three are still misjudged, far from the level required for deployment. The deep reason is that the discriminative signature of this paper is primarily the alignment pattern: for negative samples like "friend" vs. "frind" or "the river" vs. "the liver," which differ by only one phoneme, the diagonal pattern of their affinity matrices is almost isomorphic to that of positive samples. They cannot be distinguished at the pattern level, and discrimination can only rely on subtle differences in matrix values, which is precisely the most fragile signal.
- **Monotonic Diagonal Prior is a Simplifying Assumption**: The Gaussian diagonal target (bandwidth 0.2) implicitly assumes that speech rate is roughly uniform along the phrase and there are no long pauses within the phrase. In real speech, fluctuations in speech rate and pauses between words cause the alignment path to deviate from the diagonal band. The deviation between the target and the true optimal alignment interferes with training in the form of supervision noise. The bandwidth parameter and matching boundary are both fixed values, and the paper does not report sensitivity analysis for these hyperparameters.
- **Narrow Coverage of Noise**: The de-noising loss and training augmentation only cover one type and one interval: noisy human speech with an SNR of 5 to 15 dB. Other noise types (music, keyboard, reverberation) and lower SNRs do not appear in training, and the paper does not report performance under extremely low SNRs.
- **Text Front-End Relies on G2P Quality**: The ceiling of open-vocabulary capability is determined by pronunciation prediction. Foreign pronunciations of proper nouns and names, and user-specific personalized pronunciations (e.g., reading one's own name in a non-dictionary way) cannot be modeled, yet these are high-frequency sources of user-defined keywords. The paper itself admits that phoneme estimation for proper nouns in the Qualcomm set is difficult.
- **Complete Lack of Edge Metrics**: The paper criticizes audio-visual schemes for being unsuitable for embedded systems due to model scale, but the entire paper does not report its own parameter count, computational load, inference latency, or memory usage. The design of the first convolutional layer with stride 2 implies attention to compute power, but without numerical support, it is impossible to verify whether the architecture is truly suitable for edge deployment.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Evaluation Remains on Isolated Phrase Classification, Without Verifying Streaming Detection**: All evaluations are binary classifications of "given a short audio segment and a text keyword, judge match or not." Real keyword detection is a streaming task on continuous audio streams, requiring handling of keywords crossing detection windows, concatenation with non-keyword speech, and evaluation using streaming metrics such as false alarms per hour. The paper does not report any streaming or continuous speech experiments. The target design for prefix and suffix partial matches implies the authors' consideration of continuous speech, but the evaluation does not deliver on this layer.
- **Sample Size for the Four-Word Long Subset is Too Small**: The number of evaluation rounds by word length is 4,391, 2,605, 467, and 56. The four-word level has only 56 rounds, with six pairs per round, totaling over 300 samples. The performance numbers at this point have extremely high variance, and the conclusions at the four-word end of Figure 5 are statistically unreliable.
- **Baselines are Self-Reproduced by Authors**: The three baselines are retrained by the authors on the same corpus, ensuring corpus fairness, but the reproduction quality cannot be independently verified. Especially given the strong performance of the Attention scheme on the Google set and its collapse on the Qualcomm set, there is a possibility of reproduction bias.
- **Loss Weights and Training Schedule Lack Ablation**: The two loss weights are set to 0.5 and 0.3, and the switching time point for focal loss, without providing selection basis or sensitivity analysis. These hyperparameters directly determine the relative intensity of the three losses, and a different dataset may require re-tuning.
- **Language and Single-Corpus Limitations**: Training and evaluation are all based on English (the CMU dictionary is also an English resource). Tonal languages and languages with polysemous character-to-pronunciation mappings are not touched upon. The training corpus is single (LibriSpeech audiobook domain plus Google Commands evaluation), and cross-domain generalization capability is not examined.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

First, strengthen phoneme-level discrimination for one-phoneme-difference negative samples: add discriminative constraints on the phoneme content dimension in addition to the affinity matrix pattern (e.g., contrastive learning or hard negative mining for confusable phoneme pairs), directly attacking the 32.9 Hard set EER, which is the current biggest performance洼地 (low point). Second, complete streaming detection: splice with voice activity detection and streaming acoustic front-ends, evaluate on continuous audio using false alarms per hour, and verify the benefits of the prefix and suffix negative judgment design in real streaming scenarios. Third, make the alignment prior adaptive: replace the fixed Gaussian diagonal target with learnable positional priors or soft dynamic programming to accommodate speech rate fluctuations and inter-word pauses. Fourth, complete the edge evidence chain: report parameter count, operator count, and latency, and combine quantization and pruning to turn "suitable for embedded" from a motivation sentence into measured numbers. Fifth, expand multi-lingual text front-ends and user-specific pronunciation registration, advancing open-vocabulary capability from "dictionary plus out-of-vocabulary prediction" to "self-adapting to user pronunciation." Sixth, bridge the gap between closed-set and open-set: allow mounting word-specific branches when the keyword happens to be in the training vocabulary, balancing this paper's strength in open-vocabulary with the benefits of in-vocabulary priors, and bridging the 12.5 percentage point EER gap on the Google Commands set.
