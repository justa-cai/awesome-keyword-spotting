## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Edge computing devices such as smart speakers and wearable devices are getting closer to our living environments, and the continuous voice data they generate is the core fuel for machine learning applications like Keyword Spotting (KWS). However, precisely because data collection occurs in bedrooms and on wrists, privacy issues have become acute: the practice of AI companies aggressively collecting user data from edge devices to improve service quality conflicts directly with the strong restrictions on user data collection imposed by the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) approved by the EU in recent years. How to utilize these valuable data scattered across edge devices under the premise of compliance is the entire problem space established at the beginning of the paper.

The paper outlines a spectrum of solutions in three layers. The first layer consists of provably private cryptographic schemes—Secure Multi-Party Computation (MPC) and Homomorphic Encryption (HE). These provide strict privacy guarantees but have huge computational and communication overheads, making them impractical for public cross-device scenarios and more suitable for cross-silo collaboration among a few institutions. The second layer consists of heuristic privacy schemes—Federated Learning (FL) proposed by Google: data does not leave the device; each user holds a complete copy of the model locally, trains it with private data, and periodically sends parameter updates to the server; the server performs a weighted average of all user updates according to FedAvg to obtain the global model, which is then distributed for the next round. FL has been scaled and deployed on Google Assistant and Google Keyboard. The third layer is the TinyML context in which this paper resides: Giménez et al. (cited as [3], Electronics 2022) first brought FL into the micro-controller community, training a 2-layer MLP with 16K parameters on a TinyML development board using FL to complete a binary classification keyword discrimination task.

The pain points are exposed in this third layer. FL has a hard prerequisite: users must be able to train the **entire model** locally. For embedded systems with extremely limited memory and power consumption, this prerequisite does not hold for industrial-scale models. The experimental platform, Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense, has only 256KB SRAM and 1MB Flash (see Section 3.1 for hardware description), and the runtime memory required to train a model is far greater than that for inference—in addition to the parameters themselves, it must accommodate forward activation caches, backward gradients, and an additional momentum buffer of the same size maintained by SGD with momentum. Roughly speaking, the memory overhead in float32 training state is at least three to four times the number of parameters, not to mention audio buffers and MFCC feature caches. [3] could only use a small MLP with 16K parameters precisely because it was forced by the SRAM of micro-controllers—but a binary classifier with 16K parameters is still far from a "meaningful industrial task."

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

- **Memory Wall of FL**: FL requires the entire model to reside on the device and complete the local forward and backward processes. When the model is large, SRAM cannot hold the training triad (parameters, gradients, momentum), making the task directly infeasible. This is not a precision issue, but a feasibility issue—FL on micro-controllers can only train toy models (the 16K parameter 2-layer MLP, binary classification in [3]), and cannot handle more complex datasets and use cases.
- **Overhead Wall of MPC/HE**: Cryptographic schemes with provable privacy have excessive computational and communication overheads, making them unsuitable for public cross-device scenarios and forcing them to retreat to cross-silo scenarios.
- **Serial Bottleneck of Split Learning (SL)**: Split Learning (proposed by Vepakomma et al. in 2018, cited as [7]) splits the model into two halves, client and server. The client only runs the first few layers, and the activations and labels are sent to the server to continue calculation. However, the multi-client collaboration in original SL is round-robin; one client trains before the next one gets a turn, resulting in poor parallelism.
- **Two Known Defects of SFL itself**: Split Federated Learning (proposed by Thapa et al. at AAAI 2022, cited as [5]) incorporates the model aggregation idea of FL into SL to solve the parallelism problem, but Section 2.2 of the paper explicitly acknowledges that SFL has two recognized weaknesses—high communication overhead (activations and gradients must travel back and forth between device and server at every step) and vulnerability to model inversion attacks (the client sends labels along with activations to the server at every step, creating a larger attack surface than FL). Recent work (asynchronous training to reduce communication in [1], model inversion defense in [6]) only partially mitigates these issues, and the authors emphasize that careful evaluation is still needed before deployment.
- **Inference Dependency of Standard SFL**: Models trained via split learning still rely on remote communication with the server during inference—the device only holds the first few layers, while the latter half is in the cloud. This is a structural defect for low-latency, offline-available keyword recognition scenarios (wake words must respond locally and instantly).

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

The core question the paper aims to answer is: **Can a device hold only a very small client slice on a micro-controller with 256KB SRAM, participate in training a complete large model far exceeding its memory capacity, and then squeeze the compressed entire model back into the device after training to achieve independent inference?** This breaks down into four sub-challenges: (1) Under the constraint that SRAM can only hold the first layer of the network and the training batch size can only be 1, run and verify the correctness of the entire SFL process (forward cut, gradient backpropagation, model synchronization) on a real micro-controller; (2) Under the same hardware constraints, prove that SFL can use large models that FL cannot use, thereby achieving higher accuracy (13.89 percentage points higher than the SOTA FL implementation on the English digits dataset); (3) Connect the framework conversion pipeline from "training state on server, inference state back to device" (PyTorch to Keras to int8 quantized TFLite), eliminating the defect of SFL relying on the server for inference; (4) Simulate multi-client federated training with only one board.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

**Hardware Platform** (Section 3.1): Arduino Nano 33 BLE board (Sense version with microphone), described in the original text as "2-bit ARM Cortex-M4 CPU running at 64 MHz with 256KB SRAM and 1MB Flash memory, operating at 3.3V"—the "2-bit" is obviously a typo (Cortex-M4 is a 32-bit core, and the board actually uses the nRF52840), but the constraints of 64MHz clock speed, 256KB SRAM, 1MB Flash, and 3.3V power supply are the starting point for all design decisions in the paper.

**System Role Division** (Figure 1): One Arduino board acts as a real client; one PC plays three roles simultaneously—server (running the server-side model implemented in PyTorch), master controller (managing serial communication timing), and virtual remaining clients (simulating multiple boards by replicating the client model plus MFCC frontend in PyTorch). The board and PC are connected via a serial link. There is a key engineering compromise in the data flow: since the board does not yet support data annotation and storage functions, the authors store the client's private data on the PC first, push it to the board via serial port, and the board performs MFCC feature extraction locally before feeding it to the client-side model—the original audio is indeed featureized on the board, but the "data native to the device" link is simulated. The onboard microphone can capture real-time audio for inference, but due to limited storage space and the difficulty of allowing users to synchronize labels on the board, true collection of training data is left for future work.

**Why cut at the first layer, why is the client so small**: The split point is chosen very shallowly, with the client retaining only the first layer of the entire network. The client-side model has two candidate forms (Section 3.3): a 1-layer MLP (650×25, consistent with [3]) or a 1-layer Conv2D (12×1×3×3). The motivation is direct—the input of the first layer is the largest tensor in the entire network (650-dimensional MFCC flattened features), but as long as the output width is compressed to 25 or 12 channels, the number of parameters on the board is fixed at the thousand level; all subsequent large layers reside on the server, which has no memory limits and fast training speed. The authors explicitly state: the purpose of using SFL is to "be able to train with very large models because the server has no memory limits" (Section 1).

**Model Zoo** (Table 1):

| Model | Client (On-board) | Server (PC) |
|---|---|---|
| Model 1 (MLP) | FC25 | FC7 |
| Model 2 (CNN) | Conv12×3×3 | Conv16×3×3 → FC128 → FC7 |
| Model 3 (CNN) | Conv12×3×3 | Conv30×3×3 → FC256 → FC7 |

The authors' explanation for this capacity ladder (Section 3.3): MLP is sufficient for simple datasets where features are linearly separable, and adding complexity on the server side yields little benefit; on small datasets, "large CNNs are more prone to overfitting and have worse validation accuracy"; whereas on the larger ENdigits dataset, CNNs significantly outperform MLPs, so two tiers of Conv backends were made on the server side (16 channels connected to FC128, 30 channels connected to FC256). The MLP baseline is a 2-layer FC (650×25×7). The paper claims the total parameters are 650×25+25×7=4725, with 4550 on the board—note that calculating the original expression directly yields 16425 (650×25=16250, plus 25×7=175), and the numbers 4725 and 4550 do not match the equation. The original text is as is, likely a typesetting or input typo, so caution is needed when citing.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

Algorithm 1 gives the complete formalization of SFL. Let $M$ clients each hold private data $(X_i, Y_i)$, and the complete DNN with $L$ layers be split into the server-side model $S$ (parameters $W_S$) and each client-side model $C_i$ (parameters $W_{C_i}$):

1. **Model Synchronization**: At the beginning of each epoch, perform federated averaging $C^* = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} C_i$, and then all clients align $C_i \leftarrow C^*$. This step is the FedAvg aggregation idea of FL applied to client slices—it periodically pulls back different local slice copies trained on heterogeneous data across clients to consensus, and is the key difference between SFL and the round-robin training of SL.
2. **Parallel Client Forward**: For each batch, client $i$ calculates $A_i = C_i(W_{C_i}; x_i)$ in parallel, and sends the activation $A_i$ along with the label $y_i$ to the server via serial port.
3. **Serial Server Forward and Backprop**: The server sequentially calculates the cross-entropy loss $l = l_{CE}(S(W_S; A_i),\ y_i)$ for each client, backpropagates through the loss to obtain the gradient with respect to the activation $\nabla_{A_i} l$, sends it back to the client, and updates $W_S$.
4. **Parallel Client Backprop**: After receiving $\nabla_{A_i} l$, the client continues chain backpropagation locally and updates $W_{C_i}$.

Mathematically, this is breaking the chain rule at the cut point: $\frac{\partial l}{\partial W_{C_i}} = \frac{\partial l}{\partial A_i} \cdot \frac{\partial A_i}{\partial W_{C_i}}$. The local Jacobian required for the second factor is calculated only on the client, while the first factor is calculated by the server and sent back. The device side therefore never needs to know the shape of $W_S$, and the server never touches the original audio $x_i$—the privacy boundary lies on the cut surface. The cost is that every training step requires two traversals of the serial port (activations up, gradients down), which is the source of the communication overhead weakness acknowledged in Section 2.2; simultaneously, labels are sent up in plaintext with activations, constituting the source of the model inversion attack surface.

**MFCC Frontend** (Section 3.2): Audio input is 1 second, 16,000 samples (i.e., 16kHz sampling rate). MFCC hyperparameters: 13 coefficients, frame length 0.02 seconds, step 0.02 seconds (no overlap), 32 filter banks, FFT length 256, normalized window 101. MFCC uses the embedded implementation of Edge Impulse—can be deployed directly to the board; a Python replica of MFCC is also used for full online simulation training on the PC, aiming to accelerate hyperparameter search (running MFCC on the board is too slow, making hyperparameter search impractical). 1 second of audio divided into frames of 20ms without overlap yields about 49-50 frames, and 13 coefficients flattened result in an input of about 650 dimensions, matching the 650 input dimension of the client MLP.

**Training Hyperparameters** (Section 3.4): Batch size is fixed at 1 (limited by board resources); learning rate is 0.005 for CNN and 0.0005 for MLP (ten times difference, reason not elaborated—from the results, CNN can take larger steps due to convolutional inductive bias, while MLP can only take small steps slowly); SGD momentum 0.6; training/validation split 9:1; SFL training epochs fixed at 3.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: First Deployment of SFL on Micro-controllers (Very Shallow Cut + Dual-Framework Heterogeneous Implementation)

This is the first contribution of the paper: SFL is deployed for the first time on an ultra-low-power, low-cost micro-controller to perform a meaningful multi-class digital keyword recognition task (previously, the FL work in [3] was only binary classification). The most valuable engineering aspect is the **heterogeneous dual-end implementation** (Section 3.5): the client model is hand-written in C++ on the board (connected to the embedded MFCC), and the server model is written in PyTorch on the PC; another PyTorch replica of the client model plus MFCC frontend is used to virtually create the remaining clients, forming multi-client SFL. This means the two sides of the cut run in different frameworks and precision environments, and gradients travel back and forth between the C++ world and the PyTorch world via the serial port—the fact that it converges itself verifies the consistency of forward/backward semantics on both sides. Although multi-client training is implemented, the authors admit it is serial processing (parallelization is listed in future work).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Decoupled Pipeline of "Training Large Models, Deploying Compressed Models"

This is the smartest engineering insight in the paper (Contribution 3, Section 5.3): **Memory requirements in the training state and memory requirements in the inference state are two different things; SFL offloads the former to the server, and the latter is recovered to the device using compression + quantization.** Standard SFL inference also relies on server communication. The paper's approach is that after training convergence, the server combines the client slice and server slice into a complete global model, manually moves weights from PyTorch to Keras (the two frameworks have different parameter layouts, and the authors describe it as "causing tons of trouble"), and then converts it to a TFLite model with int8 quantization enabled, deploying it directly back to the Arduino board for local low-latency inference. Inference only requires the parameters themselves (1 byte per parameter after int8, one-quarter of float32) plus one activation cache, and does not require gradients, momentum, or intermediate quantities of the training batch—that is why a model that cannot fit on the board during training can be squeezed back in during deployment. The memory constraints of training and deployment are thus decoupled.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Empirical Evidence of "SFL Breaking the Memory Wall" Using Model Capacity Ladder

The core control of the experimental design (Section 5.2): The MLP model (Model 1) represents the performance FL can achieve on this hardware—because FL simply cannot train the entire CNN on the board; the two CNN models (Model 2/3) are only possible under SFL. The three models are compared on validation accuracy on the same dataset and under the same training protocol, turning the causal chain of "memory wall → can only use small models → accuracy ceiling" into measurable numbers (see Experimental Results section). This control design itself is the argumentative skeleton of the paper: the value of SFL lies not in a superior training algorithm, but in **removing the artificial cap on model capacity**.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

- **Compared to FL (McMahan et al., FedAvg)**: FL transmits parameter updates, and the entire model resides on the device; SFL transmits intermediate activations and cut-point gradients, and the device only hosts the first layer. The communication object changes from "parameter difference" to "activation/gradient tensors at every step," with higher frequency but smaller single size (depending on the depth of the cut point). FL cannot train large models on 256KB SRAM, but SFL can.
- **Compared to SL (Vepakomma et al.)**: SL clients train in turn, with no aggregation; SFL performs FedAvg-style client slice aggregation every epoch, supporting (formal) parallel multi-clients.
- **Compared to On-board FL in [3]**: [3] uses a 16K parameter 2-layer MLP for binary classification; this paper performs multi-class (7 classes) recognition on the same hardware, and the server side can be scaled to CNNs with millions of parameters.
- **Compared to Original SFL (Thapa et al., SplitFed)**: The original runs on workstation/server-level hardware; this paper achieves MCU-level deployment and adds the deployment loop of "compressing back to the device for inference after training"—original SFL does not solve the problem of inference relying on the server.
- **Compared to Centralized Training**: Centralized training requires uploading raw data, which is unacceptable for privacy; SFL processes raw data purely locally. The paper concludes that SFL achieves both "higher utility than FL" and "better privacy than centralized training." Note that this is only heuristic privacy, with no formal guarantees, and is fundamentally different from the provable privacy of MPC/HE.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Two datasets (Section 3.2):

- **CNdigits (Chinese Digits, Self-collected)**: The authors collected their own reading of Chinese digits online using the open-source Edge Impulse platform. The classes are digits 1 to 5, plus "silence" and "unknown," totaling **7 classes**, with **50 samples** per class, totaling 350 samples. The scale is extremely small, with a single speaker, belonging to a proof-of-concept nature.
- **ENdigits (English Digits)**: Taken from the Speech Commands dataset by Warden 2018 (cited as [8]), corresponding to the above classes, totaling **7,000 samples**. As a larger, more realistic collection, it bears the main performance comparison.

Both sets use the same MFCC frontend and 9:1 training/validation split. The paper does not report details such as whether the class distribution is balanced, the specific size of the validation set, or the number of speakers.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The only metric is **validation accuracy** (Section 4: the prediction accuracy of the trained model on the validation set). The rationale can be understood from the nature of the task: 7-class balanced classification, and the paper aims to compare learning schemes (FL achievable capacity vs. SFL achievable capacity) rather than deployment metrics; accuracy is the most direct utility measure. However, a deep reading must point out the thinness of the metrics: **the paper does not report** the size of the quantized model, on-board inference latency, time per training round, total serial communication volume, energy consumption, confusion matrix, or per-class precision and recall—these are exactly the deployment-side evidence that TinyML papers usually must provide. The "low latency" mentioned in the abstract has no numerical support; Section 5.5 admits that due to delays in framework conversion, "there was no time for final deployment and on-board testing," meaning the inference loop only reached the step of "converting to TFLite."

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Correctness Verification** (Section 5.1, Figure 2): Model 1 (MLP) is trained on CNdigits using SFL. The curve shows that the model **converges rapidly within about 70 rounds**, reaching an accuracy of **over 90%** (both training and validation curves are provided). The significance of this set of experiments is to exclude "implementation bugs"—the pipeline of heterogeneous dual-ends and serial gradient transmission can converge to an accuracy matching the task difficulty, verifying that the SFL implementation is correct. On small datasets, MLP is sufficient, and the authors also confirm that "a very large CNN model is not needed."

**Core Comparison of SFL vs. FL** (Section 5.2, Figure 3 Training Accuracy / Figure 4 Validation Accuracy): Three models compete on ENdigits. All models can exceed 80% training accuracy after 2 epochs; due to SGD with batch size 1, the training accuracy fluctuates greatly; CNN converges significantly faster than MLP. Final validation accuracy: **Model 1 (MLP, i.e., the upper limit form of FL on this hardware) 66.25%, Model 2 (Small CNN) 78.29%, Model 3 (Large CNN) 80.14%** (Figure 4). 80.14% − 66.25% = **13.89 percentage points**, which is the source of the claim in the abstract of being "13.89% higher than the SOTA FL implementation ([3])." The conclusion chain is: larger and more complex models perform better on ENdigits, and only SFL can use large models, therefore SFL is superior to FL.

Deficiencies in the comparison that need to be pointed out: This is a comparison between the "strongest SFL model" and the "only available model for FL locked by the memory wall," with capacity and structure variables mixed together (architecture confounding factor), rather than proof that the SFL training algorithm itself is superior under the same capacity—the victory of SFL is essentially the capacity dividend brought by memory feasibility. Furthermore, the paper does not compare with the accuracy upper bound of centralized training (the server originally has all simulatable data, and adding a centralized baseline is low cost), does not repeat with multiple random seeds, and 66.25/78.29/80.14 are all single-run numbers.

**Deployment Side** (Section 5.3): The server collects the converged global model (client slice + server slice), converts it via PyTorch→Keras→TFLite (int8 quantization), and claims it can be deployed directly to the Arduino board for local inference. The accuracy loss after quantization and the model byte size are **not reported by the paper**. Roughly calculating based on the topology in Table 1, one can feel the magnitude: the MFCC of 1 second of audio is a single-channel map of about 13×49. The server-side Conv30×3×3 of Model 3 (12 channels input) outputs about 30×11×47≈15.5k dimensions, connected to FC256, and the weights of this layer alone are about 4 million. After int8 quantization, it is about 4MB, which already exceeds the 1MB Flash of the nRF52840—this also explains from the side why the actual on-board deployment of Model 3 could not be completed; while Model 1 (about 16k parameters, or 4725 according to the paper's口径) is far smaller than Flash after quantization, making it the form that can truly be deployed back to the device. This paragraph is calculated by the author based on topology, for engineering judgment reference, not numbers reported in the paper.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have a formal ablation table; what actually serves the function of ablation is the **model capacity ladder** (Table 1 × Figures 2-4) and the cross-analysis of dataset scale:

- **Interaction of Capacity × Data Scale**: On the small dataset (CNdigits, 350 samples), MLP already reaches 90%+. The authors observe that "large CNNs overfit on small data and have worse validation accuracy" (Section 3.3, qualitative statement, no curves provided); on the large dataset (ENdigits, 7000 samples), the capacity dividend is realized, with a monotonic increase from 66.25% to 80.14%. This empirical rule of "small data small models, large data large models" is consistent with common sense in generalization theory and is one of the most transferable engineering judgments in the paper.
- **Optimization Noise**: Batch size 1 causes large fluctuations in the accuracy curve (Section 5.2), which is empirical evidence that MCU memory constraints directly penetrate the optimization dynamics—if SRAM were more generous and batch size could be enlarged, the curve should be significantly smoother.
- **Convergence Speed**: CNN converges faster than MLP (Figure 3), which can be attributed to the prior of local translation invariance of convolutional kernels on time-frequency maps adapting to speech features.

**Missing Ablations** (all not reported by the paper): The impact of cut point depth (why cut the first layer instead of the second—if cut one layer deeper, the communication activation tensor becomes smaller but the on-board memory becomes larger, there is a trade-off curve, which the paper does not touch); the comparison between client model forms MLP and Conv12×1×3×3 (both candidates are given in the text but not compared separately); the specific value and scaling law of the number of clients $M$; the aggregation frequency (is once per epoch optimal?); serial baud rate and bytes of communication per sample; comparison of synchronous SGD and asynchronous. These blanks outline the space for future work.

## Main Contributions

The paper lists three contributions (Section 1), which can be summarized into four points at the implementation level:

1. **First Deployment of SFL on Micro-controllers**: Running the entire SFL process on an ultra-low-power, low-cost (64MHz/256KB SRAM/1MB Flash level) embedded system, with a task of meaningful multi-class digital keyword recognition, rather than the binary classification toy task of previous TinyML-FL work [3].
2. **Empirical Evidence that SFL Breaks the Training Memory Wall**: Proving that SFL allows devices to participate in training models far exceeding their memory capacity, with large models (CNN) achieving 13.89 percentage points higher validation accuracy (80.14% vs. 66.25%, Figure 4) than the small MLP, which is the only available model under FL hardware constraints—the shortcoming of FL is attributed to capacity capping rather than the algorithm itself.
3. **Closing the Loop of "Inference Back to Device"**: After training, the entire model is compressed and quantized into int8 TFLite and sent back to the device for local inference, resolving the structural defect of standard SFL inference relying on server communication; the decoupling of "offloading training state memory to the server, compressing inference state memory back to the device" is the most translatable engineering idea in this paper.
4. **Prototype Implementation of Multi-Client Training**: Implementing multi-client SFL training via PyTorch virtual clients (serial mode), and打通ing the gradient interoperability between C++ (on-board client) and PyTorch (server and virtual clients) heterogeneous dual-ends.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Inherent Weaknesses of SFL Inherited Intact**: Communication overhead (activations up + gradients down through serial port at every step) and vulnerability to model inversion attacks (labels uploaded in plaintext with activations) are the two known problems acknowledged in Section 2.2 of the paper. This paper does not perform any practical tests or hardening, only reminding that "careful evaluation is needed." The serial communication volume and bandwidth requirements are not reported in the paper.
- **Privacy is Only Heuristic**: Unlike the provable privacy of MPC/HE, the privacy boundary of SFL/FL relies on the assumption that "raw data does not leave the device," with no formal guarantees against gradient leakage attacks; the paper does not elaborate on any attack experiments.
- **Training Protocol Cut to Fit Hardware**: A set of hyperparameters with batch size locked at 1, epochs fixed at 3, and momentum 0.6 is not fully searched (on-board MFCC is too slow, forcing hyperparameter search to be done in the PC simulation environment), and the ceiling of optimization quality is questionable.
- **Deployment Pipeline with Injuries**: The standard pipeline discovered by the authors PyTorch→ONNX→TensorFlow→TFLite failed because TFLite Micro does not support the Transpose operator (used to align the convolutional weight layout differences between PyTorch and TensorFlow), forcing manual weight transfer through Keras. The process is costly and error-prone—this is also the direct reason why on-board deployment testing was not completed in the end (Section 5.5).
- **Multi-Client is Serial Simulation**: There is only one real hardware client, and the rest are PyTorch virtual clients on the PC; the Parallel written in Algorithm 1 degenerates into Sequential in practice, and the point-to-point topology of the serial port does not support true parallelism.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Thin and Self-Collected Datasets**: CNdigits has only 350 samples, 50 per class, self-recorded by the author (single-speaker bias), and ENdigits has 7,000 samples taken from a subset of Speech Commands. There are no descriptions of speaker composition or channel conditions for either.
- **Confounding Variables in Key Comparisons**: The 13.89% advantage compares "Large CNN (SFL)" against "Small MLP (FL upper limit)," with capacity and training scheme tightly bound, making it impossible to separate "SFL algorithm gain" from "model capacity gain"; lacks centralized training upper bound, lacks multiple seed repetitions, and lacks statistical significance.
- **Broken Evidence Chain on Deployment Side**: Post-quantization accuracy, model volume, on-board latency, and energy consumption are all not reported, and the authors admit that final on-board deployment and testing were not completed—the third contribution (inference loop) stops at "conversion successful" without reaching "actual running test."
- **Low Manuscript Completion**: Conference template placeholders are not cleared, hardware bit-width typo ("2-bit" CPU), parameter arithmetic and numerical inconsistency (650×25+25×7 written as 4725), and chart axis information damaged during conversion. Specific numbers should be checked against the original text when citing.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper lists four directions (Section 5.5), which can be expanded with deep reading: First, **Bluetooth communication** to replace the serial port, getting rid of wired connections and approaching real deployment forms; Second, **On-board data collection and annotation support**—letting raw data truly be native to the device rather than simulated on the PC, completing the last link of the SFL privacy story; Third, **True parallel training for multi-clients**, requiring redesign of communication topology and aggregation scheduling; Fourth, **Instant deployment pipeline**—once the global model converges, the server automatically produces a compressed quantized version for distribution, automating the manual conversion link completed in this paper. The author adds three more valuable directions: Systematically ablate the impact of cut point depth and communication volume to find the Pareto frontier of "on-board memory - serial traffic"; practically test the risk of activation + label leakage under model inversion attacks and stack-validate with defense schemes (such as [6]); Use differential privacy noise or gradient compression to reduce communication volume per step, responding to the two inherent weaknesses of SFL. For KWS engineers, what can truly be taken away from this paper is that asymmetric structure: **training state memory is four times the number of parameters, inference state can be compressed to one-quarter of the number of parameters, using SFL to move training to the server, and using quantization to bring inference back to the device, with both sides crossing the wall of 256KB SRAM.**
