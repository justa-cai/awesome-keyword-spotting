## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The task of keyword spotting (KWS) is to identify a specified keyword from a segment of audio containing speech. Typical forms include command words (e.g., left, right, yes) and wake words (e.g., Hey Siri, Ok Google). These applications are now almost exclusively deployed on edge devices—smartphones, microcontrollers, and IoT devices—and often require always-on operation: KWS listens continuously, and upon detecting a wake word, triggers a higher-cost speech recognition model (either on-device or in the cloud). The paper lists five benefits of this edge computing workflow: power saving, bandwidth saving, privacy protection, reliability, and low cost with low latency. However, "always-on" operation also means that KWS is the only computational load running at full capacity year-round on the device. Every inference latency and every byte of memory usage is amplifiedaround the clock, so the resource constraints of microcontrollers directly determine product experience.

The paper provides specific hard constraint numbers for the STM32 series as a representative example (Section 1): On-chip Static Random Access Memory (SRAM) handles input/output, weight, and activation data storage during runtime, with capacities ranging from 20KB to 512KB; external DRAM can be added for larger capacity, but at the cost of higher power consumption and lower speed; Read-Only Memory (FLASH) for saving weights when powered off is typically between 64KB and 1MB; CPU (Cortex-M) clock speeds usually range between 72MHz and 216MHz. These three shackles (memory, storage, compute power) suppress neural network models along two dimensions: the number of parameters and the number of operations. A standard KWS pipeline is "audio feature extraction → neural network → post-processing." The challenge lies in fitting deep models into this pipeline while meeting low-latency requirements.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

Research on small models themselves has been quite extensive: small-sized DNNs (Chen et al., 2014), CNNs (Sainath & Parada, 2015), CRNNs (Arik et al., 2017), and LSTMs with max-pooling loss have all been proposed. On the compression side, Han et al.'s classic pruning work showed that approximately 90% of weights in convolutional layers can be removed without dropping accuracy. Considering that unstructured pruning is difficult to accelerate, Mao et al. proposed structured pruning with various granularities such as fine-grained, vector-level, kernel-level, and filter-level. The trade-off is "regularity vs. accuracy." Regarding quantization, Mishchenko et al. studied the application of low-bit quantization and quantization-aware training on small-sized KWS; ARM CMSIS also provides intrinsic function libraries for SIMD instructions.

However, the paper points out a gap: there is a disconnect between these works and their actual benefits on microcontrollers. There are four specific shortcomings. First, conclusions from existing pruning research mostly remain at the accuracy level ("removing 90% of weights doesn't drop accuracy") or at the acceleration level on server/GPU platforms; on scalar CPU microcontrollers, pruned computations do not automatically disappear—if the loop structure still iterates through every weight, multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations involving zero weights still consume full clock cycles, meaning "paper sparsity" is not realized. Second, research on structured pruning granularity trade-offs (Mao et al.) focuses on accuracy and regularity, lacking measured data on latency and power consumption on real MCU systems. Third, quantization research (Mishchenko et al.) focuses on accuracy, lacking first-hand measurements of end-to-end real-device performance ("how many milliseconds are saved by quantization, and how many more by combining with SIMD"). Fourth, many evaluations use bare-metal microbenchmarks instead of real systems, ignoring implementation-layer factors such as real-time operating system (RTOS) scheduling, DMA transfers, FPU usage, compiler optimization behaviors, and instruction pipelines—factors that can indeed render theoretical acceleration invalid. For example, a zero-skipping `if` statement might become a negative optimization due to pipeline interruption.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

The paper aims to address challenges at two levels. First, the engineering level: to truly run a complete KWS system ("audio capture → real-time feature extraction → model inference") on a Cortex-M7 microcontroller and provide credible baseline measurements—not simulated on a PC, but a real deployment with FreeRTOS scheduling, DMA interrupts, and FPU acceleration. Second, the evaluation level: on the same real system, systematically answer a set of engineering questions that have not been previously measured—Can unstructured pruning actually accelerate on microcontrollers? Is adding `if` statements to skip zero weights a gain or a loss? Is it worth changing the order of nested convolution loops? What is the real latency benefit of quantizing from float32 to int16? How much additional gain can SIMD instructions bring? Are these methods compatible with each other (e.g., will zero-skipping branch judgments disrupt the regular data access required by SIMD)? The answers to these questions directly determine which direction algorithm researchers should optimize when designing compression methods for edge devices.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall structure of the paper's baseline system is shown in Figure 1, and the hardware platform in Figure 2: the Alientek Apollo STM32F767 development board, with the main controller STM32F767IGT6, based on the Cortex-M7 core @216MHz, equipped with 512KB of static RAM. The system design can be viewed in three layers: the audio link, feature extraction, and scheduling/memory layout. Each layer reflects the motivation of "designing for real-time performance and measurability."

**Audio Link:** The WM8978 codec chip samples stereo audio at 16kHz. Data is automatically transferred to the microcontroller's double buffer via the I2S protocol using DMA, requiring no CPU intervention after initialization. Whenever one buffer is filled (approximately every 31ms), a DMA interrupt is triggered. In the interrupt service routine, single-channel new data is taken, normalized to float, and slid into the time-domain window buffer—50% old data is shifted out to the left, and 50% new data is shifted in from the right. A self-consistency verification worth expanding here: a 1024-point FFT at a 16kHz sampling rate corresponds to a 64ms window length. 50% overlap means a new spectral column is generated every 512 samples (32ms), which aligns with the DMA interrupt period of approximately 31ms. The Mel-spectrogram circular queue stores the latest 30 columns; 30 × 32ms ≈ 0.96s, which is the "approximately 1 second" context mentioned in the paper.

**Feature Extraction:** All performed on-chip. The time-domain window is first multiplied sample-by-sample by a Hanning window, then a 1024-point FFT is performed. This is multiplied by a pre-calculated Mel coefficient table stored in the microcontroller to obtain the output of a 40-band Mel filter bank. Finally, the logarithmic magnitude is taken to generate one Mel column. The entire feature extraction link takes approximately 6ms (Section 2.2). **Scheduling:** FreeRTOS manages two tasks: the feature extraction task has higher priority and can preempt the model inference task even if the model is currently inferring, ensuring the real-time nature of the audio stream without data loss; the inference task runs in a loop at a lower priority. At the start of each inference, it retrieves the latest Mel spectrogram via a FreeRTOS mutex lock and normalizes it to a normal distribution. **Memory Layout:** Model parameters are placed in on-chip SRAM, and the two largest possible activation buffers (6.5KB and 0.5KB) are placed in external DRAM—the reason is easy to infer: float32 parameters total 468.5KB, almost filling the 512KB on-chip SRAM. After deducting DMA buffers, the Mel coefficient table, and RTOS overhead, activation data must be externalized. Engineering details are reproducible: data is stored as 32-bit floats and floating-point operations are accelerated using the STM32's FPU; all code is written in C and compiled with ARM Compiler V6 at the fastest optimization level; latency is measured using hardware timers (TIM).

**Model Layer:** Deliberately simplified. The architecture basically copies `cnn-one-fstride4` from Sainath & Parada's 2015 work—the entire network has only one convolutional layer, followed by flattening and a four-layer DNN. The final 6 output neurons undergo softmax and average over the target label to obtain the decision. The convolution kernel width is 30, exactly equal to the full width of the input spectrogram's time axis, so there is no stride or pooling on the time axis; one convolution directly covers the full 1-second context. On the frequency axis, the kernel height is 8 and the vertical stride is 4 (50% overlap), so pooling on the frequency dimension is directly replaced by the vertical stride. Why design it this way: A single convolutional layer minimizes operator types and operation counts, satisfying edge device constraints; using stride instead of pooling saves a complete downsampling traversal; covering the full time axis with kernel width ensures each decision uses the full context, avoiding the complexity of time-domain sliding windows. Scale numbers (Section 2.2): Total parameters 119,936, of which 44,826 are in the convolutional layer and 75,110 in the four fully connected layers, occupying 468.5KB in float32; Total MACs 476,576, of which 401,760 are in the convolutional layer and 74,816 in the fully connected layers. These numbers allow us to reverse-engineer the structure: 44,826 = 186 × (1×8×30 + 1), i.e., 186 output channels, 1 input channel, kernel size 8×30, each with a bias; Number of frequency positions = (40−8)/4+1 = 9, number of time positions = 1, convolution MAC = 186 × 9 × 1 × 240 = 401,760, which perfectly matches the paper's numbers. The memory storage order of weights is out-channel → in-channel → row → col; the default calculation loop order for convolution is out-channel → out-row → out-col → in-channel → kernel-row → kernel-col—this "output-position-first" default order is precisely the target challenged by the weight-priority loop order later in the text. Baseline single inference takes approximately 31ms, and the system outputs a classification result approximately every 37ms (approximately 6ms feature extraction + approximately 31ms inference, Abstract and Section 4).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The focus of this paper's methodology is not on inventing new operators, but on clarifying the mechanisms of several known acceleration methods in the context of microcontrollers. The mathematical basis is as follows.

**Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) Count:** Convolutional layer MAC = Number of output channels × Number of frequency positions × Number of time positions × (Number of input channels × Kernel height × Kernel width). Substituting 186×9×1×(1×8×30) yields 401,760. The significance of this formula is that it exposes the different "dimensions" on which pruning can act: removing a single weight reduces the MAC count at the innermost level (fine-grained); removing an entire row within a kernel reduces the kernel height dimension (vector-level); removing an entire kernel or group of kernels reduces the output channel dimension (kernel-level/filter-level)—and only the latter can truly "reduce loop iterations" in the loop structure.

**Zero-Skipping Conditional Statements:** Adding `if (w == 0)` to skip within the MAC loop. Algebraically, this is equivalent to removing zero-term items from the sparse tensor summation, with the total MAC count decreasing proportionally to the sparsity. The cost is one comparison and one branch per weight. On a pipelined CPU, branches may incur the cost of pipeline interruption, and compilers may not optimize dense loops with branches as expected by the programmer—this is the mechanism explaining why fine-grained sparsity with `if` statements might actually become slower (Section 3.1).

**Loop Reordering:** Convolution and matrix multiplication are essentially multiple summations. Exchanging the order of summation does not change the mathematical result (strictly true for integer operations; float operations have rounding order differences, which the paper does not discuss). The default order uses output position as the outer loop, where the same kernel value is repeatedly loaded during iterations for different output points. The weight-priority order moves the kernel value dimension to the outer loop, completing calculations for all output points involving that weight in one traversal—if this weight is zero, a single `if` statement can skip the entire block of calculations. The amortized cost of zero-skipping judgment drops from "once per (weight, output point) pair" to "once per weight." Figure 4 provides a schematic of the two loop orders and compares memory access/access counts with `if` execution counts (specific values are not reported in the main text, only marked in the figure). Vector-level sparsity is implemented using a 3D mask array to mark deleted rows within kernels and adding `if` statements. Since this model has only one input channel, kernel-level and filter-level sparsity degenerate into the same thing—directly reducing the number of model output channels, accomplished at the code level.

**SMLAD Instruction (Figure 6):** This is one of the SIMD instructions for Cortex-M. One instruction multiplies two pairs of int16 values and accumulates them into one int32 value, i.e., {b1,b0}·{a1,a0}+c. Theoretically, this doubles the throughput relative to scalar int16 MAC. The paper uses it to process two adjacent weights and two adjacent activations simultaneously, serving as a second layer of acceleration beyond quantization. Quantization itself changes the representation of weights and activations from 32-bit floats to 16-bit integers, directly halving memory occupancy and memory traffic, and avoiding expensive floating-point operations.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Reproducible Complete Real-Time KWS System

The first contribution is actually a "measurement platform": from WM8978 capture, I2S/DMA double buffering, Hanning window plus 1024-point FFT plus 40-band Mel filtering plus logarithmic magnitude on-chip feature extraction, to CNN inference under FreeRTOS dual-task preemptive scheduling, the entire link runs in real-time on Cortex-M7 @216MHz, producing a classification result approximately every 37ms, with all code open-sourced. Why this counts as innovation: Most compression method evaluations either run theoretical numbers on PCs using simulators or only measure operator microbenchmarks on bare metal. Questions like "how much net gain do pruning/quantization provide in a real system" can only be measured accurately on a complete system with RTOS scheduling, DMA interrupts, FPU, and a real compiler. The existence of this platform gives credible grounding to all conclusions in Section 3 and allows others to reproduce and extend the work.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Weight-Priority Loop Order Accelerating Unstructured Pruning

Measurements in Section 3.1 (detailed later) show that unstructured pruning has almost no benefit under the default loop order, and adding `if` statements might even result in a loss. Thus, the paper tackles the memory access structure itself: reordering the nested loops of convolution to move the weight dimension to the outer loop, changing the zero-skipping judgment from one-to-one at the innermost level to judging each weight only once, thereby skipping all output point calculations involving zero weights in one go. Effect (Figure 5): Without zero-skipping, the weight-priority order is slightly faster than the default order. With zero-skipping, unstructured pruning only surpasses the default order when the pruning rate exceeds 80%, and at 90% weight removal, the speed reaches two times. The paper honestly gives the boundaries of this method: the combination of weight-priority loop order and zero-skipping may be incompatible with other microcontroller acceleration methods—mechanistically, it is easy to understand: SIMD requires adjacent data to be packed into pairs for one instruction, with regular and continuous memory access as a prerequisite, while zero-skipping by weight exactly disrupts this regularity.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Empirical Matrix of Compression Methods for MCU

The third contribution is a methodological "matrix-style measurement": placing five dimensions—pruning granularity (fine-grained/vector-level/kernel-level/filter-level), zero-skipping (`if` statement on/off), loop order (default/weight-priority), numerical type (float32/int16), and SIMD enablement (SMLAD on/off)—on the same real system to measure them individually and cross-combine them, forming combinatorial conclusions rather than single-point conclusions. This is not proposing new algorithms, but providing decision data for edge-side algorithm optimization: for example, "zero-skipping under default order is purely negative gain," "int16 plus SMLAD reliably gains nearly two times," "unstructured pruning is only valuable at sparsity above 80% and after changing the loop order." Such data has direct guiding significance for subsequent algorithm design (whether to go structured or unstructured, whether to go quantization or sparsity).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

**Compared with Sainath & Parada's `cnn-one-fstride4`:** The model architecture is directly inherited, but the original work solved the architecture search problem of "which type of CNN structure is suitable for small-sized KWS," with evaluation dimensions being accuracy and parameter count; this paper fully implements the architecture on an MCU real-time system and measures the real-device effects of compression methods, with a completely different focus.

**Compared with Zhang et al.'s Hello Edge:** Hello Edge cares about "which type of model can fit into a microcontroller while maintaining accuracy," still from a model selection perspective; this paper cares about "whether compression methods are actually faster and more power-efficient on microcontrollers," from a compression method perspective.

**Compared with Mao et al.'s sparse granularity research:** Mao studied the trade-off between "granularity regularity and accuracy" on conventional computing platforms; this paper moves the same granularity spectrum to a Cortex-M scalar CPU to study its impact on latency and power consumption, with conclusions even opposite to intuition—fine-grained sparsity with high accuracy is the hardest to realize acceleration, and regularity itself is valuable.

**Compared with Lai & Suda's CMSIS-NN:** That is operator library-level optimization (providing SIMD-integrated int8/int16 kernels); this paper performs measurements at the algorithm selection level on top of it and points out the compatibility issues between weight-priority plus zero-skipping and such acceleration libraries.

**Compared with Mishchenko et al.'s low-bit quantization research:** That is quantization-aware training research from an accuracy dimension; this paper supplements the real-device latency and power consumption dimensions.

In one sentence to summarize the difference: This paper translates "theoretical compression rate" into "real-device milliseconds and power consumption."

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

Training and testing use the Speech Commands dataset v0.01 (Warden, 2018). The paper selects 4 keywords: yes, no, left, right from it; another class, background, is constructed using sounds from the dataset's background folder, and the unknown voice class is constructed by sampling from other categories, totaling 6 classes, corresponding to the 6 output neurons of the model. Data scale (Section 2.1): 2000 training samples, 350 test samples, with validation accuracy of approximately 90% after 20 training epochs. It is worth pointing out that the full v0.01 set is an English command word dataset of approximately 65,000 samples, each one second long, with thirty word classes. The usage in this paper occupies only a tiny fraction of this, and the official test protocol was not adopted—this clearly indicates that the experimental positioning is "system feasibility and acceleration method measurement," not model research pursuing accuracy SOTA.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Three metrics. First, single inference latency: measured using hardware timer TIM, on real firmware with ARM Compiler V6 fastest optimization level and FPU enabled, rather than simulator estimates—the rationale is that the binding resource in microcontroller scenarios is real latency, and the deviation between theoretical MACs and actual latency is precisely the object this paper aims to expose. Second, power consumption: measured on the real development board configuration (Figure 2), curves shown in Figure 3, Figure 5, Figure 7; however, specific power values and measurement method details are not reported in the paper, with the main text only providing trend-based conclusions. Third, memory occupancy: parameters occupy 468.5KB in float32, dropping to nearly half after int16 quantization. Accuracy is not reported as a metric for compression experiments (only baseline is approximately 90%), which is an important premise for understanding the boundaries of this paper's conclusions.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

All comparisons in this paper are conducted within the self-built baseline, without direct head-to-head comparison with other published KWS systems, which needs to be stated first. Baseline float32 system: single inference takes approximately 31ms (Section 2.2; precise value is 30.8ms in the context of Section 3.3), and the system outputs a classification result approximately every 37ms. Quantization comparison (Section 3.3, Figure 7): After quantizing float32 to int16, inference latency drops from 30.8ms to 21.4ms (approximately 1.44x acceleration), and memory occupancy is nearly halved; further enabling SMLAD SIMD instructions reduces it further to 15.6ms. Relative to the float32 baseline, the total acceleration is approximately 1.98x, close to doubling; but relative to the pure int16 single-layer acceleration, it is only 21.4/15.6 ≈ 1.37x, lower than the theoretical two times of SMLAD—the reason is not analyzed in detail in the paper, but a reasonable explanation is that inference time includes softmax, normalization, data transfer, and loop overhead in addition to MACs, and these parts do not benefit from SIMD (this is the author's inference). Regarding power consumption, the improvements from quantization and SIMD are verified (Abstract and Figure 7), but specific milliwatt values are not reported. Accuracy comparison with external SOTA is not reported by the paper—neither are accuracy numbers for compressed models nor horizontal comparisons with contemporaneous models like DS-CNN or BC-ResNet, because the training protocol (2000-sample small subset) is incompatible with standard evaluation.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Section 3 as a whole is a set of ablation experiments, divided into three parts.

**Part 1, Pruning Granularity Ablation (Section 3.1, Figure 3):** Without adding `if` statements, the amount of zero values in weights has almost no impact on computation time and power consumption—the dense loop executes as usual, and MACs for zero weights still consume full cycles, directly proving that "paper sparsity" is not realized on scalar CPUs. Adding zero-skipping `if` statements to fine-grained (unstructured) sparsity may bring negative time effects, with the mechanism being that the instruction pipeline may be interrupted, and the compiler may not understand the code as expected. For structured pruning (vector-level and filter-level), advantages appear even when the pruning ratio is small, because they truly reduce the number of loop iterations or channels. Vector-level configuration is implemented using a 3D mask array plus `if`, while filter-level directly reduces channels in the code.

**Part 2, Loop Order Ablation (Section 3.2, Figure 5):** Without zero-skipping, the weight-priority order is slightly faster than the default order. With zero-skipping, CNNs with unstructured pruning start to be faster than the default order when the pruning rate is greater than 80%, and at 90% weight removal, the speed reaches two times. The paper also points out that the combination of weight-priority order and zero-skipping may be incompatible with other MCU acceleration methods. These numbers draw a clear engineering boundary: to enjoy the acceleration of unstructured pruning, sparsity must be above 80%, and one must bear the risk of conflict with other optimization methods.

**Part 3, Quantization and SIMD Ablation (Section 3.3, Figure 7):** Latency drops from 30.8ms to 21.4ms (int16), then to 15.6ms (with SMLAD); under int16 conditions, the comparisons from the first two parts were rerun—zero-skipping under default loop order is purely negative gain, while zero-skipping under weight-priority order still has benefits when pruning rate is greater than 80%, indicating that the loop order conclusion is robust across numerical types.

The combined conclusion from these three parts (Abstract and Section 4): For microcontrollers, there are significant challenges in accelerating unstructured pruning models; structured pruning is more friendly than unstructured pruning; the performance improvements from quantization and SIMD instructions are verified by measurements.

## Main Contributions

First, delivered and open-sourced a complete real-time small-sized KWS system: audio capture, feature extraction, and model inference all run on STM32F7 (Cortex-M7 @216MHz, 512KB SRAM), continuously outputting a classification result approximately every 37ms, providing a credible real platform for all subsequent acceleration evaluations. Second, provided first-hand system measurement data for compression methods on microcontrollers: covering independent and combined effects of five dimensions—four sparse granularities, zero-skipping `if` statements, two loop orders, int16 quantization, and SMLAD SIMD—filling the data gap between "theoretical compression rate" and "real-device milliseconds/power consumption." Third, proposed and quantified the weight-priority loop order as a software acceleration method for unstructured pruning, providing a clear applicable threshold (only surpasses baseline when pruning rate is above 80%, two times speed at 90%) and pointing out its compatibility risks with other acceleration methods. Fourth, consolidated judgments that can directly guide engineering and algorithm design: unstructured sparsity is difficult to realize acceleration on scalar CPUs, structured pruning is more friendly to MCUs, and quantization plus SIMD is a "no-brainer" combination optimization—these judgments have direct reference value for choosing edge-side model compression routes (whether to do structured sparsity or low-bit quantization).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, the model and task scale are too small: the setup of a single convolutional layer plus four-layer DNN, 6-class classification, and approximately 90% validation accuracy has a significant gap from modern KWS architectures (multi-convolutional layer residual structures) and accuracy levels (above 95% under standard protocols); on deeper networks, the time proportion of convolutional and fully connected layers and cache behavior will change, and whether the conclusions of this paper can be extrapolated needs verification. Second, the applicable window for unstructured acceleration is narrow: it is only effective when the pruning rate is above 80%, and the combination of weight-priority order and zero-skipping has known compatibility risks with other acceleration methods (such as the regular memory access requirements of SIMD), limiting practical usability. Third, loop reordering introduces rounding differences under float32 due to changed accumulation order, which the paper does not discuss (this is not an issue under int16). Fourth, the accuracy of compressed models is not reported by the paper, so readers cannot evaluate the quality cost of these acceleration methods, seeing only the latency and power consumption side. Fifth, the dimensions of each fully connected layer are not reported (the main text only gives aggregated parameter count 75,110 and MAC count 74,816); precise reproduction relies on the open-source code.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First, the data usage is too small: 2000 training samples and 350 test samples occupy only a few percent of the full Speech Commands v0.01 set, and the official test split was not used. The approximately 90% accuracy is not comparable to results from standard protocols in the literature, limiting the persuasiveness of the accuracy dimension. Second, power consumption measurement is opaque: the main text only presents power consumption trends via curves in Figures 3/5/7; specific values, measurement circuits/sampling methods are not reported in the paper, so power consumption conclusions can only be accepted qualitatively. Third, the platform is singular: measurements were only performed on Cortex-M7 (with FPU, dual-issue, cache), while mainstream low-power cores like Cortex-M0/M3/M4 without cache or FPU have very different pipeline and memory access behaviors, so extrapolating conclusions requires caution. Fourth, quantization only did int16: the paper mentions int8 but did not experiment with it, nor did it compare with optimized inference libraries that have become de facto standards like CMSIS-NN or TFLite Micro—these libraries' int8 plus SIMD kernels might change the relative benefits of each method. Fifth, there is a lack of joint accuracy-latency trade-off curves, making the evaluation of compression methods incomplete.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the directions stated in the paper, more embedded deep learning acceleration methods can be evaluated to effectively guide algorithms for edge device optimization. Specific expansions include: pushing quantization to int8 and introducing per-channel quantization and quantization-aware training, while reporting accuracy costs, to complete the trade-off curve; rerunning this experimental matrix on optimized kernels of CMSIS-NN or TFLite Micro to test whether the conclusions hold under industrial-grade inference stacks; extending the platform to Cortex-M0/M4 and other low-power cores like RISC-V and ESP32 to test the platform robustness of the conclusions; combining the weight-priority loop order with block sparsity and codebook compression (Han-style complete compression pipeline) to explore more realization paths for unstructured sparsity on the software side; and repeating the entire measurement set on larger models and standard evaluation protocols to close the chain of evidence for acceleration conclusions with accuracy evidence.
