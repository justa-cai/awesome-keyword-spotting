## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS, also known as wake-word detection) is the frontend entry task for almost all smart voice assistants today: when a user speaks a specific keyword, the assistant is awakened to provide services. Its engineering attributes are extremely unique—it runs continuously (always-on), performs continuous computation on edge devices, and has extremely strict power consumption and latency budgets. The corresponding Audio Tagging (AT) task involves outputting sound event class labels for a fixed-duration audio segment, such as cat meowing, dog barking, or knocking sounds. A typical application is assisting hearing-impaired individuals in perceiving inaudible events in the environment. These two tasks have long been regarded as two independent research fields in both academia and industry, each with its own datasets, benchmarks, and evaluation conference tracks.

However, the paper begins with a technical insight: if both are abstracted as "inputting a fixed-length audio segment (e.g., one second) and outputting a label indicating whether a keyword (KWS) or sound event (AT) is present," then they are the same task at the implementation level. This isomorphism brings three practical pain points:

First, **resource exclusivity**. The paper explicitly points out that in most real-world applications, these two tasks can only run one at a time—edge devices can only support one always-on model at a time. To possess both capabilities simultaneously, the traditional approach is to deploy two models, which doubles storage and memory usage, which is unacceptable for low-power voice frontends.

Second, **KWS's lack of noise robustness**. Standard KWS model label structures only have two classes: "target keyword" and "non-target (noise/unknown)." The non-target class is a large, unstructured hodgepodge with no internal structure. Training data (such as Google Speech Commands) consists of relatively clean close-talking speech, causing the false alarm rate to skyrocket when the model encounters music, babbling children, or interfering words with similar pronunciations in real scenarios. The paper quantifies this later using the Musan dataset: a baseline trained purely on GSCV1 achieves a rejection rate of only 84.88% under music/voice/babble noise (Table 3).

Third, **semantic blindness**. Traditional KWS models have no idea "what else is in this audio besides speech," making it impossible to support conditional wake-up logic such as "allow wake-up only when a child is speaking," nor can it report sound environment information to the user.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper points out shortcomings from the internal logic of two research lines:

**Shortcomings in the KWS research line**: The mainstream work in this field focuses on compressing parameters and improving inference speed (the paper cites [2]). Representative achievements include the TC-ResNet series (0.06M–0.3M parameters), neural architecture search models NAS1/NAS2 (0.28M/0.88M), and Keyword Transformer (KWT-2/KWT-3, 2.4M/5.3M). These models have achieved 96%–97% closed-set accuracy on the GSCV benchmark, but they share the same structural defect: the label system is "target word + a vague unknown bucket." The model is never taught to understand the semantic composition of non-target audio; it only knows "this is not my word." This binary rejection knowledge has naturally fragile extrapolation capabilities; performance degrades significantly when switching domains (e.g., foreign languages, music).

**Shortcomings in the AT research line**: The AT direction is performance-oriented and rarely focuses on deployment metrics such as inference time and model size. Although models like CNN14 (76M parameters) and AST (87M parameters) have high tagging accuracy, the paper points out incisively: existing AT models in the literature **cannot** be used for KWS because KWS is an always-on edge-side function, whereas these CNN/Transformer models require GPUs for fast inference. In other words, the AT community holds massive sound event semantic knowledge (the Audioset 527-class ontology) but lacks a carrier to deliver it to the edge-side KWS scenario.

**Three obstacles to joint training itself** (systematically梳理 in the paper's introduction): First, different levels of supervision intensity—large-scale AT training relies on weak supervision (only segment-level labels, time positions unknown) because strong annotation of large-scale datasets is too costly; whereas KWS is conventionally trained with strong supervision. Second, different forms of objective functions—AT must use multi-label objectives (music and human voice can coexist in one audio segment), while standard KWS uses single-label categorical cross-entropy. Third, different sample durations—KWS datasets generally consist of short segments of about one second, while AT datasets (Audioset) mainly consist of ten-second segments. Direct mixed training would cause severe padding mismatch. These three obstacles explain why no one has done unified modeling before: it wasn't that no one thought of it, but that each hurdle required a separate design scheme.

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

The core question the paper aims to answer is: Can a **single** lightweight model and **one** training pipeline simultaneously support both KWS and AT tasks, such that (1) the performance of each task does not degrade significantly; (2) the model remains small enough for edge-side always-on operation (the paper selected a 2.9M parameter MobileNetV2); (3) the sound event semantic knowledge contained in AT training data can truly translate into robustness improvements for KWS in real noise, rather than just treating Audioset as a regular noise source mixed in. Point (3) is the key to distinguishing this from "simple data augmentation"—the paper specifically sets up a control group in Section 4.2 called "Baseline w/AS" (treating Audioset as non-target noise added to CE training) to test this.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall idea of UniKW-AT is to **reconstruct KWS as a subtask of AT**: not by attaching AT to the KWS framework, but by standing from the perspective of the AT pipeline and treating keywords as a special type of "sound event." Given the KWS label set $L_{KWS}$ containing $K$ keywords and the AT label set $L_{AT}$ containing $C$ sound events, directly perform the union of label sets $L = L_{KWS} \cup L_{AT}$. Specifically in the experimental configuration: $L_{AT}$ takes the $C = 527$ class sound event ontology of Audioset, and then appends the $K = 10$ target keywords ("Yes/No/Up/Down/Left/Right/On/Off/Stop/Go" from GSCV1) to obtain $C + K = 537$ output nodes (Figure 1 explicitly draws "527 + 10 = 537 Labels").

Two label mapping decisions are worth digging into for their "why":

**Decision 1: Delete the traditional KWS "noise" label.** Conventional KWS models must have a non-target label, but in UniKW-AT, it is completely deleted. The reason is that the 527 classes of Audioset already cover most noise types—music, dog barking, wind and rain, TV sounds all have their own classes. This is the most important conceptual shift in the entire paper: upgrading "noise" from an unstructured rejection bucket to a group of explicit categories with semantics. The model changes from a negative judgment of "this is not my word" to a positive judgment of "this is music." The latter has much stronger generalization capabilities because each noise category is backed by real data. The jump in Musan rejection rate from 84.88% to 99.86% in Table 3 is the return on this design.

**Decision 2: Map all non-target keywords in the KWS dataset to the AT "Speech" label.** The 20 words in the original GSCV1 30 words that are not among the 10 target words are not treated as independent categories, nor as noise, but are uniformly mapped to the "Speech" (speech) event. This forms a two-level discrimination structure in the output space: first discriminate "is it speech," then discriminate "is it the target keyword." Target keyword samples will simultaneously activate two labels: (Speech, Target Word), while non-target speech only activates Speech. This essentially reproduces the hierarchical structure of "speech detection → keyword confirmation" in speech recognition using a multi-label mechanism, and is naturally friendly to open sets—any non-target speech in any language should fall under Speech. This explains the 99.91% rejection rate on VL106 (106 foreign languages) (Table 3).

**Data Duration Alignment**: Raw samples from both datasets are uniformly randomly cropped to the target duration $t$ (default 1 second). The backbone network selected is MobileNetV2 (2.9M parameters). The frontend features are 64-dimensional Log-Mel Spectrograms (LMS) at a 16 kHz sampling rate, with a 32 ms window length and 10 ms frame shift. Training uses the Adam optimizer, initial learning rate 0.001, batch size 64, for up to 500 epochs, with longest-sequence zero-padding within the batch. Model selection uses mAP as the primary validation metric, taking the top-4 models from the joint held-out set of the GSCV1 validation set and AS balanced for evaluation.

The motivation for this architecture selection is typical "appeasing both sides": for the KWS community, 2.9M is indeed on the large side (TC-ResNet8 requires only 0.06M); for the AT community, 2.9M is considered lightweight (CNN14 is 76M). The paper frankly admits in the discussion that MobileNetV2 is "can be considered too large" in the KWS context, but this is a pragmatic compromise between the two tasks—it is already one of the smallest tiers capable of covering the 537-class output space with reasonable accuracy.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

Let the input feature be $x$, the multi-label target be $y \in [0,1]^{(C+K)}$, and the trainable network be $F(\cdot)$, with prediction $\hat{y} = F(x) \in [0,1]^{(C+K)}$. The training objective is multi-label binary cross-entropy (Equation (1) in the paper):

$$L_{BCE}(\hat{y}, y) = y \log \hat{y} + (1 - y) \log(1 - \hat{y})$$

where $y$ can have multiple positive labels simultaneously. Each output node is an independent sigmoid probability, with no normalization coupling between nodes—this mathematical property is the foundation for the subsequent innovation of "strippable output layer."

The single-label post-processing on the inference side (Equation (2) in the paper) is the most interesting part algorithmically. Since multi-label output has label ambiguity—speech-related labels appear for both target and negative samples (target word outputs the pair (speech, keyword), non-target word only outputs speech)—to calculate KWS accuracy, it must first be compressed into a single label:

$$\bar{y} = \begin{cases} \arg\max \hat{y}_{KWS}, & \max \hat{y}_{KWS} \ge \gamma \\ \arg\max \hat{y}_{AT}, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where $\hat{y} = [\hat{y}_{AT}, \hat{y}_{KWS}]$, $\gamma$ is the decision threshold, and the default value $\gamma = 0.4$ is tuned on the GSCV1 held-out validation set. The semantics of this rule are: as long as the confidence of any keyword node exceeds $\gamma$, take the argmax within the keyword vocabulary; otherwise, fall back to the AT branch to take the argmax (which usually falls on Speech, correctly expressing "is speech but not a keyword"). $\gamma$ is a tunable sensitivity knob during deployment. Section 4.3 displays the two endpoints of its complete trade-off curve.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Label Space Merging and Noise Semanticization

As described above, the core action is to directly merge the 527-class Audioset ontology and $K$ keywords into a $C + K$ dimensional output. The value of this innovation lies not in the network structure (the structure is just a standard MobileNetV2), but in the **problem formulation**: KWS is formulated for the first time as a multi-label sound event detection problem. This naturally grants three capabilities that were impossible in previous KWS frameworks: (1) explicit noise semantics—each type of noise has a name and dedicated parameters; (2) sound event output—the model can tell the upper layer "there is music/dog barking in the environment now"; (3) post-processing space for conditional wake-up—any combination of AT labels can be used as wake-up prerequisites (the example given in the paper is "wake up only when a child is speaking"). These capabilities do not increase any inference cost; they simply expand the output dimension from 11 to 537.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Random Cropping + Pseudo Strong Labels (PSL) to Solve Duration Mismatch

This is the most hardcore part of the methodology. The difficulty lies in the fact that GSCV1 samples are all shorter than $t = 1$ s, while most Audioset samples are 10 s. A naive merging scheme can only zero-pad KWS samples to 10 s, which leads to a large amount of meaningless padding (the ablation in Section 4.4 proves that this path causes GSCV1 accuracy to collapse to 69.59%, Table 5). UniKW-AT chooses the opposite: **randomly crop** AT samples to 1 s to align with KWS duration.

However, random cropping immediately introduces the **label misalignment** problem: Audioset labels are weak labels at the 10 s segment level, and a randomly cropped 1 s segment may not contain the event referred to by the label—applying the "dog barking" label to a 1 s crop that happens to be quiet is feeding annotation noise to the model. The paper's solution is to cite the authors' own ICASSP 2022 work (Reference [16]): use a **pretrained machine annotator** to estimate soft labels for each random crop, obtaining Pseudo Strong Labels (PSL), which serve as the training target for the AT branch (Figure 1 upper part fully draws this data flow: AT dataset → random crop → machine annotator estimates soft labels → 527-dimensional target).

Note that the paper uses the phrase "partially solved," and the authors are aware that PSL can only alleviate rather than eradicate label misalignment. Ablation data (Table 5) shows the huge contribution of PSL: removing PSL and reverting to original hard labels causes AS mAP to drop from 33.42 to 20.77, a drop of 12.65 points; more surprisingly, GSCV1 also drops from 97.53 to 96.94—cross-task label noise pollutes the representation of the KWS branch through the shared encoder. Cleaning up this direction benefits both tasks simultaneously. This result also indirectly illustrates that the premise of joint training benefits is that both supervision signals are sufficiently clean; annotation noise in any one path will infect the other through shared low-level layers.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: BCE Objective and Strippable Output Layer (Ours-Strip)

Why insist on using BCE instead of adapting the softmax cross-entropy (CE) commonly used in KWS into the joint framework? The reason given by the paper is **engineering practicality during inference**: in real deployments, one often cares about only a small subset of the 537 classes. In a network trained with BCE, the output probabilities of each label are independent of each other. Therefore, the rows (weights and biases) corresponding to unwanted labels in the final linear layer can be physically deleted directly. The model becomes smaller, faster, and more power-efficient, and the performance of the remaining labels is unaffected. The paper denotes the version after deleting all AS output nodes as "Ours-Strip," reducing parameters from 2.9M to 2.2M, while GSCV1 accuracy remains unchanged at 97.53% (Table 2). In contrast, CE's softmax couples all categories into a single normalized distribution; deleting any node would change the probability semantics of the remaining categories, making it fundamentally unstrippable.

The significance of this design for productization is greater than it appears: manufacturers can crop the output subset according to region and scenario—for example, households with many music scenes retain (Music, Speech, Keywords) three classes for conditional wake-up, while low-end devices keep only $K + 1$ classes for pure wake-up. One training produces multiple deployment forms, a freedom that the CE framework cannot provide.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Thresholded Post-Processing and Conditional Wake-up

The thresholding mechanism in Equation (2) makes the "multi-label output → deployment decision" an explicitly tunable step. $\gamma = 0.4$ is the default value on the GSCV1 validation set, but the paper demonstrates the trade-off under different $\gamma$: lowering $\gamma$ to 0.01 (more sensitive, easier to report keywords) raises the Common set from 99.85% to 99.96%, but drops the Difficult set from 82.73% to 57.45% (Section 4.3). In other words, $\gamma$ directly controls the sensitivity/specificity exchange and can be set separately according to the deployment environment—use a low threshold in a quiet bedroom to reduce missed wake-ups, and a high threshold in a noisy living room to suppress false wake-ups. Coupled with the semantic output of the AT branch, conditional wake-up can be performed (e.g., requiring Speech and a certain sound event to be satisfied simultaneously), which constitutes the core argument for the "flexibility for real-world KWS" repeatedly emphasized in the paper.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to **dual-model schemes**: a KWS model + an AT model sums parameters and requires two runtimes, whereas UniKW-AT produces two semantics simultaneously with a single model and one forward pass. Compared to **Baseline w/AS** (mixing Audioset as a regular noise source into CE training): this is the most important control set up by the paper, because Table 3 shows that Baseline w/AS already achieves rejection rates of 98.90/99.81/99.80, indicating that the bulk of robustness indeed comes from the action of "introducing AS data" itself; the incremental value of UniKW-AT lies in (a) pushing the rejection rate up slightly further (GSCV2 tied at 98.90, VL106 +0.10 to 99.91, Musan +0.06 to 99.86), (b) retaining complete sound event output and conditional wake-up capabilities, and (c) strippable output layer. Compared to **large models in the AT community** (CNN14, AST, ResNet-50): these models require GPU-level computing power and cannot enter edge-side always-on scenarios at all; UniKW-AT achieves 33.42 mAP on balanced AS with 2.9M parameters, which is the strongest among similar models without ImageNet pre-training or additional data augmentation (Table 2). Essentially, the differentiator of this paper is unification at the **task formulation layer**, not innovation at the network structure layer.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Two datasets are used for training (Table 1 provides the complete scale):

- **GSCV1 (KWS Training)**: Google Speech Commands V1, totaling 65,000 clips of 30 keywords, read by thousands of people. The paper takes the common 11-class subset—10 target words (Yes/No/Up/Down/Left/Right/On/Off/Stop/Go), with the remaining 20 words mapped as non-target. The official train/validation/test split is 51,088 / 6,798 / 6,835 clips, with the training set totaling about 18 hours. Each sample is at most 1 second long. The test set composition is 2,567 target keyword clips + 4,268 non-target samples.
- **Audioset balanced subset (AT Training)**: The main text writes 60 hours, 21,292 clips of no more than 10 s, while Table 1 records 21,155 clips (58 hours). There is a slight discrepancy between the two numbers; it is safer to rely on Table 1.

Evaluation sets are divided into KWS and AT sides. On the KWS side, besides the official GSCV1 test set, three additional non-target evaluation sets (Eval-Neg) are built, all cut into 1 s segments to match training duration:

- **GSCV2 (In-domain negatives)**: Delete all 10 target words from GSCV2 to obtain 67,284 clips (18 hours) of negative samples;
- **Musan (Noise negatives)**: Three types of noise: music, voice, and babble, totaling 394,432 clips (110 hours), examining noise robustness;
- **VL106 (Out-of-domain negatives)**: VoxLingua107 minus English, 106 languages, randomly sampling 200 speech clips per language, 21,200 clips cut into 209,240 1-second samples (68 hours), examining cross-language extrapolation.

On the AT side, the official Audioset evaluation subset is used: 18,229 clips, about 50 hours, 10-second samples.

The real-world experiment (Section 4.3) uses private internal data from Xiaomi's "Xiao Ai Tongxue" voice assistant: the training set has 131 hours, 144,582 clips of 3-second speech (note that in the real product, the target duration $t$ was set to 3 s instead of the academic setting of 1 s, indicating that the framework is insensitive to $t$), of which 100,582 clips (70%) contain the target word "Xiao Ai Tongxue." Evaluation uses two sets: Common (93,800 clips / 80 hours, 50% positive and 50% negative) and Difficult (3,731 clips / 3 hours, all difficult negatives—similarly pronounced words like "Zhe Wei Tongxue," babbling children, music with human vocals, etc., typical false wake-up sources).

The design intent of this evaluation matrix is clear: GSCV1 manages closed-set accuracy, GSCV2 manages in-domain rejection, Musan manages noise, VL106 manages cross-language, and the real set manages product deployment—a complete funnel from academia to engineering.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

KWS uses accuracy, and AT uses mAP, which are standard metrics in their respective communities, ensuring direct comparability with literature numbers. However, for multi-label models, KWS accuracy must first pass through the single-label post-processing of Equation (2), which is a special feature of this paper's evaluation process; $\gamma = 0.4$ is tuned on the held-out set to avoid overfitting the threshold on the test set. During AT evaluation, only the $C$-dimensional output of $\hat{y}_{AT}$ is taken. Model selection uses mAP as the primary validation metric (on the joint held-out set of the GSCV1 validation set and AS balanced), taking the top-4 for evaluation—choosing mAP over accuracy for model selection is because mAP is more sensitive to the overall quality of the 537-dimensional output and can reflect the health of both branches simultaneously.

It is worth pointing out that KWS uses closed-set accuracy rather than the industry-standard "false rejection rate at a fixed false alarm rate" (e.g., FRR at 0.5 FA/h) or DET curves. This creates a conversion gap between the results and real deployment metrics; the paper does not report any false alarm/false rejection metrics calculated per hour.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Dual-task main comparison (Table 2)**: UniKW-AT (Ours, 2.9M) achieves 97.53% on GSCV1 and 33.42 mAP on the AS evaluation set. The reference frame on the KWS side: TC-ResNet8 (0.06M) 96.10%, TC-ResNet14-1.5 (0.3M) 96.60%, NAS1 (0.28M) 97.06%, NAS2 (0.88M) 97.22%, KWT-2 (2.4M) 97.36%, KWT-3 (5.3M) 97.24%, Wav2KWS (225M) 97.90%. That is, in the tier within 3M parameters, UniKW-AT's 97.53% is the highest; the only one exceeding it, Wav2KWS, has a parameter count about 80 times larger (225M vs 2.9M) and relies on large-scale speech representation pre-training. The reference frame on the AT side (numbers trained on balanced AS): EfficientNet-B2 without pre-training only achieves 15.70 mAP, AST 14.80, ResNet-50 (with ImageNet pre-training) 31.80, CNN14 27.80, MobileNetV2 (with pre-training) 26.50; EfficientNet-B0 with ImageNet pre-training is 33.50, B2 is 34.06. Reading these numbers honestly: UniKW-AT's 33.42 exceeds all opponents without external pre-training, and is only 0.08/0.64 mAP away from the pre-trained EfficientNet-B0/B2, while the parameter count is only 2.9M vs 5.3M/13.6M—under the constraint combination of "no pre-training, no augmentation, fewest parameters," it is the best. The paper also emphasizes that it has the smallest parameter count among all AT models participating in the comparison (2.9M). The discussion section provides another benchmark: exceeding the industrially common YAMNet (33.4 vs 30.6 mAP), with fewer parameters (2.9M vs 3.7M); however, the training recipe details of YAMNet are not aligned, so this comparison is for reference only.

**Noise robustness comparison (Table 3)**: Three columns of comparison—Baseline (pure GSCV1 CE training), Baseline w/AS (CE training + Audioset as non-target noise), Ours. Numbers: 97.63 / 97.75 / 97.53 on GSCV1; 97.47 / 98.90 / 98.90 on GSCV2; 83.48 / 99.81 / 99.91 on VL106; 84.88 / 99.80 / 99.86 on Musan. Three conclusions: (1) The baseline trained on clean data collapses severely out-of-domain (dropping 14 points on VL106, 13 points on Musan), confirming the pain point judgment in the introduction; (2) Adding AS data as noise solves the bulk of robustness (VL106 improves by 16.33 points); (3) UniKW-AT is not inferior to or better than Baseline w/AS on all non-target evaluation sets, at the cost of being 0.22 points lower on clean GSCV1 (97.53 vs 97.75). The authors themselves admit in the discussion that it is "only marginally better" than conventional KWS baselines, and the true claim is framework flexibility.

**Real-world comparison (Table 4)**: Compared to Xiaomi's internal KWS model Base-Inhouse, UniKW-AT achieves 99.85% vs 99.98% (slightly inferior by 0.13 points) on the Common set, and 82.73% vs 0% (crushing) on the Difficult set. A rejection accuracy of 0% for Base-Inhouse on difficult negatives means it suffered false wake-ups on all 3,731 clips of similar pronunciation, children's babbling, and music with vocals—this extreme numberon one hand confirms the powerlessness of traditional binary KWS in the face of semantic difficult negatives, and on the other hand suggests that the internal model may have made extreme threshold configurations for low missed wake-up rates (the paper does not report its configuration details). Tuning $\gamma$ to 0.01 can pull Common back to 99.96%, at the cost of dropping Difficult to 57.45%, fully displaying both ends of the threshold knob.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Table 5 breaks down two key components, with each row containing significant information:

**Random Cropping (−Crop)**: Remove random cropping and use the naive merging scheme of zero-padding KWS samples to 10 s. The consequence is catastrophic: GSCV1 collapses from 97.53% to 69.59% (dropping 27.94 points), and AS mAP drops from 33.42 to 17.37 (the worst in the table). Meanwhile, the numbers for the three negative sets actually look "better" (GSCV2 99.88, VL106 99.97, Musan 99.96)—but the paper explicitly points out this is an illusion: the model almost no longer detects the presence of keywords, becoming an extremely conservative rejection machine, and the high negative scores are the mirror image of the collapsed positive scores. This ablation row illustrates that **training/evaluation duration consistency is the foundation of the entire framework**: after KWS samples are zero-padded to 10 s, the keyword occupies only one-tenth of the receptive field, and the input statistics are completely misaligned with the distribution during 1 s evaluation. It also warns future researchers: the most inconspicuous "data engineering" (cropping alignment) in joint training is more fatal than any structural design.

**Pseudo Strong Labels (−PSL)**: Remove the machine annotator and directly use Audioset original hard labels to supervise 1 s random crops. AS mAP drops from 33.42 to 20.77 (dropping 12.65 points), GSCV1 drops from 97.53 to 96.94, and GSCV2/VL106/Musan all see slight declines across the board. Two levels of interpretation: the significant degradation on the AT side directly reflects label misalignment (10 s weak labels applied to arbitrary 1 s crops, where a large number of crop segments have no event occurring, equating to systematic annotation noise); the drop on the KWS side indicates that the shared encoder absorbs label noise from the other task, and the cleanliness of the two supervision paths is coupled through low-level representations. The paper specifically uses "Surprisingly" to describe the rise of GSCV1 from 96.94 to 97.53 with PSL—cross-task implicit benefits are a previously unexpected property of the joint framework.

Incidentally, record two internal numerical inconsistencies in the paper for attention in future citations: Table 3's Ours is 98.90 on GSCV2, while Table 5's Ours is 98.92; the conclusion section writes "rejection 99.86% on VoxLingua106," while the VL106 numbers in Table 3/5 are 99.91 (99.86 is the Musan number, suspected to be a typo). There is also a small discrepancy between the main text's AS training set of 21,292 clips and Table 1's 21,155 clips.

## Main Contributions

1. **First proposal and verification of unified modeling for KWS and AT** (the paper calls it "first attempt at joint KWS and AT modeling"). The contribution occurs at the task formulation layer: KWS is redefined as a subtask of multi-label sound event detection, with the label set directly taking the union (527 + 10 = 537), combined with two mapping decisions: mapping non-target words to Speech and deleting the noise label, forming a complete, reusable recipe.
2. **Solved cross-task duration mismatch using random cropping + pseudo strong labels**, and provided hardcore ablations: not using cropping causes GSCV1 to collapse by 27.94 points, and not using PSL causes AS mAP to drop by 12.65 points. These two ablations have direct reference value for anyone wanting to do multi-task audio joint training.
3. **Proved dual-task performance feasible on edge devices**: A single 2.9M model achieves GSCV1 97.53% + AS 33.42 mAP, which was the AT model with the fewest parameters at the time, and does not require ImageNet pre-training or data augmentation (Table 2).
4. **Advanced noise robustness from "adding noise data" to "semanticizing noise"**: Comprehensively outperforms baselines on three out-of-domain sets (Musan/VL106/GSCV2) (Table 3), and improves rejection of difficult negatives on real product data from 0% to 82.73% (Table 4).
5. **Engineering byproducts**: The strippability of output nodes brought by BCE (Ours-Strip, 2.9M → 2.2M, no loss in accuracy) and the deployability of the $\gamma$ threshold provide mechanisms for "one training, multiple deployments."

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**Parameter count is still too large for the KWS community**. The paper itself admits that MobileNetV2 (2.9M) "can be considered too large within the KWS community"—compared to TC-ResNet8's 0.06M, it is nearly 50 times larger. Although Ours-Strip can compress it to 2.2M, there is still an order of magnitude gap from true micro KWS models, and there is no accompanying quantization/pruning/distillation. For MCU-class devices with memory budgets in the hundreds of KB range, this solution is currently not viable.

**Perceptible accuracy tax on clean data**. On GSCV1, 97.53% is lower than Baseline w/AS's 97.75% (Table 3) and also lower than the large model Wav2KWS's 97.90%. The multi-label 537-dimensional output dilutes model capacity, and the threshold post-processing in Equation (2) introduces additional decision friction. The paper's stance is to exchange 0.22 points of clean accuracy for out-of-domain robustness and flexibility, which is usually worthwhile in products, but is indeed a net loss in closed-set metrics.

**Doubtful source of robustness gains**. Table 3 shows that Baseline w/AS (simply mixing Audioset as noise into CE training) already achieves VL106 99.81 / Musan 99.80, and UniKW-AT's incremental gain is only +0.10 / +0.06 points. The honest conclusion is: the bulk of robustness comes from "introducing AS data," and the net incremental gain of the multi-label semantic framework is small; its irreplaceable value lies in AT output and conditional wake-up capabilities, not in the rejection rate itself. The discussion section expresses this frankly, but the abstract's wording may lead readers to overestimate the framework's contribution to robustness.

**PSL relies on an external machine annotator**. Pseudo strong labels come from a pretrained annotation model (the authors' previous work [16]), meaning (a) the training pipeline has one more component that needs maintenance; (b) the quality upper limit of the annotator is the quality upper limit of PSL, and its inference cost is not reported by the paper; (c) "partially solves" label misalignment (the paper's original words), and residual misalignment still exists. For new categories/languages, the coverage capability of the annotator is also an implicit constraint.

**Conditional wake-up lacks quantitative experiments**. Capabilities like "wake up only when a child is speaking" are proposed as potential uses in the discussion, but there is not a single experimental number for conditional wake-up in the entire paper—while the 537-dimensional output logically supports it, the paper does not verify it.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

**Lack of deployment-side metrics**. The entire paper does not report any of inference latency, real-time rate, memory usage, or power consumption—which is the primary question for KWS papers and the basis for the authors' choice of a lightweight backbone. Without latency numbers, "edge-side feasible" is only an inference at the parameter level.

**KWS metric system is too academic**. Closed-set accuracy + full negative set rejection rate, without the industry-standard false rejection rate at a fixed false alarm rate (e.g., trade-off curves/DET for FA/h and FR), and $\gamma$ is only tuned on a single validation set. There is no direct conversion between the acceptance metrics of real wake-up systems and the paper's metrics.

**Several comparisons are not aligned**. The YAMNet comparison (30.6 vs 33.4 mAP) does not explain whether the opponent's training configuration is comparable; the extreme result of Base-Inhouse being 0% on Difficult is not accompanied by its threshold/configuration description; the augmentation/pre-training markers († and ?) for various baselines in Table 2 are mixed, and only some rows are strictly comparable under the same constraints. In addition, the paper itself has several numerical inconsistencies (GSCV2 98.90/98.92 in Table 3 vs Table 5, conclusion text 99.86% vs table VL106 99.91, main text 21,292 vs Table 1's 21,155), which need to be verified by the reader when citing.

**Scale ceiling not explored**. On the AT side, only the balanced 58-hour subset was used; the full 2 million clips of Audioset were not used; the number of keywords was only verified for $K = 10$ (academic) and $K = 1$ (real product), and the scalability of multiple wake words is unknown; the real experiment only covers the Chinese monolingual scenario.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

Directions pointed out by the paper itself: finding a **better backbone network**—finding a point more suitable than MobileNetV2 between the size constraints on the KWS side and the semantic capacity on the AT side (looking back now, temporal convolutions like TC-ResNet/MatchboxNet or combinations of distillation + quantization are natural candidates); and **multiple post-processings for different environments**, making $\gamma$ thresholds and conditional wake-up configurable strategies.

Based on the full-text analysis, the author believes there are several directions worth advancing: (1) **Access the full Audioset unbalanced data**; the PSL mechanism was originally designed for large-scale weak supervision, and the balanced subset only uses a small part of its potential; (2) **Complete delay/power/memory measurements**, and combine them with quantization and distillation pipelines to compress 2.2M–2.9M into the true micro KWS tier; (3) **Quantitative verification of conditional wake-up**, for example, constructing a hierarchical test set of "music with human vocals + children's speech" to actually measure the false wake-up suppression rate of the (Speech ∧ Target Word) combination logic, turning the paper's biggest flexibility selling point into hard numbers; (4) **Multilingual/multi-keyword expansion**; the 99.91% rejection rate on VL106 suggests that the Speech label has good cross-language generalization, which is worth re-verifying on multilingual wake-up products; (5) **Hierarchical output space**, using the tree structure of the Audioset ontology to do hierarchical softmax or loss weighting, which may further alleviate the capacity dilution problem of the 537-dimensional output; (6) **From tagging to temporal localization (SED)**, allowing the unified framework to output event time boundaries simultaneously, serving longer interaction windows.

As the pioneering work in this direction (the authors call it an initial approach), the value of UniKW-AT lies more in confirming the feasibility of the formulation "KWS is a sub-problem of AT" and providing a map of pitfalls (duration alignment, PSL, BCE strippability, threshold post-processing). There is still a large expanse of unexplored land on this map for subsequent work.
