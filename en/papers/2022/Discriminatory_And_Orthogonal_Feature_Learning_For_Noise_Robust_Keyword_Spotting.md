- **Date**: October 2022 (arXiv:2210.11519v1, submitted 2022-10-20, IEEE Journal Letter format)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11519
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, noise robustness, metric learning, spectral norm, orthogonal regularization, dynamic filter, small-footprint model

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is a persistent component in embedded speech interfaces such as smart speakers and wearable devices: devices need to listen for wake-word commands around the clock, which means the model must run continuously on processors with extremely limited computational power and energy consumption. The paper's introduction explicitly states that because KWS applications operate in a streaming environment, the network must be minimized in design to improve model efficiency; **model parameter count and FLOPS are key elements in evaluating KWS performance**. This constraint defines the boundaries for all designs in this paper: any improvement method cannot exceed the parameter and computational budget on the inference side.

The second pain point is that real-world usage environments are rarely clean. Household TV sounds, street traffic, and café voices all superimpose additive noise onto the wake-word speech, and the noise encountered after device deployment often does not match the noise distribution in the training data (unseen noise). Under low Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) conditions, a model with 97% accuracy on a clean test set may drop below 75% (in Table II, the baseline LDy-TENet12 achieves only 75.92% under WHAM 0dB, and TENet12 achieves 75.19%). Therefore, the problem is not "whether it can be accurate on clean data," but "whether it can flatten the collapse slope of the model under unseen noise without adding inference costs."

The authors' previous work (Reference [19], LDy-TENet) already provided a lightweight solution: attaching a lightweight dynamic filter to the front end of a temporal CNN to extract robust feature maps in noisy environments, and jointly training them end-to-end with KWS classification weights using Cross-Entropy (CE) loss. This approach is structurally efficient, but this paper observes a fundamental flaw in its supervision signal: **CE loss only requires features to be "separable," not "stable."** The geometric behavior of CE is to find a decision boundary that separates classes; as long as the boundary is separable, it satisfies the requirement. However, noise robustness requires the geometric structure of features to remain stable under noise perturbation—the paper's original words are "the network is trained to find separable features for the classification task, while robust features require the network to find robust domains." These two are not equivalent, which is the gap this paper aims to fill.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing routes for noise-robust speech processing into three types and points out their shortcomings in the KWS scenario one by one:

- **Generative Speech Enhancement Route**: Denoising Autoencoders (DAE) [1]-[3] reduce noise by minimizing the distance between the decoder output and clean speech; GANs [4], Griffin-Lim iteration [5], and end-to-end waveform enhancement directly optimizing evaluation metrics [6] also belong to this category. The shortcoming is that these methods aim to reconstruct clean signals, resulting in high front-end computational costs. The paper explicitly states that such high-computation front-ends do not match the resource-constrained nature of KWS—equipping a heavy enhancement network for a persistent listening task is infeasible within energy budgets.
- **Classification Loss-Driven Front-End Feature Enhancement**: Instead of explicitly reconstructing clean signals, the enhancement module is placed at the front end of the main task model and jointly optimized using classification loss [7], [8]. This route saves the enhancement objective, but the supervision signal is still only CE—as mentioned above, CE cannot explicitly constrain the stability of feature geometry under noise. The dynamic filter in the previous work [19] belongs to this category and is constrained by it.
- **Direct Application of General Metric Learning**: Traditional discriminative embedding learning methods, including center loss [20], FaceNet triplet loss [21], DeCov decorrelation regularization [22], multi-similarity loss [23], circle loss [24], etc., combined with sampling strategies or circular decision boundaries, can indeed improve embedding discriminability. However, Reference [25] points out their structural defect: **pairwise cosine or Euclidean distance-based methods tend to favor dominant classes (dominant classes) that are easier to train or have larger training sample sizes**—when enumerating all sample pairs within a mini-batch, the number of sample pairs for large classes dominates the gradient at a quadratic level. The consequence is that these methods perform well under high SNR conditions close to clean data, but degrade under low SNR conditions where class contributions are flattened by noise (the experimental phenomenon in Table III directly supports this).

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Synthesizing the above, the core problem to be solved in this paper can be broken down into four interlocking challenges:

1. **How to translate "robust features" into an optimizable mathematical objective.** Robustness is a vague term; the paper's answer is to concretize it into three geometric quantities in the embedding space: inter-class variance (to be large), intra-class variance (to be small), and inter-class orthogonality (to be uncorrelated), and to explain that these three quantities fall exactly within the coverage of metric learning.
2. **How classification objectives and geometric objectives can coexist.** Multiple losses in training may lead to gradient direction competition (from the perspective of multi-task multi-objective optimization cited in [28]); direct weighted summation may oscillate, requiring the fusion of competing geometric objectives into a single stable optimization object.
3. **Inference cost must be strictly zero increment.** Any branch, layer, or loss added for training robustness cannot enter the test-time computation graph; otherwise, it violates the KWS parameter/FLOPS budget.
4. **Avoiding dominant class bias in pairwise metrics.** Geometric supervision should act on class-level statistics (class centers) rather than sample pairs, allowing each class to contribute naturally balanced gradients.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall pipeline is shown in Fig. 1: Input audio features first pass through a **Lightweight Dynamic Conv** (inheriting the dynamic filter from previous work [19]), whose output goes one way to the **KWS Model** (backbone is TENet12 [12]) to produce classification results and calculate CE loss; the other way to a **Dynamic Embedding Model** (consisting of two layers of temporal convolution T-Conv plus ReLU plus temporal average pooling TAP) to produce embedding vectors and calculate triplet loss; additionally, class-wise loss is applied to the embedding vector of the KWS model itself (output of the final CNN layer after global average pooling). The three losses jointly update the entire network. **The dynamic embedding model exists only during the training phase and is discarded entirely during the testing phase**; class-wise loss also does not participate in inference, so the inference graph is exactly the same as the previous work LDy-TENet12—this is why the parameter count (102K) and FLOPS (6.64M) of LDyLMIO-TENet12 and LDy-TENet12 in Table I are identical digit by digit.

The design motivation for this "two-way metric learning" deserves elaboration:

- **Why give the dynamic filter a separate embedding head**: If geometric constraints are only applied at the end of the classifier, the front-end dynamic filter can only benefit indirectly through backpropagation through the entire network. After passing through more than ten layers of convolution in TENet12, the gradient signal will be diluted and its direction blurred. Directly connecting a lightweight embedding head with two layers of temporal convolution to the filter output is equivalent to letting the gradient of geometric supervision reach the filter weights in one step, directly carving the spatial structure of front-end features. This is the reason for decoupling "filter-side supervision" and "classifier-side supervision."
- **Why choose TENet12 as the backbone**: TENet [12] is a multi-scale temporal convolution small-footprint KWS network, stacking 12 convolutional blocks and 1 fully connected layer using depthwise plus pointwise temporal convolutions, with 32 output channels per block. The original version has 100K parameters / 6.42M FLOPS (Table I), making it a representative backbone for small-footprint KWS at the time, and also the host network for the dynamic filter in previous work [19]. Continuing to use it allows cleanly isolating "loss function changes" as the single variable.
- **Why apply class-wise loss to the global average pooling output**: The GAP output is a 32-dimensional fixed-length vector (experimental configuration, Section III-A), with low dimensionality and condensed semantics, naturally suitable as a carrier for class-center statistics, and requiring no additional layers.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**(1) Dynamic Embedding and Triplet Loss (Eq. 1, Eq. 2)**. The dynamic embedding model calculates for input $x_i$:

$$H_i = TAP(Conv(\max(0, Conv(x_i, w_1)), w_2))$$

where $w_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{[K,F,F_{df}]}$ and $w_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{[K,F_{df},F_{dim}]}$ are the learnable variables for metric learning, and TAP performs feature averaging over the time dimension. **Why TAP is needed**: The output of temporal convolution is still a feature sequence with variable time steps, while metric learning requires calculating distances pairwise within a mini-batch. It is necessary to first average out the time dimension to compress it into a fixed-length vector of $F_{dim}$ dimensions. In the experiment, $w_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{[9,40,40]}$, $w_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{[9,40,128]}$, with a stride of 2 (Section III-A), meaning the dynamic embedding is 128-dimensional. The following is calculated on it:

$$L_M = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \sum_{j=1}^{M} y_{ij}^* \, \|H_i - H_j\|_2^2 + \alpha$$

$y_{ij}$ is +1 for the same class and −1 for different classes, $\alpha$ is the margin, and $M$ is the mini-batch size. This loss minimizes the Euclidean distance of intra-class embeddings and maximizes the Euclidean distance of inter-class embeddings. It needs to be pointed out faithfully: the paper calls it triplet loss [21], but the formula form is a signed distance with margin for all sample pairs within the batch, which is closer to contrastive loss in form; the meaning of the superscript asterisk is not further defined in the paper, and must be inferred by oneself during reproduction (this is a discrepancy between name and reality in the paper's writing).

**(2) Intra-class Compactness Loss (Eq. 3)**. On the KWS model embedding $E$, subsets $E^k \in \mathbb{R}^{[D,M_k]}$ (the $k$-th class, $D$ dimensions, $M_k$ samples) are constructed by class, minimizing the distance of each sample to its class center:

$$L_I = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{k=1}^{C} \sum_{i=1}^{C_k} \|E_i^k - \bar{E}^k\|_2^2$$

where $C$ is the number of classes, and $\bar{E}^k$ is the class center of the $k$-th class. This term directly corresponds to the design goal of "intra-class variance should be small." The paper emphasizes that using Euclidean distance for class variance simultaneously serves the pair of goals "increasing inter-class distance and decreasing intra-class distance."

**(3) Class Center Matrix and Two Geometric Quantities (Eq. 4, Eq. 5, Eq. 6)**. Splicing the $C$ class centers into a matrix $E' = [\bar{E}^1, \bar{E}^2, ..., \bar{E}^c] \in \mathbb{R}^{[D,C]}$ (Eq. 4), then defining two $C \times C$ matrices:

- **Inter-class Distance Matrix** $MDM$ (Eq. 5): Elements are the squared Euclidean distances between each pair of class centers, with diagonal elements being zero. Its Frobenius norm is expected to be **maximized**—class centers push each other away.
- **Class Center Covariance Matrix** $MIM$ (Eq. 6): $MIM = \frac{1}{C-1}(E' - \bar{E}')(E' - \bar{E}')^T$, where $\bar{E}'$ is the global mean vector of all class centers. It is expected that $MIM = I$: zero off-diagonal elements mean class centers are decorrelated (orthogonal), and diagonal elements of 1 mean each class variance is normalized. This term corresponds to the design goal of "inter-class orthogonality."

**(4) Spectral Norm Fusion Loss (Eq. 7)**. Two geometric objectives, one to be maximized and one to be minimized, and the paper points out that they compete with each other in certain stages of training (citing [28]), so they must be fused into a single minimization objective:

$$L_O = SN(p(MIM) + e^{-MDM} - I)$$

where $SN(\cdot)$ is the spectral norm. This formula condenses four ingenious design decisions, each with a clear "why":

- **Matrix filter $p(\cdot)$ maps diagonal elements to zero**: Orthogonality is determined only by the off-diagonal elements of $MIM$. If diagonal elements (variance normalization) are mixed into the same regularization, it introduces redundant constraints, so they are zeroed out first.
- **Exponential function $e^{-MDM}$**: Flips "maximizing class center distance" to "minimizing an exponentially decaying term," achieving two birds with one stone—unifying the optimization direction and compressing the value range of $MDM$, so that the influence of the $\|MIM - I\|$ component is extended to a larger range. The paper's reason is "inter-class orthogonality itself also promotes inter-class distance," so the orthogonality term should dominate and the distance term should follow with amplitude limiting, avoiding unbounded growth of the distance term overpowering the orthogonality constraint.
- **Using spectral norm instead of Euclidean distance as the minimization object**: The paper explicitly claims "minimizing the spectral norm of the loss function shows more stable and improved performance than directly applying Euclidean distance loss." The intuition is that the spectral norm only constrains the maximum singular value of the matrix—i.e., it only penalizes divergence in the worst direction, and is a soft constraint on the rest of the spectral structure, unlike element-wise norms that pull the entire matrix to zero (pulling class center distances to zero as well), making training more stable.
- **Power iteration method to calculate spectral norm, with optimal iteration count of 10**: The paper determined this through experiments; this is one of the sources of training overhead (but the paper does not report the quantitative value of this overhead).

**(5) Total Loss (Eq. 8)**:

$$L_{Total} = L_{CE} + \lambda_1 L_M + \lambda_2 L_I + \lambda_3 L_O$$

Weights are taken as $\alpha = 1$, $\lambda_1 = 0.25$, $\lambda_2 = 0.01$, $\lambda_3 = 0.01$. **Why $\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3$ are pressed so small to 0.01**: The paper's empirical observation is that $L_I$ and $L_O$ have a stronger influence in training than other learning losses; if not suppressed, they will interfere with the learning of $L_{CE}$—i.e., if geometric regularization is too strong, it will sacrifice classification accuracy, so small coefficients are used to protect the basic classification performance. The paper names the combination of $L_M + L_I + L_O$ as **LOVO loss** (the abbreviation is written as "LOw Variant Orthogonal" in the abstract and "LOw Variability Orthogonal" in Section II, which is inconsistent; recorded faithfully; translated by content meaning as "Low Variability Orthogonal" loss).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Two-Way Decoupled Metric Learning Framework

The innovation is not in any single loss (triplet loss [21], class center idea [20], orthogonal regularization [26] are all existing components), but in **splitting metric learning into two paths according to network responsibilities and applying them separately**: the filter side uses sample-pair-wise triplet/contrastive loss to directly optimize the separability of front-end features, and the classifier side uses class-center-level loss to optimize the end discriminative geometry. The deep reason for this decoupling is gradient path economics—supervision signals should be applied at the position closest to the object being optimized. The responsibility of the dynamic filter is to "extract robust feature maps in noise," so directly connecting a 2-layer convolution embedding head to its output to calculate distance loss is more efficient and unambiguous than backpropagating from the end of the classifier through more than ten layers. A side product is that this embedding head is discarded after training, and the robustness benefit is entirely沉淀 (precipitated/settled) in the filter weights, with zero change in the inference graph.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Class-Center (Non-Pairwise) Metric Learning to Avoid Dominant Class Bias

Unlike the pairwise distance paradigm of [20]-[24], the $L_I$ and $L_O$ in this paper act on the **class center**, a class-level statistic. Why this avoids bias: Pairwise methods enumerate all sample pairs within a mini-batch, and classes with large sample sizes contribute $O(M_k^2)$ pairs of logs, with gradients dominated by dominant classes [25]; whereas class centers compress each class into a single point, regardless of the number of intra-class samples, the contribution structure of each class to the loss is essentially one unit, making gradients naturally class-balanced. The paper reverse-verifies this mechanism with experimental phenomena in Table III: pairwise methods (FaceNet [21], multi-similarity [23], circle loss [24]) are slightly better under clean and high SNR conditions (because dominant class bias is harmless under clean conditions, and pairwise supervision is more detailed), but systematically lag behind non-pairwise methods under low SNR, while this paper's method leads in all low SNR conditions (see Experimental Results section for details).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Spectral Norm-Driven Joint Orthogonal-Distance Regularization

Eq. 7 encapsulates two geometric objectives with opposite directions, which would compete with each other (maximizing class center distance vs. diagonalizing class center covariance to $I$), into a single minimization object, taking only the maximum singular value. The difference from the closest related work is clear: DeCov [22] decorrelates representations by minimizing the off-diagonal terms of the activation covariance to reduce overfitting, acting on per-sample activations; Spectral Normalization [29] was originally used for Lipschitz constraints on GAN discriminators, acting on weight matrices; the object of action in this paper is the **covariance of the class center matrix**, and it simultaneously encodes the distance term (amplitude-limited via exponentiation). This is equivalent to designing a specialized regularizer for class center geometry that only exerts force in the worst direction (spectral norm only looks at the maximum singular value), rather than directly moving a general-purpose tool.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **For Generative Enhancement (DAE/GAN/Griffin-Lim [1]-[6])**: Does not reconstruct clean signals, but directly shapes the geometry of discriminative features; zero overhead on the inference side, whereas generative front-ends have high computational costs, conflicting with KWS resource constraints.
- **For CE-only Feature Enhancement Front-ends ([7], [8] and previous work [19])**: Explicitly adds three geometric constraints (intra-class compactness, inter-class openness, inter-class orthogonality) in addition to CE, making up for CE's defect of only finding decision boundaries without guaranteeing geometric stability; and retains the dynamic filter structure of [19], with improvements being purely in the training phase.
- **For General Metric Learning ([20]-[24])**: Supervision moves from the sample-pair level to the class-center level, avoiding dominant class bias; and the loss is spread to both the filter side and the classifier side, rather than just hanging on the classifier embedding.
- **For General Regularization (DeCov [22], Spectral Normalization [29], Orthogonal Regularization [26])**: The regularization object is the class center covariance rather than activations or weights, and the distance objective and orthogonality objective are fused into the same spectral norm loss, solved using power iteration (10 times).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

- **Training/Evaluation Main Dataset: Speech Commands v1** [27]. The paper states: The dataset contains 30 keywords, 10 keywords are selected plus two extra classes (unknown and silence) for model training—i.e., the standard 12-class division (10 target words + unknown word class + silence class). The split ratio is 80% training, 10% validation, 10% testing. Training follows the dataset's official guidelines: injecting background noise (mike noise) and performing random time shifts. **Note that the model never saw the following three noise libraries during training**, which forms the basis for "unseen noise" evaluation.
- **Unseen Noise Libraries (Noise Mixing Only in Testing)**: DCASE 2019 [30], Urbansound8K [31], WHAM [32], all urban background noises. The noise mixing method is to randomly extract audio samples from the noise library and mix them with the original test data, with SNR set to five levels: 20dB, 15dB, 10dB, 5dB, 0dB.
- **Feature and Training Configuration** (Section III-A): Input is 40-dimensional MFCC, 30ms window, 10ms overlap (retold according to the paper's original text), 64 Mel filters; batch size 100, trained for 30K steps, Adam optimizer with initial learning rate 0.001, decaying by 0.1 times every 10K steps; temporal convolution weights of the dynamic embedding model $w_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{[9,40,40]}$, $w_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{[9,40,128]}$, stride 2; KWS embedding used for LOVO loss takes the output of the TENet12 global average pooling layer, dimension 32. Each setting is repeated 8 times (but the paper does not report variance or standard deviation).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Classification Accuracy (%)**: Reported separately under clean conditions and for each noise library × each SNR level, which is the口径 (caliber/standard) aligned with all comparative literature (TCNet, TENet, AutoKWS, SincConv, LConv/DyConv, etc.).
- **Average Accuracy (Avg. Accuracy)**: Defined in the footnote of Table II as the mean accuracy of clean and all noise conditions, using a single value to summarize the comprehensive performance of "clean retention + noise robustness."
- **Parameter Count and FLOPS** (Table I): Standard dual metrics for small-footprint KWS literature. The paper's introduction explicitly states that these two quantities are key elements in evaluating KWS performance, so all comparisons include these two columns to prove the "zero cost" claim.
- **Metrics Not Adopted**: The paper does not report false alarm rate/rejection rate (FA/FRR), AUC, and other KWS operating point metrics, nor does it report training overhead increments and actual device latency—these are left for limitation discussions.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Table II itself constitutes a complete loss combination ablation matrix ($L_M$, $L_I$, $L_O$ single items, $L_{IO}$, $L_{MI}$, $L_{MO}$ dual items, $L_{MIO}$ triple item, plus LDy baseline), with four findings:

1. **Filter-side supervision ($L_M$) is most effective for clean discriminability**: The paper concludes that "$L_M$ performs best in clean environments, indicating that metric learning on the output of the dynamic filter helps extract discriminative features for KWS" ($L_M$ clean 97.10 vs LDy 96.95). Strictly looking at the table, $L_I$ is also 97.12 under clean conditions, and $L_{MI}$ is also 97.12, with a difference of 0.02 percentage points from $L_M$—considering 8 repetitions but no variance reported, this difference at this level should be considered noise-level, and the reliable single-item ranking should look at the average of all conditions.
2. **The strength ranking of single losses is $L_I > L_M > L_O$**: Average of all conditions $L_I$ 92.19, $L_M$ 92.09, $L_O$ 91.88 (last row of Table II). Intra-class compactness is more important than orthogonality, and pure orthogonality constraint ($L_O$) is the weakest single item.
3. **$L_{IO}$ dual-loss combination is significantly robust under high noise, and the paper provides a mechanistic explanation**: $L_I$ manages intra-class variance, $L_O$ manages inter-class distance and orthogonality. **Applying $L_O$ alone cannot ensure that the inter-class distance margin is truly enhanced** (orthogonality only guarantees directions are uncorrelated, not centroid spacing), and applying $L_I$ alone does not manage inter-class separation. Only the combination of the two can simultaneously lock in the two geometric quantities of "inter-class openness + inter-class orthogonality," eliminating the uncertainty of a single loss (original text: "jointly utilizing the two-loss function ($L_{IO}$) would mitigate this uncertainty"). Data-wise, $L_{IO}$ is 80.57 at Urban 0dB and 78.29 at WHAM 0dB, both significantly leading any single item.
4. **Complete combination $L_{MIO}$ is comprehensively optimal at low SNR and has the highest average of all conditions**: The three 0dB items of DCASE/Urban/WHAM are all first (89.71/80.92/78.95), and the average of all conditions is 92.63, the highest in the field; the cost is that the clean condition is 0.10 lower than the single item $L_M$ (97.00 vs 97.10)—**there is a small trade-off between robustness and clean accuracy**, which corroborates the hyperparameter choice of $\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3$ being forced to 0.01: if geometric regularization is too strong, it will squeeze the learning space of CE.

Another pervasive "ablation" fact: The parameter count and FLOPS of LDyLM-TENet12 and LDyLMIO-TENet12 in Table I are exactly the same as LDy-TENet12 (102K/6.64M), numerically confirming the core promise of "training-time loss, zero inference cost."

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed LOVO combined loss**, concretizing the vague goal of "noise robust features" into three geometric quantities: intra-class variance, inter-class variance, and inter-class orthogonality, and implementing all three as training-time losses $L_M + L_I + L_O$, with zero increment in parameter count and FLOPS on the inference side (Table I: 102K/6.64M identical digit by digit to previous work).
2. **Proposed a two-way decoupled metric learning framework**: Pairwise triplet loss on the dynamic filter side directly optimizes front-end feature separability, and class-center-level loss on the classifier side optimizes end discriminative geometry. Gradients are applied close to the responsibility, and the auxiliary head is discarded after training.
3. **Avoided dominant class bias in pairwise metric learning [25] using class-center (non-pairwise) supervision**, achieving more robust performance relative to center loss, FaceNet, multi-similarity, circle loss, and DeCov under low SNR unseen noise (Table III total average 92.32 vs best competitor 92.22, WHAM 0dB complete method leads by 1.23 percentage points).
4. **Designed spectral norm-driven joint orthogonal-distance regularization** (Eq. 7): Matrix filter zeros diagonal, exponentially amplitude-limited distance term, spectral norm plus 10 power iterations, fusing two competing geometric objectives into a single stable minimization object.
5. **Empirical gains**: Relative to previous work LDy-TENet12 under unseen noise 0dB conditions, +3.03 percentage points on WHAM (the 3% claimed in the abstract is this), +2.37 on Urban, +1.30 on DCASE; average of all conditions +0.97 (Table II).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Hyperparameters are purely manually tuned with no sensitivity analysis**: $\alpha=1$, $\lambda_1=0.25$, $\lambda_2=\lambda_3=0.01$ are only given as empirical values. The fact that $\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3$ are pressed to 0.01 itself indicates that the balance between geometric terms and CE is quite fragile—what happens if the coefficient is amplified by an order of magnitude is not reported by the paper, and reproducers can only tune blindly.
- **Training overhead is not quantified**: Spectral norm requires 10 power iterations per step, and the dynamic embedding model is an additional two layers of convolution forward pass; the paper does not provide any quantitative numbers for these training-time costs (training time, VRAM increment are both "not reported by the paper").
- **Eq. 2 has a discrepancy between name and reality, and symbols are undefined**: Called triplet loss [21] but the form is a signed distance with margin for all sample pairs (closer to contrastive loss), the meaning of the asterisk on $y^*$ is not explained, and the notation for the upper limit of the inner summation in the formula also relies on context inference, directly affecting reproducibility.
- **Inconsistent naming**: The expansion of LOVO is inconsistent between the abstract (LOw Variant Orthogonal) and Section II (LOw Variability Orthogonal), which is a writing flaw.
- **Class centers are estimated within small batches, and variance issues are not discussed**: Batch size 100, 12 classes, averaging about 8 samples per class; class centers are estimates from very small samples; the impact of batch size on class center quality, and thus on the effect of $L_I$/$L_O$, is not touched by the paper (comparing center loss original text uses a class center memory bank updated across batches).
- **Single noise model**: Only additive noise is considered, not covering composite degradations common in real deployments such as reverberation, far-field channels, and overlapping multi-speaker speech.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **8 repeated experiments but no variance reported**: Multiple key gaps are in the magnitude of 0.02–0.10 percentage points (e.g., clean condition $L_{MIO}$ 97.00 vs $L_M$ 97.10); without standard deviation and significance tests, the statistical credibility of these conclusions is questionable.
- **Missing KWS operating point metrics**: False alarm rate, rejection rate, ROC/AUC, or detection rate at fixed false alarm are not reported, while real wake-up products indeed determine life and death on such metrics; accuracy cannot extrapolate product behavior.
- **Missing embedding space visualization**: The only figure in the entire paper is the pipeline diagram (Fig. 1); there is no t-SNE/UMAP to show whether "class center orthogonality, intra-class compactness" really occurs; the geometric assumption has only end-to-end accuracy as indirect evidence.
- **Narrow noise domain selection and no domain relationship analysis**: The three unseen noise libraries (DCASE/Urban8K/WHAM) are all urban domains, and the domain distance from the mike noise injected during training is not analyzed, making it impossible to distinguish whether the gain comes from "geometric regularization" or "just happens to be domain-close."
- **Old dataset version**: Speech Commands v2 has long been released and is larger in scale; still using v1 (30 words, about 1-second short words) for evaluation, the extrapolability of conclusions to larger vocabularies/longer keywords is unknown.
- **No device-level validation**: The small-footprint claim stops at parameter count and FLOPS; latency and power consumption were not measured on real MCUs/DSPs; nor was it compared on the noise table (Table II) with robust training routes such as speech enhancement front-ends and adversarial training (this table only has model structure baselines).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Multi-objective automatic weighting**: Replace manual $\lambda$ with methods such as uncertainty weighting or GradNorm (the paper has cited the multi-objective optimization perspective in [28] but did not use it), allowing the balance between geometric terms and CE to adjust dynamically during training, potentially preserving both clean accuracy and robustness, and alleviating the 0.10 percentage point cost on the clean end.
- **Cross-batch class center memory bank**: Drawing on the running class center mechanism of center loss to reduce the variance of small batch class center estimation, expected to be beneficial for the stability of $L_I$/$L_O$.
- **Expand evaluation dimensions**: Speech Commands v2, composite degradations of reverberation plus noise, multi-speaker, real far-field recordings, as well as FA/FRR operating points and embedding visualization, to complete the geometric story into a complete evidence chain.
- **Overlay with model compression**: LOVO is a pure training-time method, orthogonal to quantization, pruning, and distillation; verifying whether orthogonal geometric constraints are maintained after low-bit quantization is a necessary step to push this method to real edge-side deployment.
- **Cross-task transfer**: Class center orthogonality constraints may be applicable to any small class-number edge-side classification tasks (acoustic event detection, speaker verification front-end), and its universality is worth verifying.
