# Improving Label-Deficient Keyword Spotting Through Self-Supervised Pretraining

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Holger Severin Bovbjerg, Zheng-Hua Tan (Department of Electronic Systems, Aalborg University, Denmark; Second author also affiliated with the Pioneer Centre for AI, Denmark)
- **Date**: October 2022 (arXiv:2210.01703, v3 updated in May 2023; formally published at ICASSP 2023 SASB Workshop, June 10, 2023, Rhodes, Greece)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01703
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, self-supervised learning, Data2Vec, label-deficient, Keyword Transformer, Speech Commands, pretraining and fine-tuning

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the "gatekeeper" for intelligent voice assistants. The paper opens by pointing out (Section 1) that the common architecture for personal assistants like Google Assistant and Apple Siri involves a small KWS system running persistently to handle wake-up commands, only launching the full large-vocabulary Continuous Speech Recognition (ASR) system after activation, with the goal of saving resources (citation [1]). This architecture imposes two rigid constraints on KWS models: first, it must listen continuously year-round, so the model must be small and power-efficient; second, it faces end-users directly, so false wake-ups and missed wake-ups directly harm product experience, meaning accuracy requirements do not decrease simply because the model is small.

While deep learning has significantly improved KWS accuracy, the cost is a strong dependence on labeled data. The paper emphasizes that training a well-generalized deep KWS model requires a large amount of labeled speech, and collecting and annotating speech is a labor-intensive, high-cost process, which is particularly fatal for low-resource languages (Section 1). There is a background logic worth expanding here: the annotation for general ASR can reduce costs through semi-automatic methods (using existing models for rough transcription followed by manual proofreading), but KWS annotation requires precise determination of "the position, boundary, and presence/absence of each wake-word occurrence," and negative samples (non-wake-words, silence, noise) must also be sufficiently covered, making manual costs unavoidable. Furthermore, commercial wake-words are custom vocabularies—changing a product name requires re-collecting a batch of data, meaning annotation costs recur repeatedly rather than being a one-time investment.

Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) appears to be the cure: it learns representations from unlabeled data, requiring only a small amount of labeled data for fine-tuning downstream. However, the paper keenly points out a mismatch between current speech SSL research and the actual needs of KWS (Section 1): current research focuses on "general speech models," trained on large corpora like Librispeech and LibriLight, and evaluated on the SUPERB benchmark, requiring multiple high-end GPUs for weeks of training. This paradigm is doubly infeasible for the KWS scenario—in terms of compute, most teams do not have the budget of several weeks of GPU time for a wake-word model; in terms of product, KWS specifically requires small and efficient models, which contradicts the direction of "fine-tuning large models." In other words, the dividends of SSL have primarily been realized by large models, while the small KWS models that most need to save on labeled data have not benefited.

This is the gap the paper addresses: systematic empirical evidence at the intersection of small models + label scarcity + KWS was largely missing prior to this work.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

Section 1 of the paper breaks down the shortcomings of existing technical routes into three points, each pointing to a specific technical dead end:

- **Compute and Scale Thresholds of General Large Model SSL**. Methods like wav2vec 2.0 (citation [2]) and WavLM (citation [3]) have indeed demonstrated the power of SSL representations, but their training involves hundreds of millions of parameters, requiring multiple high-end GPUs and weeks of time. For terminal resident tasks like KWS, even if they can be pruned after fine-tuning, the cost of the pretraining phase itself excludes most application developers. Moreover, the design goal of these models is "one model for multiple downstream tasks" (the SUPERB evaluation paradigm), which does not match the KWS requirement for a single task and extreme lightness.
- **Knowledge Distillation Does Not Eliminate Dependence on Large Models**. Works such as citations [8][9][10] (simple distillation baselines, SEED, etc.) have studied how to transfer representations from large models to small models, but the premise of distillation is the prior existence of a trained large teacher model. That is, it merely shifts the compute cost from "every training run" to "the first training run," delaying the problem rather than solving it. For KWS application developers, "train a large model first, then distill" remains an unviable path.
- **Historical Doubts About Whether Small Models Can Handle Self-Supervised Proxy Tasks**. The academic community previously assumed that small models lacked sufficient capacity, making self-supervised pretraining prone to overfitting proxy tasks and failing to learn useful representations. The paper cites an image domain study (citation [11]) as counter-evidence: this study trained small models directly with contrastive SSL (without distillation) and found that small models could also solve self-supervised proxy tasks without overfitting, improving five image recognition models with 2.5M to 11M parameters. This evidence suggests that "SSL for small models" is feasible in principle, but it is in the image domain and uses contrastive methods; SSL for small models in the speech domain using non-contrastive methods remains unanswered. Another work (citation [12], Audio ALBERT) found redundancy in the parameters of various layers of large speech models and compressed the network using weight sharing, but the starting point was still "compressing large models" rather than "directly training small models."

Additionally, one can add a layer of implicit shortcoming not explicitly stated by the paper: the SOTA models on the KWS academic benchmark (Google Speech Commands), such as the KWT used in this paper (citation [14]), are all trained on full labeled data. Their results cannot answer "what to do when only 20% of the labels are available"—which is the norm for deploying custom wake-words in the industry.

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

The core question the paper aims to answer can be precisely stated as: Without introducing any large models, without using multi-GPU clusters, and with model parameters not exceeding 5.4M, can self-supervised pretraining alleviate the scarcity of labeled data for KWS, to what extent, and under what conditions (must pretraining data be in-domain? must all weights be updated during fine-tuning?). The four specific sub-questions correspond to the four findings provided by the paper: Can small models benefit from SSL? Can pretraining on 80% unlabeled data followed by fine-tuning on 20% labeled data catch up to training on 100% labeled data? Is cross-domain pretraining data equally effective? Is it feasible to freeze pretraining weights and only train the classification head? These four questions are interlinked, forming a complete closed-loop argument for the feasibility of "SSL for small model KWS."

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system follows the classic three-stage structure of deep KWS (Figure 1 in the paper): feature extraction, DNN acoustic model, and posterior processing. This division is itself a continuation of engineering tradition—the feature and posterior ends can be independently optimized using signal processing techniques, while the deep model only handles the intermediate acoustic modeling.

**The motivation for choosing the base model KWT (Keyword Transformer, citation [14]) is driven by framework constraints**. Data2Vec's encoder is a transformer, so the acoustic model must be a transformer architecture to directly apply the pretraining framework; and KWT happened to be the SOTA transformer model on the Google Speech Commands benchmark at that time. KWT's design originates from the vision transformer (ViT, citation [16]), replacing the input from images to Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC), effectively replacing "image patch sequences" with "speech frame sequences." The two are isomorphic at the abstract level of "token sequences entering the transformer encoder." This choice reflects a typical engineering compromise: rather than reinventing an architecture for the KWS task, it selects a verified SOTA architecture to host the SSL experiment, keeping the variable controlled to "with or without pretraining."

**Front-end Feature Configuration** (Section 2.1): MFCC uses a 480-sample window length, 160-sample frame shift, and 40-dimensional features. Converted at a 16kHz sampling rate, this is a 30ms window and 10ms frame shift, which is a standard configuration for speech recognition, with nothing special about it—this itself reflects the paper's stance: SSL benefits do not rely on fancy front-ends. Each MFCC vector then passes through a linear layer, projected into an embedding space consistent with the transformer input dimension, corresponding to the patch embedding in ViT.

**Acoustic Model Body**: 12 transformer blocks, followed by a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) classification head, outputting posteriors for Nc keyword classes (in this experiment Nc=35). The original KWT concatenates a CLS classification token at the input end, taking its final encoding as a global representation fed into the classification head. This paper makes a small but key modification: **do not use the CLS token, but instead use the mean of the encodings at each time step as the input to the classification head**. The paper explicitly reports that this modification "achieves better performance in both cases with and without self-supervised pretraining" (Section 2.1). Why is mean pooling superior for speech? A reasonable explanation is: in ViT, there is no natural reading order between image patches, requiring a dedicated token for aggregation; whereas speech is a short-time stationary time-series signal, and the keyword may appear at any position in 1 second of speech. Mean pooling is insensitive to position, and the encoding of every time step receives gradient signals from the classification head, providing denser training signals, which naturally fits the "global judgment" task of 1-second short words. The CLS token, on the other hand, receives supervision at only one position, making it harder to train well for small models with small data.

**Model Specifications** (Section 3): The number of attention heads k ranges from 1 to 3, and the encoder dimension d ranges from 64 to 192, resulting in three tiers: KWT-1 (0.6M parameters), KWT-2 (2.4M), and KWT-3 (5.4M). Upon deep reading, an internal inconsistency in the text was found: Section 2.1 states that d satisfies kd=64, while Section 3 explicitly states k takes 1 to 3 and d takes 64 to 192. The combinations should be KWT-1 (k=1, d=64), KWT-2 (k=2, d=128), and KWT-3 (k=3, d=192), meaning the actual constraint is more like d=64k, and the expression in Section 2.1 is suspected to be a typo. The setting of these three scales serves the core question of the experimental design—the relationship between model size and SSL benefits. This span of scales (approximately 9 times difference in parameters) is sufficient to observe scale effects.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

Data2Vec (citation [13]) is a **non-contrastive, self-distillation** type self-supervised framework. Its mathematical core can be broken down into three things (Section 2.2, Figure 3):

**First, the teacher and student are two copies of the same architecture, with teacher weights being the Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the student weights**. The student is updated via gradient descent, while the teacher is not trained directly but smoothly tracks the student's historical weights. This solves a classic bootstrapping paradox: if the prediction target is generated by the model being trained itself, it will drift or collapse during training (e.g., constantly outputting zero); the EMA teacher moves slower than the student, so the target has both the "quality from a stronger version" and relative stability.

**Second, the training objective is regression-based (minimizing difference), not contrastive**. The teacher encodes the full input, the student encodes the masked input, and the student learns to "predict the teacher's internal representation of the full input from incomplete information." Specifically, the target y_t that the student predicts f_t(x) should approximate is constructed from the last K layers of hidden states of the teacher model (Equation 1 in the paper):

$$y_t = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{l=L-K+1}^{L}\hat{h}_l^t$$

where L is the total number of transformer blocks (12 in this paper), $\hat{h}_l^t$ is the normalized hidden state of the l-th block at the t-th time step, and the sum only takes the top K layers. The loss uses Mean Squared Error (MSE, Section 3.2). Why take the average of the top K layers instead of a single layer? The design consideration of the original Data2Vec is: higher-level hidden states encode more abstract semantic information, while a single-layer target might cause the model to only imitate the representation preferences of one layer; multi-layer averaging provides a smoother, more information-rich regression target. The paper does not report the specific value of K used in this paper's experiments (following the original literature setting).

**Third, the masking strategy borrows from the time-domain masking of wav2vec (citation [19])** (Section 3.2): Starting frames are sampled with probability pmask, followed by Nmask=10 consecutive MFCC vectors being replaced with a learnable MASK embedding, masking approximately 65% of time steps overall; each input sample generates an independent mask. The student sees the masked sequence, while the teacher sees the full sequence. The masking rate of 65% is much higher than BERT-like text masking (15%) because speech frames have high information redundancy (adjacent frames are highly correlated); with a low masking rate, the task is too easy, and the student is not forced to learn deep structures.

**Connection with Supervised Training**: During pretraining, the classification head in Figure 2 is replaced with a linear regression head to predict teacher hidden states; after pretraining is complete, the regression head is swapped back for the classification head, and the entire model is fine-tuned on 20% labeled data (Section 3.2). This "head swapping" operation indicates that the pretraining learns representations internal to the encoder, decoupled from downstream classification.

**Training Recipe** (Sections 3.1, 3.2): The supervised baseline uses cross-entropy loss, SpecAugment (random block masking in time and frequency dimensions, citation [17]), 140 epochs of training, batch size 512, AdamW optimizer (learning rate 1e-3, weight decay 0.1), 10 epochs of linear warmup plus cosine annealing. Pretraining uses MSE loss, Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.5e-3, weight decay 0.01), 1-cycle learning rate schedule, batch size 512, EMA decay annealing steps 1000, trained for 200 epochs. Most hyperparameters of the two recipes are directly taken from the original Data2Vec, with the paper noting minor adjustments due to data and hardware differences. Notably, the supervised baseline also used SpecAugment—this ensures a fair comparison, so that SSL gains cannot be attributed to differences in data augmentation.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Fully Implementing Non-Contrastive SSL in Sub-Million Parameter KWS Models

The paper's most core contribution is not inventing a new algorithm, but completing a rigorous verification of paradigm feasibility: Data2Vec was previously validated on speech with large models of hundreds of millions of parameters; this paper moves it to small models of 0.6M to 5.4M parameters and systematically proves its effectiveness. The technical implication of this is greater than it appears—the capacity bottleneck of small models might make the high-dimensional regression task of "predicting teacher hidden states" unsolvable or meaningless, but experiments prove that small models can also digest this target. The paper also emphasizes the feasibility in terms of cost (Section 3): all experiments were completed on a virtual machine with 10 CPU cores, 40GB memory, and a single NVIDIA T40 GPU (16GB VRAM). Pretraining for the largest model took about 10 hours, and fine-tuning about 1 hour. Compared to the threshold of large model SSL ("several high-end GPUs, weeks of training"), this is a magnitude-level cost reduction, meaning ordinary teams or even university laboratories can reproduce it. The paper has open-sourced all code (GitHub: HolgerBovbjerg/data2vec-KWS).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Classification Head Adaptation Using Mean Pooling Instead of CLS Token

As mentioned earlier, changing the original KWT's CLS token mechanism to mean pooling of all time-step encodings, and the paper reports that this modification brought improvements under both pretraining and non-pretraining conditions (Section 2.1, specific improvement values not reported). This small change is a step towards "truly speechifying the vision architecture" and also aligns the classification head with Data2Vec's frame-by-frame regression objective in the time dimension—the pretraining regression is supervised frame-by-frame, and the fine-tuning mean pooling also lets all time steps participate in decision-making, making the gradient flow consistent between the two.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Label-Scarce Experimental Protocol and Cross-Domain Pretraining Verification

The design of the experimental protocol (Section 2.3) is itself a methodological contribution to the paper: randomly removing 80% of the training set of Google Speech Commands V2 as the unlabeled pretraining set (18.8 hours), leaving only 20% (4.7 hours) as the labeled training set. The key to this split is that **the total amount of data remains unchanged**—it precisely simulates the real-world constraint of "lots of data but annotation budget only covers 20%," making the comparison between "SSL + few labels" and "full labels" comparable in total information quantity rather than gaining an advantage in data volume. Simultaneously, the Librispeech 100-hour clean set was used for cross-domain pretraining (read audiobooks vs. command words) to test whether pretraining data must be in-domain. This protocol can be directly reused by subsequent low-resource KWS research.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the large model SSL route (wav2vec 2.0, WavLM): This paper does not require a large model as the final product or training prerequisite; the model always stays within 5.4M parameters. The difference from contrastive SSL lies in the training signal—contrastive learning requires constructing negative sample pairs and relies on implicit negatives from large batches, while Data2Vec uses the EMA teacher's hidden states as a continuous regression target, requiring no negative samples, which is particularly important for small models because small models have limited capacity, and the representation space constraints of contrastive learning (pushing away large numbers of negatives) might excessively consume capacity. Compared to the knowledge distillation route (citations [8][9][10]): Distillation requires a large teacher first, while Data2Vec's "teacher" is the student's own EMA shadow, self-sufficient within the training loop, completely bypassing the dependence on large models—this is a self-response to the paper's introduction's criticism of the distillation route for "not solving the problem of training a large model first." Compared to the pioneers of small model SSL in the image domain (citation [11]): This paper moves the feasibility verification from images to speech, from contrastive to non-contrastive, and from classification tasks to KWS, which is more sensitive to time-series structures and noise. Compared to the original KWT (citation [14]): It retains the main architecture but modifies the classification head, and for the first time examines its behavior under label-scarce conditions.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

The main dataset is Google Speech Commands V2 (citation [15]): 105,829 recordings of 1 second, 35 keyword classes (Section 2.3). The label-scarce split is shown in Table 1: pretraining set 67,874 entries (used without labels), labeled training set 16,969 entries, validation set 9,981 entries, test set 11,005 entries. In terms of hours, the pretraining part is 18.8 hours, and the labeled part is 4.7 hours. The cross-domain pretraining data is the Librispeech 100-hour clean training set—the scale is about 5 times that of the in-domain pretraining data, but the domain difference is significant (read audiobooks vs. multi-speaker command word recordings). The paper does not report details of the specific word list and class distribution for the 35 classes (stating the dataset classes are balanced, citing the original literature).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric used is only classification accuracy (Section 3.1). The rationale is explicitly given by the paper: Speech Commands V2 is quite balanced across different keywords, so accuracy will not be distorted by class imbalance. This choice has pros and cons: the pro is simplicity, reproducibility, and comparability with the original KWT paper; the con is that what is truly concerned in KWS deployment is the trade-off between false alarm rate and miss rate at operating points (DET curves, AUC, etc.), and a single accuracy cannot characterize this. As a workshop paper, this simplification is understandable, but it also means the results' guidance for actual deployment is discounted (see Limitations section).

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Main results are in Table 2 (all are test set accuracies; all models used only 20% labeled data for supervised training or fine-tuning; Baseline is pure supervised without pretraining, SC is Data2Vec pretraining using the Speech Commands pretraining set, LS is pretraining using the Librispeech 100-hour set):

| Model | Parameters | Baseline | SC Pretrain | LS Pretrain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KWT-1 | 600k | 0.8572 | 0.9394 | 0.9426 |
| KWT-2 | 2.4M | 0.8584 | 0.9507 | 0.9455 |
| KWT-3 | 5.4M | 0.8411 | 0.9527 | 0.9476 |

Three key readings:

**First, the absolute improvement from SSL is huge and holds across scales**. The absolute accuracy improvement of SC pretraining relative to the baseline ranges from 8.22% to 11.18% (Abstract and Section 4), calculated from Table 2: KWT-1 improved by 8.22 percentage points (0.9394-0.8572), KWT-2 improved by 9.23 percentage points, and KWT-3 improved by 11.16 percentage points. A fascinating detail: the main text states the best result for KWT-3 is 95.29% (Section 4), while the corresponding cell in Table 2 is 0.9527; the upper limit of 11.18% in the abstract is derived from 95.29 minus 84.11—there is an internal inconsistency of about 0.02 percentage points in the paper, which is a typesetting-level flaw and does not affect the conclusion.

**Second, the baseline results reveal the counter-intuitive phenomenon of "larger models perform worse" under small data**. In Table 2, without pretraining, the largest KWT-3 (84.11%) is actually lower than KWT-1 (85.72%) and KWT-2 (85.84%). A reasonable explanation is: 16,969 labeled samples are insufficient to constrain a 5.4M parameter model, leading to overfitting; but after pretraining, the advantage of model scale is restored (KWT-3 is best after pretraining, 95.27%). This indirectly proves that SSL pretraining plays a dual role of regularization and initialization—it turns "model capacity" from a liability back into an asset.

**Third, cross-domain pretraining loses almost no benefit**. The accuracy difference between LS and SC pretraining is only between 0.32% and 0.52% (Section 4, verified from Table 2: on KWT-1, LS is actually 0.32% higher; on KWT-2 and KWT-3, SC is 0.52% and 0.51% higher, respectively). The engineering value of this conclusion is extremely high: it indicates that pretraining data does not need to be specifically collected for the command word domain; public reading material can be used—low-resource languages can directly use existing audiobooks, podcasts, and other unlabeled audio in that language for pretraining, bypassing the dead loop of "needing command word data first to save command word labels."

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Ablation experiments (Section 4.1, Table 3) were conducted in two dimensions, with very hard conclusions:

**Freezing feature extraction fails completely**. Freezing the weights of the SC pretraining model and only training the classification head ("SC-FE" row): KWT-1 is only 0.4292, KWT-2 is only 0.4974, KWT-3 is only 0.4960—not only far lower than the fine-tuning results, but also about 35 to 42 percentage points lower than the randomly initialized supervised baseline (0.84+). The paper's interpretation is: the representations learned by pretraining are insufficient to serve directly as input features for a linear classifier; all weights must be updated (Introduction, Finding 4). Why can't small model SSL representations be linearly probed? The deep reason is that Data2Vec's training objective is the continuous regression of "predicting teacher hidden states," which shapes representations as intermediate quantities for reconstruction, not guaranteeing class linear separability; large models (e.g., wav2vec 2.0 Large) have parameter redundancy and highly over-complete representations, so freezing them and attaching a lightweight head can work, while small models of 0.6M to 5.4M have every dimension of parameters fully pressed by the task, and representations must be reshaped through task-specific fine-tuning to be used for discrimination. This result defines the applicable boundary of this paper's method: it is a paradigm of "pretraining plus full model fine-tuning," not providing a plug-and-play feature extractor.

**20% labeled fine-tuning catches up to or even exceeds 100% labeled training**. The "Full" column in Table 3 is 100% labeled pure supervised training: KWT-1 is 0.9638, KWT-2 is 0.9498, KWT-3 is 0.9079. Comparing with the SC pretraining plus 20% fine-tuning results in Table 2, the paper explicitly states that full-labeled training "does not outperform" pretraining plus 20% labeled fine-tuning, with the latter being on par or slightly better (Section 4.1; calculated from the two tables, on KWT-2, pretraining fine-tuning is 0.09 percentage points higher, and on KWT-3, it is 4.48 percentage points higher). The fact that KWT-3 with full labeled data only reaches 90.79% is particularly telling: 5 times the labeled data did not cure the large model's overfitting, but pretraining did. This is the most convincing set of comparisons in the paper—it upgrades the value of SSL from "improving by several percentage points" to "replacing 80% of the annotation budget."

**Full labeled data plus pretraining still yields benefits**. The last column of Table 3 "Full + LS pretrain" (Librispeech pretraining plus full labeled fine-tuning): the three models reached 0.9713, 0.9716, 0.9716, all breaking 97%, and the increase for KWT-2 and KWT-3 relative to no-pretraining full training was the largest (Section 4.1). This shows that pretraining and more labeled data are not an upper limit of substitution—when data is sufficient, pretraining still adds value, and it almost flattens the three scales of models (difference only 0.03 percentage points), meaning model capacity is no longer a bottleneck under sufficient supervision and good initialization.

**Cost Comparison** (Section 3): On a single T40 GPU, pretraining for the largest model takes about 10 hours, and fine-tuning about 1 hour. Compared to the large model SSL route of "multiple high-end GPUs, weeks of training," or the two-stage cost of "train large model first, then distill," this paper's route demands resources at a lower magnitude, while delivering the effects described above.

## Main Contributions

1. **First systematic proof that non-contrastive self-supervised pretraining is effective for small KWS models** (Abstract, Section 5): Three KWT variants with 0.6M to 5.4M parameters all achieved absolute accuracy improvements of 8.22% to 11.18% under label-scarce conditions (Table 2), overturning the implicit assumption that "SSL only belongs to large models" in the KWS domain.
2. **Quantified SSL's ability to substitute annotation budgets**: 80% unlabeled pretraining plus 20% labeled fine-tuning, performance catches up to or even exceeds 100% labeled pure supervised training (Table 2 vs. Table 3 "Full" column), meaning four-fifths of the annotation workload can be offset by unlabeled data.
3. **Proved domain independence of pretraining data**: The difference in pretraining effects between Librispeech audiobooks and Speech Commands command words is only 0.32% to 0.52% (Table 2), meaning low-resource languages can borrow any unlabeled audio in the same language.
4. **Defined method boundaries**: Freezing weights for feature extraction failed miserably (Table 3 "SC-FE", 0.43 to 0.50), indicating that small model SSL representations must be paired with full-parameter fine-tuning and cannot be used as generic feature extractors.
5. **Methodological byproducts**: A reusable label-scarce KWS experimental protocol (80/20 split plus cross-domain pretraining control) and open-source implementation (GitHub), as well as the classification head improvement of mean pooling replacing the CLS token (Section 2.1).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Representations are not linearly probeable**. The results of Table 3 "SC-FE" (0.4292 to 0.4960) indicate that pretraining representations are incompatible with the "freeze and use" deployment form. For product scenarios that need to quickly switch multiple wake-words on a single base, each new word requires full model fine-tuning, limiting the reuse value of pretraining to the initialization level.
- **Does not touch upon streaming and posterior processing**. Figure 1 in the paper draws the "posterior processing" module, but the main text does not expand on any details (smoothing windows, decision thresholds, causal constraints for streaming inference are all not reported by the paper). KWT's global self-attention performs attention on the entire 1-second input, naturally non-streaming, and directly moving it to a resident wake-up scenario still requires architecture-level work; the paper does not discuss this deployment gap.
- **No efficiency metrics reported**. Parameters are listed (Table 2), but inference latency, FLOPS, memory usage, and real-time rate on target embedded hardware are all not reported by the paper. The selling point of "small model" currently stands only on parameter count; engineering smallness also depends on operator overhead—the transformer's attention operator may not be friendlier to microcontrollers than convolution.
- **Fine-tuning requires full parameter updates**. Related to the previous point, full model fine-tuning means that pretraining benefits are temporarily incompatible with "lightweight adaptation" (adapter, linear probe, etc.) routes, and portability in future on-device continuous learning scenarios is questionable.
- **Hyperparameters inherited from large model recipes**. Pretraining hyperparameters are mostly copied from the original Data2Vec (Section 3.2), with minor adjustments due to data and hardware differences; whether small models require specifically tuned masking rates (65% was set for large models), EMA decay, and target layer count K (paper does not report the value), this paper did not perform this layer of ablation, potentially leaving free performance space.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Single run, no variance reported**. All results do not report standard deviations or confidence intervals under multiple random seeds, and the paper does not report any significance tests. The main effect of 8 to 11 percentage points is far beyond typical seed fluctuations, so the main conclusion is likely stable, but the difference of 0.32% to 0.52% between SC and LS, and the slight advantage of 0.09 percentage points, likely fall within the range of random fluctuations, and the conclusion should be stated more cautiously.
- **Single dataset, single condition**. All experiments are based on the 35-word closed-set classification of Google Speech Commands V2, 1-second fixed-length slices, near-field clean recordings. There is no verification under noise, reverberation, or far-field conditions (the paper itself lists noise robustness as future work in the conclusion), while real wake-word scenarios are dominated by far-field plus noise.
- **Metrics are too simple**. Only balanced accuracy is used; false alarm rate and miss rate operating points commonly used in KWS deployment (e.g., recall at specific false positive rates), DET curves, or AUC are not reported, making it impossible to answer "how much is an 8-point accuracy increase worth at the deployment operating point."
- **Lack of horizontal comparison methods**. There is no comparison with any other self-supervised methods (e.g., contrastive wav2vec 2.0 small versions) or knowledge distillation baselines under the same protocol, and the implicit proposition that "Data2Vec is the optimal choice for small model SSL" was not tested; the cited small model SSL in the image domain (citation [11]) was also not reproduced and compared in the speech domain.
- **Singularity of pretraining data scale**. In-domain pretraining only tested one tier of 18.8 hours, without scanning the benefit curve of pretraining data volume from 1 hour to 100 hours, making it impossible to answer the practical question of "how to best allocate the ratio between annotation budget and unlabeled data volume."

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Directions pointed out by the paper itself (Section 5): First, investigate the impact of self-supervised pretraining on the noise robustness of KWS models, pushing verification from clean command word recordings to real acoustic environments; second, deeply understand the capability boundaries of self-supervised pretraining on small models. Combining with deep reading, several directions can be reasonably extended: scan the "unlabeled data for labeled data" exchange curve across multiple pretraining data scales and multiple annotation ratios, providing a quantitative basis for annotation budget decisions; specifically search for the sweet spot of masking rate, target layer count, and EMA decay for small models, rather than following large model recipes; introduce parameter-efficient fine-tuning such as adapters or partial layer freezing to test whether full model fine-tuning can be avoided while retaining most benefits; orthogonally combine with model compression (quantization, pruning) to verify the tolerance of SSL-initialized models to compression, opening up the complete on-device link of "pretraining saves labels, compression saves inference"; promote the protocol to wake-word tasks in truly low-resource languages to test the extrapolation limits of cross-domain conclusions; supplement whether pretraining benefits remain after streaming transformation (e.g., switching to causal attention or downsampling front-ends). Overall, this short workshop paper answers a high-value question with the minimum experimental complexity; its value lies mainly in establishing feasibility and drawing boundaries, rather than providing a ultimate solution—the space it leaves for subsequent work is as clear as the conclusions it contributes itself.
