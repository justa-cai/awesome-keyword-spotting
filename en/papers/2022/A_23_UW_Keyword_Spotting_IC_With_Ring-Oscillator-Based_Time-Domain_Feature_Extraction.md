## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

This paper does not address the question of "how to design the algorithm," but rather "how to build the hardware backbone of the wake-up chain." Audio IoT devices (remote controls, wireless earbuds, smart tags) require always-on keyword spotting (KWS), which imposes four simultaneous constraints on the entire system: always-on operation, real-time response, miniature packaging, and button-cell-level battery life. The quantitative goal stated at the beginning of the paper is a system-level power consumption of less than 100 μW—note that this budget includes not only the KWS chip itself but also the microphone and other system components. This number directly dictates the subsequent design choices: why a single-ended microphone is used, why the feature extractor is implemented in the time domain, and why the classifier is limited to a two-layer GRU.

The latency constraint also has a clear origin: research on spontaneous speech perception indicates that a delay exceeding 20 ms becomes annoying to users (Reference [1]). Therefore, a KWS system must process sound from the microphone to the classification output within 20 ms; otherwise, it would degrade the user experience in interactive scenarios. This constraint eliminates a batch of ultra-low-power but high-latency solutions and serves as a key filter in the comparison tables later in the paper.

Figure 1 illustrates the standard processing chain: Microphone → Feature Extractor (FEx) generates frequency-selective feature vectors (FV) → Deep Neural Network (DNN) classifier outputs probabilities for each keyword. Where is the pain point? The paper cites measured data from a 12-class KWS chip (Reference [2]): in the complete processing chain from the analog-to-digital converter (ADC) to the DNN classifier, the FEx is the most power-hungry stage, accounting for 40% of the total chip power. In other words, the classification network itself is not the main power consumer; the analog front-end is. This 40% figure is the first motivational number in the paper—if you want to save power, start by saving the FEx.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing approaches into three types and points out their fatal flaws one by one. This梳理 itself is worth careful reading:

**Latency and Peripheral Costs of Digital FEx Routes.** Reference [6] (Shan et al., JSSC 2021) achieved an extreme power consumption of 510 nW using a digital FEx that computes MFCCs via serial FFT combined with a binarized depthwise separable CNN. However, the cost was triple: the latency was limited to 64 ms (far exceeding the 20 ms perception threshold); it required an off-chip 16-bit ADC (high-resolution ADCs are inherently power- and cost-intensive); and it had only 2 KB of binarized CNN storage, reporting accuracy for only 5 keywords, with no handling of the "Unknown" class. References [38][39] similarly rely on off-chip ADCs, with [39] placing both the digital FEx and ADC off-chip. The fundamental dilemma of the digital route is that audio signals must undergo at least one analog-to-digital conversion before entering the digital domain, and high-resolution, high-linearity ADCs are precisely the power-hungry components that cannot be easily eliminated.

**Information Loss in Sequential Scanning Analog FEx.** Reference [3] (Oh et al., JSSC 2019) features a 142 nW VAD chip that uses sequential mixers to scan frequencies, similar in principle to bio-impedance sensors. However, sequential scanning is severely undersampled for KWS—only one frequency is observed at any given moment, leading to a 512 ms latency. While the latency itself might be tolerable, the true cost is that frequency-selective information is torn apart in time, which is fatal for keyword classification requiring fine spectral textures.

**Process Scaling Deadlock of Voltage-Domain Analog FEx.** References [7][8][9] achieved power consumptions of 205 nW to 1 μW and latencies of 10 to 100 ms using voltage-domain analog FEx (multi-channel bandpass filters + half-wave/full-wave rectifiers + low-speed ADCs). On the surface, this appears to be the optimal balance between power and latency. However, the paper points out a deeper issue: voltage-domain designs are not friendly to CMOS process scaling. The reason is that the supply voltage $V_{DD}$ scales faster than the threshold voltage $V_{TH}$, causing the headroom for voltage-domain signals to continuously shrink. This shrinkage directly compresses the dynamic range (DR), which is crucial for maintaining KWS accuracy across different volume levels. Meanwhile, the intrinsic gain of transistors ($g_m \cdot r_o$) is also degrading, leading to a decrease in the DC gain of analog feedback loops. These issues can be mitigated with longer channel lengths, gain boosting, and multi-stage amplification, but each solution comes at the cost of area, power, or bandwidth. In other words, the low-power advantage of voltage-domain analog FEx will be gradually eroded in advanced process nodes—a judgment looking ten years ahead, and the fundamental reason why this paper bets on the time domain.

**Gaps in Existing Oscillator-Based BPFs.** References [12][13] did propose oscillator-based bandpass filters for audio IoT, but the paper explicitly states that they did not provide a clear design strategy for time-domain rectifiers, nor did they demonstrate audio classification tasks using fabricated oscillator BPFs. That is, the "brick" of "oscillator-based filtering" exists, but the "entire house"—the complete time-domain chain from microphone interface to classification output—has not yet been built by anyone.

**System-Level Power and Linearity Contradiction of Single-Ended Microphones.** Commercial differential-output MEMS microphones typically consume more than 100 μW, while single-ended analog output microphones (such as the InvenSense ICS-40310) consume only about 10 μW. To survive within a 100 μW system budget, single-ended is almost a mandatory choice. However, single-ended signals cannot suppress even-order harmonics, putting them at a natural disadvantage in linearity; moreover, the linearity of voltage-controlled oscillators (VCOs) based on ring oscillators is inherently worse than that of traditional transconductance amplifiers (OTAs). To feed single-ended signals directly into a ring-oscillator-based voltage-to-time converter (VTC) without causing severe distortion requires a specialized solution.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the question this paper seeks to answer is: Can we build a KWS processing chain that operates entirely in the time domain, except for the initial voltage-to-time conversion—where feature extraction uses ring-oscillator-based time-domain circuits (bandpass filtering, rectification, pulse frequency encoding, and time-to-digital conversion all implemented using digital logic gate-style circuits)—such that it (1) is unaffected by voltage headroom shrinkage, is friendly to process scaling, and can operate at a low voltage of 0.5V; (2) supports single-ended microphones to reduce system-level power consumption; (3) has a sufficiently high dynamic range (measured 54.89 dB) to support 12-class GSCD accuracy; (4) keeps latency within the perception threshold (measured 12.4 ms); and (5) achieves an overall power consumption of only 23 μW, including the on-chip GRU classifier. These five sub-goals are mutually constraining—low voltage harms linearity, time-domain encoding introduces quantization noise, and small models harm accuracy. The value of this paper lies in resolving these contradictions one by one through circuit-level inventions.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

**Forward Flow: Software Modeling Followed by Hardware Implementation.** The methodology of this paper has a skeleton worth learning: first, model the entire KWS chain (behavioral model of analog FEx + backend classifier) in Python (Figure 2), using software to search for the impact of design parameters and quantization precision on accuracy, and then map the model block by block into circuits. The software model includes: a second-order Butterworth bandpass filter bank inspired by biological cochlea modeling, full-wave rectification (taking the absolute value), low-pass averaging, downsampler, and quantizer. Unlike previous analog FEx works [7][8], they added two levels of processing before the feature vectors entered the classifier: a logarithmic compression stage (imitating the adaptive gain compression mechanism of the biological cochlea) and an input normalization stage (standard practice for DNNs). Why these two levels are necessary will be seen in the ablation section later: the baseline model's accuracy jumped from 77.89% to 91.35% after adding these two levels, an improvement of over thirteen percentage points, making it the highest "algorithmic gain" in terms of cost-performance ratio in the entire paper.

**Signal Flow of Hardware Architecture (Figure 3).** The analog front-end consists of three stages: The first stage is a nested analog frequency-locked loop (FLL)-based VTC, which converts the single-ended microphone's voltage signal into multi-phase PWM output; the second stage is a 16-channel rectifying bandpass filter (Rec-BPF), where each channel is a second-order time-domain BPF with built-in full-wave rectification, outputting PWM; the third stage is a switch-ring oscillator (SRO)-based rate encoder, which converts PWM into PFM (Pulse Frequency Modulation) signals. The digital backend: The PFM signal first enters a digital differentiator ($1 - z^{-1}$); the signal path from the SRO to the differentiator constitutes a first-order ΔΣ time-to-digital converter (TDC), corresponding to the quantizer in the software model; followed by a first-order CIC decimation filter that performs averaging and downsampling, a programmable bias subtractor ($\beta$) to remove the SRO idle frequency component, a per-channel gain calibrator ($\alpha$) to correct inter-channel gain mismatches, a logarithmic look-up table (LUT), and an input normalizer. The mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ used for normalization come from the actual recorded logarithmic LUT outputs of the chip on the GSCD training set. Finally, every 16 ms, a 16-channel, signed 14-bit feature vector is generated and fed into a two-layer GRU plus a fully connected classification network, outputting the highest probability keyword among the 12 classes with a 12.4 ms latency.

**The "Why" of Key Parameters.** Each of these design parameters has a clear reason, not arbitrary: The 16-channel BPF follows the configuration of previous works [7][8][9] to ensure comparability; the BPF quality factor is set to 2, a compromise between frequency selectivity and inter-channel information coverage; the center frequencies are distributed from 100 Hz to 8 kHz according to the Mel scale, with 8 kHz being the bandwidth of the analog front-end in Reference [2]; the input speech is oversampled by a factor of two (from 16 kHz to 32 kHz) for a specific reason—if the sampling rate remains at 16 kHz, the 8 kHz center frequency would fall exactly on the Nyquist frequency, causing aliasing; the quantizer is set to 12 bits (before logarithmic compression); the frame shift is 16 ms, consistent with References [2][6]; the logarithmic compressor output is 10 bits; the normalized feature vector is 14 bits, entering a two-layer 48-hidden-unit GRU plus a fully connected layer; activation is quantized to 14 bits (6 integer bits + 8 fractional bits), and weights are quantized to 8 bits. The paper also confesses a detail: the baseline accuracy in Figure 2 would be higher if floating-point activation were used, because 14-bit quantization cannot cover the dynamic range of the 12-bit unsigned quantizer output—the match between quantization bit-width and feature dynamic range is a pitfall they encountered.

**Why Choose GRU for the Classifier.** The reason is that GRUs are frequently used in automatic speech recognition tasks [17], and their recurrent structure naturally matches variable-length audio frame sequences. The network size is strictly constrained within 24 KB of weight storage—this is the capacity upper limit derived from the power budget, which also foreshadows the gap in accuracy comparisons later.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Core Equation of FLL Linearized VCO.** The nested FLL forces the VCO's output frequency to lock to an expression determined solely by passive reference quantities (Equation 1):

$$f_{FLL} = \frac{V_{IN}}{15 R_{REF} C_{REF} V_{REF}}$$

Voltage-to-frequency gain (Equation 2):

$$K_{FLL\text{-}VCO} = \frac{\partial f_{FLL}}{\partial V_{IN}} = \frac{1}{15 R_{REF} C_{REF} V_{REF}}$$

The logic of linearization is: $R_{REF}$, $C_{REF}$, and $V_{REF}$ are quantities independent of the input signal amplitude, so the output frequency is strictly proportional to the input voltage, bypassing the non-linear voltage-control curve of a bare VCO. The 3 dB bandwidth of the VTC (Equation 3) is $f_{3dB,VTC} = \frac{1}{2\pi} K_{FLL\text{-}VCO} K_{PD} \beta_{DAC}$, where the feedback coefficient $\beta_{DAC} = \frac{15 C_{DAC}}{C_{IN} + 15 C_{DAC}} \cdot V_{DD}$ (Equation 4). The design values take a 3 dB bandwidth of 17 kHz and a nested FLL loop gain-bandwidth product of 158 kHz (as the non-dominant pole of the entire negative feedback loop). The 17 kHz bandwidth just covers the 8 kHz audio bandwidth with margin, and the 158 kHz loop bandwidth ensures that the FLL "bites" the input fast enough within the audio band.

**Phase-Domain Representation of SRO as an Ideal Integrator.** The concept in Figure 8 is the mathematical cornerstone of the paper: The SRO switches its operating frequency between $f_{Low}$ and $f_{High}$ based on the duty cycle of the input PWM, and its average frequency is proportional to the input duty cycle; mathematically, the phase of an oscillator is precisely the integral of frequency. Therefore, the transfer function from input phase to output phase is $K_{SRO}/s$—an ideal lossless integrator. There are two layers of key insights: First, this integration has no boundaries; as long as the SRO is oscillating, it can integrate indefinitely, unlike voltage-domain integrators which saturate due to headroom limits; Second, it exhibits infinite DC gain and is independent of transistor intrinsic gain and supply voltage. These two properties are exactly what voltage-domain analog circuits lose in advanced processes, but which ring oscillators give for free.

**Transfer Function of Time-Domain BPF.** Using the SRO as a lossless integrator ($\omega_0/s$) and applying the Tow-Thomas biquad topology (Figure 9), where the inner loop sets the Q factor and the outer loop creates the high-pass shape, yields the second-order BPF transfer function (Equation 5):

$$H_{BPF}(s) = \frac{s K_{IN} f_{FLL} K_{PFD} / N}{s^2 + s (K_1 f_{FLL} K_{PFD} / N) + K_1 K_2 f_{FLL}^2 K_{PFD} / N^2}$$

Center frequency and quality factor (Equation 6):

$$\omega_0 = f_{FLL} K_{PFD} \sqrt{K_1 K_2 / N}, \qquad Q = \sqrt{K_2 / K_1}$$

The beauty of this form lies in its decomposability: The center frequency is jointly determined by the FLL locked frequency and the switch gain, while the Q factor depends only on the ratio of the two switch gains—i.e., only on the size ratio of the switch transistors. Thus, the requirement of "different center frequencies per channel, unified Q=2" is decomposed into two independent design knobs: "FLL bias sets frequency + size ratio sets Q."

**Noise Shaping of ΔΣ TDC.** The path from the SRO to the digital differentiator ($1 - z^{-1}$) constitutes a TDC with a first-order ΔΣ modulator structure: The pulse density encoding of the PFM signal encodes the envelope information, the differentiator differentiates out the SRO idle frequency (DC component), and the quantization noise is shaped to high frequencies by the first-order modulator and then filtered out by the CIC decimation filter. The measured output spectrum (Figure 17c) shows a clear first-order noise shaping slope of 20 dB/dec, confirming this path. The signal-to-noise ratio definition used in the noise robustness experiment (Equation 9) is: $SNR = 10 \log_{10}(P_{Avg,GSCD} / P_{Avg,Noise})$, which is the ratio of the average power of the recorded feature vector to the power of the added Gaussian noise.

**Schreier Figure of Merit for Comparison.** To fairly compare with previous FExs with different frame shifts, the paper borrows the Schreier FoM from the ADC field (Equation 8): $FoM_{S,DR} = DR + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{P_{Norm} \cdot 2 \cdot FrameShift}\right)$, where the normalized power $P_{Norm}$ (Equation 7) scales the power by the number of channels and frequency span. The rationale is: After a bandpass signal is down-converted to baseband by a rectifier or mixer, the analog FEx can be viewed as a near-DC input ADC with a preprocessing stage. In this case, bandwidth is replaced by the inverse of the conversion time. Frame shift appears in the denominator because the feature vector is generated only once per frame shift—the longer the frame shift, the longer the single conversion time, the narrower the equivalent bandwidth, and the larger the noise integration window, so this term must be折算 into the metric.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Nested Analog FLL-Based VTC—Using Feedback to "Straighten" the VCO

This is the core invention to solve the single-ended input linearity problem. The design starts from an unfavorable fact: The VCO's voltage-control curve is inherently more curved than that of an OTA, and single-ended signals do not suppress even-order harmonics; the combination of these two disadvantages is severe. The solution is to wrap the ring VCO with a nested analog frequency-locked loop (Figure 5). The working principle follows current balance: The input current generator maps the input voltage to a reference resistor to generate a lower-side current $I_{IN} = V_{IN}/R_{REF}$ (Amplifier A2 design gain is 34 dB); the upper-side current $I_{SC} = 15 V_X C_{REF} f_{FLL}$ flows through 15-phase switched-capacitor operations—here, the structure of the multi-phase ring oscillator is fully utilized, and the 15-phase interleaved switching minimizes the voltage ripple at the $V_X$ node. The FLL feedback closes through the A1 amplifier (27 dB gain), sub-oscillator, and switched-capacitor circuit, forcing $V_X$ to equal $V_{REF}$ and $I_{IN}$ to equal $I_{SC}$, thereby clamping the output frequency to the expression in Equation 1, dependent only on passive reference quantities.

There are three noteworthy clever details in the circuit design. First, the pseudo-differential structure: A dual VCO plus phase detector, with one input port connected to the single-ended microphone and the other grounded. The 15-array phase detector receives 15-phase FM signals from the VCO and outputs 15-phase PWM representing the phase difference between the two VCOs. Because the VCO input node is a virtual ground, the generated multi-phase PWM becomes a time-domain approximation of the input voltage—the amplitude is encoded into the PWM duty cycle. Second, the multi-phase PWM scheme pushes spurious PWM tones to high frequencies without increasing the VCO operating frequency—this is a "free lunch" of "exchanging phase count for frequency space." Third, to allow the sub-oscillator to operate at 0.5V, a varactor-controlled power-temperature compensator was added: The size of the diode-connected P1 transistor is designed such that $V_{VAR}$ is proportional to absolute temperature (PTAT). When the temperature rises, $V_{VAR}$ increases, further turning on the MOS varactor, compensating for the trend of the ring oscillator frequency increasing with temperature; simultaneously, the N1-P1 path is used instead of simple gate voltage control, because $V_{GS,N1}$ is less sensitive to VDD than $V_{SG,P2}$. Simulation results (Figure 6) show that the worst-case power-temperature variation of the sub-oscillator is compressed by 19.98 times. Ultimately, the VTC guarantees less than −70 dB distortion for dominant (second and third) harmonics under single-ended input (Figure 7 transient simulation verified stability).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: SRO Dual-Integrator Loop Time-Domain Bandpass Filter

Each of the 16 channels uses four SROs and two phase-frequency detectors (PFDs) to build a second-order BPF (Figures 10, 11). Why four SROs? The Tow-Thomas biquad topology requires two integrators, and the pseudo-differential configuration doubles each integrator into a pair, resulting in four. The two PFDs extract the phase difference between the PWM signals and close the feedback loop—the inner feedback loop ensures the target Q factor, and the outer feedback loop generates the high-pass shape of the BPF. The output of the first PFD is cross-connected to SROs with opposite polarities to achieve subtraction. This design has a system-level elegance: In the half-circuit representation, the BPF requires an external clock $f_{REF}$ to feed the PFD, but after two BPFs are connected in parallel to form a pseudo-differential configuration, the external clock is no longer needed, and the entire BPF works asynchronously—saving the clock tree and its power, and avoiding clock feedthrough contaminating the audio band.

The implementation of gain and bias is also decomposable: The multi-phase PWM inputs of the SRO are summed at internal nodes to drive buffers acting as current-mode DAC arrays, so the SRO output frequency is proportional to the sum of the input PWMs; different switch gains are implemented using switch transistors of different sizes; the unit current is provided by a local FLL bias generator. The $V_{VAR}$ bias voltage is shared among the four SROs, and the current-mode DAC works like a current mirror—the switch gain per channel is determined by $f_{FLL}$ and the current-mode DAC size ratio. For example, the VTC port gain is $K_{IN} f_{FLL}$. The center frequency coverage from 100 Hz to 8 kHz relies on coarse and fine two-stage tuning: The SRO output is first coarsely divided by N using a D flip-flop, and the FLL's $C_{REF}$ is finely tuned through size design (since the FLL locked frequency is proportional to 1/$C_{REF}$). This circuit structure, inherited from the sub-oscillator, enables the BPF to operate at a low voltage of 0.5V.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: PFD as a兼职 Time-Domain Full-Wave Rectifier—"Adding" Instead of "Subtracting"

This is the most beautiful conceptual flip in the entire paper and fills the gap left by previous oscillator-based works [12][13]. The PFD circuit itself consists of only two D flip-flops and one NAND gate (Figure 12). Its state machine is driven by the rising edges of the input PWM, and the three-state output is constituted by two signals, UP and DN, forming ternary quantization—when both are high, the NAND gate immediately resets both flip-flops. Analyzing the state diagram, the UP signal corresponds to the phase difference after positive half-wave rectification, and DN corresponds to the phase difference after negative half-wave rectification. In traditional usage (e.g., in PLLs), UP and DN are subtracted to obtain a linearized phase difference; this paper reverses this by adding them directly to obtain the full-wave rectified output. The physical meaning of this "addition" operation is: Regardless of whether the phase difference is positive or negative, its absolute value information is preserved in the pulse density.

This rectifier has advantages at three levels. First, pure time-domain nature: As long as the input signal swing (phase difference) is within ±2π, it does not exhibit any headroom-related saturation—contrasting with the dilemma of voltage-domain rectifiers in low-voltage processes. Second, implementation cost collapse: Previous voltage-domain designs [8] required several OTAs, reference sources, and passive components that are not friendly to scaling; here, only standard logic gates are needed. Third, a forward-looking observation: The shape of the UP signal is completely consistent with the ReLU activation function widely used in modern DNNs—this means this circuit can be directly transformed into a full-time-domain ReLU activation unit, which the paper explicitly lists as an extension direction in the conclusion. The rectification function is seamlessly embedded in the BPF (Rec-BPF), and the rectified PWM signal is summed in the next PFM stage, eliminating the need for an independent rectification stage.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: SRO Replaces IAF for Pulse Frequency Encoding, XOR Differentiator Fixes Two Old Problems

Previous analog FEx designs [8][9] used integrate-and-fire (IAF) circuits for rate encoding—converting input current into spike PFM signals with pulse frequency proportional to current amplitude. IAF is essentially a relaxation oscillator [28], but its integration occurs in the voltage domain and includes a voltage-domain static amplifier; both components are not friendly to scaling. The alternative solution in this paper (Figure 14): Use the SRO as a PFM encoder, leveraging its natural 2π threshold in the phase domain—integration occurs in the phase domain, inherently avoiding headroom issues; implementation requires only logic gates and bias generators, with no static amplifiers or passive components.

The accompanying digitization step also overturns the old design. The old scheme [8][9] used an asynchronous ripple-carry counter plus multi-bit registers to quantize and sample the PFM signal, which had two clear flaws: First, when sampling falls on the instant of multi-bit binary code transition, the ripple-carry counter exhibits metastability, leading to data corruption; Second, the quantization noise of the ΔΣ-modulated counter output was not filtered by high-pass shaping before being fed directly into the DNN classifier. The solution in this paper is an array of one-bit XOR differentiators (Figure 13): Because the ring oscillator output is in thermometer code, the XOR differentiator guarantees that the worst-case error is within one least significant bit (1 LSB), and this 1 LSB error is noise-shaped, allowing it to be eliminated by oversampling and decimation filtering. Decimation uses a first-order CIC filter with a decimation rate of $2^{10}$, an oversampling clock of 62.5 kHz, and a post-decimation rate of 61 Hz—corresponding to a 16 ms frame shift and 61 frames per second. The bias subtractor $\beta$ in the post-processing chain removes the DC component of the SRO idle frequency; the per-channel gain calibrator $\alpha$ corrects inter-channel gain deviations caused by SRO mismatch; the logarithmic LUT compresses; and the normalizer outputs the final features. The post-processing stage runs on a 61 Hz clock, so its power consumption is negligible.

### Key Technical Innovation 5: GRU-FC Digital Accelerator—Heterogeneous Array Customized for 14-Bit Activation

The digital backend (Figure 15) is a 16-input-48-hidden-48-hidden-12-output GRU-FC network. The entire network model is fully cached in 24 KB weight storage (WMEM), implemented using on-chip SRAM compiled from 6T bit cells provided by the foundry, with weights loaded via the SPI interface. The accelerator consists of 8 heterogeneous processing elements (HPEs) controlled by a finite state machine. Each HPE has a 14-bit multiplier, a 24-bit adder, and a lookup-table-based Sigmoid/Tanh unit. The multiplexer before the multiplier operands selects inputs from four sources—the normalizer, Sigmoid/Tanh unit, WMEM, and output buffer—to compute element-wise vector multiply-adds and hyperbolic functions in the GRU. Partial sums and outputs are stored in a shared 1.3 KB SRAM output buffer. Two power-saving details: Logic synthesis uses a high-threshold voltage device library to reduce leakage; output scores are sent to the external host (Xilinx Zynq-7007S SoC on the MiniZed board) via SPI with an interrupt flag after argmax decoding. The entire classifier performs continuous inference on random GSCD samples at a 250 kHz clock and 0.75V supply, with a measured power consumption of 9.96 μW. Table II reports the total on-chip SRAM as 27 KB.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

The essential difference from the three existing routes can be summarized in one sentence: **Migration of the signal representation domain.** Voltage-domain analog FEx [7][8][9] stores information in voltage amplitude, where headroom and intrinsic gain degrade with process scaling; Digital FEx [6][38][39] pushes the power-hungry ADC off-chip or tolerates high-resolution ADCs; Sequential scanning FEx [3] exchanges time for power, sacrificing frequency information. This paper operates entirely in the time domain except for the initial voltage-to-time conversion stage—information is stored in PWM duty cycles and PFM pulse densities, and the basic components are digital logic gates, naturally inheriting the scaling dividends of logic processes. Compared to other oscillator-based works [12][13], this paper completes the system design strategy for time-domain rectifiers and performs silicon validation with audio classification tasks. Two additional system-level differences are: Support for single-ended microphone interfaces (using the ~10 μW ICS-40310 to replace the differential microphone in Reference [2], resulting in better system-level power consumption), and explicit support for the "Unknown" class as an independent category in the 12-class task—only this paper and Reference [2] achieve this in Table II; other works [6][9][39] explicitly exclude the Unknown class in their class counts, meaning their accuracy numbers cannot be directly compared, or they would require larger models to make up for this accuracy gap.

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

The dataset is the Google Speech Command Dataset (GSCD). The training set contains 38,463 samples, of which 4,044 "Silence" class samples are randomly sampled from the background noise tracks of the dataset, and 4,044 "Unknown" class samples are randomly extracted from words outside the 12 target classes; the test set uses the standard GSCD v0.02 test set, with approximately 400 samples per class for the 12 target classes, resulting in a training-to-test ratio of approximately 8:1.

Here is a methodologically critical detail: Training was not performed on the original GSCD audio. Instead, the entire training and test set samples were played from a laptop to the VTC input of the chip via a USB sound card DAC (Sound Blaster E1), the raw feature vectors output by the chip (FVRaw) were recorded, and then the recorded features underwent $\beta$ DC correction and $\alpha$ channel gain correction, logarithmic compression, and normalization using the $\mu$ and $\sigma$ of the training set features, before being fed into the GRU-FC for training. That is, the classifier learns "the world as seen by this real chip," absorbing the mismatches and non-idealities of the analog front-end into the training data distribution. This is a standard challenge for analog FEx works—inter-chip and intra-chip mismatches cause drift between software features and silicon feature distributions—this paper addresses it head-on with a "hardware-in-the-loop" training flow. During playback, sample amplitudes were normalized to approximately 250 mVPP at the VTC input. Feature recording used a 16 ms frame window and 16 ms frame shift (no overlap between adjacent frames), and the detected class was taken as the most active output at the end of the GSCD sample.

Training configuration: PyTorch 1.8, 200 epochs, AdamW optimizer, initial learning rate 1e-3, weight decay 0.01, ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler (decay factor 0.8, patience 3), minimum learning rate 5e-4; Quantization-aware training quantizes activations and weights to 14 bits and 8 bits, respectively.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

One metric was selected for each of the four dimensions, each with a clear "why": (1) **Classification Accuracy**—using the complete 12-class validation flow of GSCDv2 [35], selecting 12 classes (Silence + Unknown + 10 target words) rather than reporting accuracy for a few words, because Unknown is the hardest class in the GSCD test set; reporting accuracy only on a subset would artificially inflate the number, a point explicitly annotated in footnote C of Table II for other works; (2) **Dynamic Range 54.89 dB**—because KWS accuracy must be maintained across a wide range of volumes, DR is the lifeline of analog FEx and the core metric for comparison with previous voltage-domain works; (3) **Schreier FoM 93.11 dB**—to fairly compare FExs with different frame shifts, DR, power, and frame shift are折算 into a single number (Equations 7, 8), which is a standard weapon in the ADC field; (4) **End-to-End Latency 12.4 ms**—directly benchmarked against the 20 ms perception threshold. The power dimension reports total power of 23 μW and a complete power breakdown (Figure 21), because the "system-level 100 μW" goal requires knowing where every microwatt is spent.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Chip Measured Accuracy.** The prototype chip achieved an overall accuracy of 86.03% on the GSCD test set (Figure 19). Per-class true positive rates show: "Silence" is the easiest class, with 100% accuracy; the classifier performs best on the two keywords "Stop" and "Yes," with 93%; the hardest is the "Unknown" class—it contains 25 non-target words (such as "Happy", "Dog"), requiring the classifier to have larger model capacity to distinguish them. The authors explicitly state that a larger network on this class would improve overall accuracy at the cost of power and area. The FEx audio response measured with "Yes" keyword samples (254 mVPP) (Figure 18) verifies the physical reasonableness of the feature extraction: The "Ye" sound has high response in low-frequency channels, and the "s" sound has high response in high-frequency channels—the phonetic common sense that voiced sound energy is concentrated in low-frequency formants and fricative sound energy is concentrated in high frequencies is faithfully presented in the silicon features.

**Gap with Software Model and Attribution.** The software model achieved 91.35% versus the chip's 86.03%, a difference of about five percentage points. The authors attribute this to the noise floor lifting at large input amplitudes (Figure 17c): At zero input, the in-band integration noise is 248 μVRMS, dominated by 1/f noise; when a 2 kHz sine wave is added to 390 mVPP, the in-band noise becomes dominated by thermal noise—the authors speculate that the increased noise comes from the rise in ring oscillator phase noise due to the increased operating frequency of the SRO [34]. This attribution directly points the direction for improvement to noise suppression techniques such as chopper stabilization [43] and dynamic element matching [5].

**Benchmarking Against Large Models.** The state-of-the-art accuracy for GRU-RNN on GSCD is 94.2% [36], but that network has 499 KB of parameters and runs on a Cortex-M7—this paper's 24 KB weight storage would need 21 times the on-chip storage to hold it, causing power and area to increase accordingly. This comparison frankly acknowledges the accuracy gap and defines the nature of the gap: It is a gap in model capacity budget, not a gap in the expressive power of time-domain features.

**Horizontal Comparison of Analog FEx (Table I).** Compared to four state-of-the-art analog FExs: M. Yang JSSC 2016 (180 nm, gm-C BPF, 64×2 channels, 55 μW, DR 55 dB, FoM 82.3); K. ### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The "ablation" in this JSSC paper is distributed across three levels: software modeling, calibration, and noise. Each addresses a "why this design" question:

**Finding 1: Logarithmic Compression + Normalization Two-Level Feature Post-Processing Contributes Over Thirteen Percentage Points (Figure 2).** The baseline software model (without compressor and normalizer) achieved 12-class GSCD accuracy of 77.89%; after adding these two levels, it reached 91.35%. Why it is effective: The dynamic range of analog FEx is naturally limited; logarithmic compression imitates the adaptive gain compression of the biological cochlea—compressing the wide dynamic range envelope into a narrow representation space, which is equivalent to performing automatic gain equalization in the amplitude dimension for the backend classifier; normalization pulls each channel's features back to a unified scale, which is a standard prerequisite for DNN training. These two levels cost almost zero hardware (a LUT and one multiply-subtract operation in the 61 Hz clock post-processing), yet they are the highest cost-performance design decisions in the entire chain. This finding has direct transfer value for engineering teams working on analog front-ends: The "algorithmic leverage" of feature post-processing can make up for the shortcomings of analog circuit dynamic range.

**Finding 2: Mismatch Between 14-Bit Activation Quantization and 12-Bit Quantizer Dynamic Range Burieds Accuracy.** The paper notes at the end of Section 2: The baseline accuracy in Figure 2 would be higher if floating-point activation were used, because 14-bit quantization (6 integer bits + 8 fractional bits) cannot cover the dynamic range of the 12-bit unsigned quantizer output. This is a lesson in quantization design—the bit-width at the feature generation end and the quantization format at the classifier end must be jointly designed; otherwise, high-bit features will be truncated by low-dynamic-range activation formats. Although the ablation was completed only at the software level, it explains the origin of the 14/8-bit choice in subsequent quantization-aware training.

**Finding 3: Channel Gain Mismatch Mainly Comes from Systematic Bias Distribution, Correctable by Layout Tuning (Figure 17a/b).** The measured gain curve before calibration showed obvious inter-channel deviations, attributed to systematic mismatch in the SRO-based PFM encoder—the voltage bias ($V_{VAR}$) generated by a single FLL circuit and shared by the 16-channel SROs has a distribution gradient on the chip. After correction by per-channel gain calibration $\alpha$, the response was normalized. The prescription given by the authors has engineering depth: Systematic mismatch can be improved with better layout planning (e.g., centralized placement of bias circuits), random mismatch with larger bias transistor sizes, and a third path is combining it with mismatch-aware DNN training [8]. This reveals the inherent tax of analog FEx relative to all-digital solutions: Every chip requires calibration.

**Finding 4: Power-Temperature Compensation Compresses Sub-Oscillator Drift by Nearly Twenty Times (Figure 6, Simulation).** Comparing the sub-oscillator frequency drift before and after adding the compensation circuit (devices marked in red in Figure 5), it was reduced by 19.98 times in the worst case. The baseline sub-oscillator's frequency tuning curve decreases monotonically with control voltage; the compensated version uses PTAT $V_{VAR}$ to drive the MOS varactor to adaptively cancel the temperature trend, and uses the N1-P1 path to reduce VDD sensitivity. Without this level of stability, the 0.5V-supplied time-domain chain would lose lock or suffer worsened mismatch under temperature fluctuations—it is a prerequisite for low-voltage time-domain designs to enter real environments. It should be noted that this is a simulation result; the paper does not report silicon temperature sweep measured data.

**Finding 5: Noise Robustness Shows Almost No Loss Up to 40 dB SNR (Figure 20).** Gaussian noise with different standard deviations was added to the recorded FVRaw to construct different SNRs (Equation 9), and the network was retrained for each SNR case using the noisy training set. Results: When the input-referred noise was as high as 432 μVRMS (corresponding to 40 dB SNR), the accuracy dropped by less than 1%. Why add noise in the feature domain rather than the acoustic domain: This is a natural extension of the hardware-in-the-loop flow—training and evaluation occur directly on the feature distribution output by the chip, and the noise model is superimposed on the same distribution, isolating the two variables of "front-end robustness" and "acoustic channel robustness." This experiment also implies: The 1/f noise floor and shaped quantization noise of the time-domain FEx are at a level that the classifier can easily learn to ignore.

## Main Contributions

**Contribution 1: First silicon-validated ring-oscillator-based audio feature extractor, and first oscillator-based BPF for KWS tasks.** Previous works [12][13] only validated the oscillator BPF unit; this paper fabricates the entire chain of VTC, BPF, rectifier, PFM encoder, and TDC and runs it through a classification task, filling the gap of "having bricks but no house."

**Contribution 2: Established a processing paradigm that is entirely time-domain except for the voltage-to-time conversion.** PWM and PFM time-domain encoding run through the entire chain, with basic components all falling on digital logic gates, fundamentally bypassing the two process scaling deadlocks of headroom shrinkage and intrinsic gain degradation, and opening the door to 0.5V low-voltage supply. The paper's judgment is: At advanced process nodes, this time-domain technology will surpass voltage-domain designs in power and area.

**Contribution 3: Design strategy for PFD as a兼职 full-wave rectifier.** This single sign flip of "adding UP and DN instead of subtracting" compresses the voltage-domain rectification function [8], which previously required multiple OTAs, reference sources, and passive components, into two D flip-flops and one NAND gate, and incidentally discovered that the UP signal is isomorphic to the ReLU activation function—leaving an interface for full-time-domain neural network activation units.

**Contribution 4: SRO-based ΔΣ TDC replaces the old scheme of IAF plus ripple counter.** The 2π threshold integration in the phase domain avoids headroom saturation; the array XOR differentiator simultaneously solves two old problems: metastable data corruption (worst case 1 LSB and noise-shaped) and quantization noise not being filtered before entering the DNN. First-order noise shaping is confirmed by a 20 dB/dec slope in the measured spectrum.

**Contribution 5: System-level complete validation and best figure of merit.** 65 nm fabrication, 2.03 mm², 23 μW (analog 0.5V + digital 0.75V), 12.4 ms latency, GSCD 12-class 86.03% (including Unknown as an independent class), single-ended microphone support—occupies positions in the constraint quadrilateral of "low latency + full classes + on-chip FEx + on-chip classifier" that others have not fully filled; the Schreier FoM of 93.11 dB for analog FEx is the best among compared designs, and DR of 54.89 dB leads at 16 ms frame shift.

**Contribution 6: Hardware-in-the-loop training-evaluation methodology.** The classifier is trained and tested directly on chip-measured features, absorbing analog mismatch into the training distribution; the accompanying $\beta/\alpha$ calibration and $\mu/\sigma$ normalization flow provides a reusable template for similar works.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**Hard Binding Between Accuracy and Model Capacity.** 86.03% is five percentage points away from the software model's 91.35% and eight percentage points away from the large model SOTA of 94.2% [36]. The former is attributed to the noise floor lifting at high input amplitudes (SRO phase noise rises with operating frequency); the latter is essentially the capacity ceiling of 24 KB weight storage—making up for it requires 21 times the storage. There is little room left in the 23 μW budget for larger networks, unless the SRAM leakage is addressed (leakage accounts for 25% of accelerator power, of which SRAM accounts for 78%).

**Tax of Per-Chip Calibration.** Analog channel gain mismatch requires per-chip $\alpha$ (and $\beta$) calibration, a step not needed for all-digital solutions [2]. Although the cause has been located to bias distribution and device mismatch, the three paths of layout optimization, larger devices, and mismatch-aware training are only "improvements"; none can eradicate it like digital circuits.

**Distance Between Dynamic Range and the Real Acoustic World.** The 54.89 dB DR leads in the comparison table, but real-world sound pressure dynamic range can easily exceed this number. The paper itself admits that the amplitude input to the VTC is "assumed to be large enough"—real systems need an ultra-low-power pre-amplifier [33] before the VTC—meaning the current validation was completed under "ideal drive" conditions, and the real front-end with direct microphone connection is not yet closed-loop. Front-end automatic gain control is listed as a necessary enhancement for tackling harder tasks.

**Noise Suppression Technology Stack Not Yet Fully Deployed.** Standard weapons such as chopper stabilization and dynamic element matching have not yet been deployed; the 248 μVRMS zero-input noise floor dominated by 1/f noise still has room for compression—this is both a limitation and an already indicated improvement path.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

**Narrowness of Validation Under Single Conditions.** Power was measured at a single point of 25°C room temperature; the effect of power-temperature compensation is backed by simulation (Figure 6); the paper does not report silicon temperature sweeps, or measured accuracy/DR under power supply variations. Statistical data on inter-chip variation (PVT corners, batches) is not reported—yet this scheme relies on per-chip calibration, and the variation distribution is the true determinant of calibration costs.

**Modeling Method of Noise Experiments.** Noise was superimposed on the recorded feature vectors in the form of Gaussian noise (and the network was retrained for each SNR case), rather than adding real environmental noise or reverberation at the acoustic end. Adding noise in the feature domain isolates variables cleanly, but it also means that the spectral coloring of the microphone, acoustic path, and real noise was not tested. Robustness under real far-field and additive channel noise is not reported by the paper.

**Simplification of Task Setting.** 16 ms frame window and frame shift with no overlap, and the detected class is taken as the most active output at the end of the sample—this is an "offline" protocol of "giving an answer after the entire speech segment ends"; metrics for streaming detection, false alarm rate, and wake-word specific tasks are not reported (the paper lists streaming KWS as a future direction). The 12 classes are also a simplified subset of GSCD; the full 35-class set was not validated.

**Residual Issues with Comparison Fairness.** The class counts (2/5/7/12), datasets (TIDIGITS vs. GSCD), and inclusion of off-chip ADCs differ among works in Table II. The paper honestly annotates this in footnotes and argues for the meaning of the Unknown class, but horizontal accuracy numbers can still only be compared qualitatively. Additionally, the attribution of accuracy gaps (noise floor) lacks direct closed-loop verification—for example, the paper does not report a control experiment such as "the gap shrinks to X after adding chopper."

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

The roadmap explicitly given in the paper's conclusion and discussion sections, combined with extensions derivable from the main text, can be summarized into six points:

**First, Circuit-Level Root Cure for Noise and Mismatch.** Deploy chopper stabilization [43] and dynamic element matching [5] to the front-end to lower the noise floor and narrow the five-percentage-point gap between the chip and the software model; combine layout planning (centralized placement of bias circuits), larger device sizes, and mismatch-aware DNN training [8] in a three-pronged approach, with the goal of completely eliminating the per-chip calibration step.

**Second, Dynamic Range Expansion and Real Front-End Closed Loop.** Add front-end automatic gain control and an ultra-low-power pre-amplifier [33] before the VTC, moving the chip from a "function generator-driven validation platform" to a real system with direct microphone connection—this is the ticket for time-domain FEx to move towards "more challenging real-world audio inference tasks."

**Third, Task Upgrade.** The full 35-class GSCD set and streaming mode KWS [44] (Rybakov et al., Interspeech 2020 streaming keyword detection paradigm) are two targets named by the paper; the accuracy短板 of the Unknown class also needs to be supplemented with larger model capacity combined with leakage optimization (custom storage cells [6]).

**Fourth, Spillover of Time-Domain Technology.** The observation that the PFD's UP signal is isomorphic to ReLU points to full-time-domain DNN activation units—advancing the time-domain components of this paper from feature extraction to inside network computation is the most direct one among the "various efficient circuit design opportunities" in the authors' eyes. Similarly, components such as SRO integrators and time-domain biquads can be migrated to other sensing domains outside of audio.

**Fifth, Realization of Process Scaling Dividends.** The paper's core assertion—that time-domain designs will surpass voltage-domain designs in power and area at advanced nodes—has currently only been validated once at 65 nm. Repeating this design at 28 nm, 16 nm, and even more advanced nodes, and quantifying the growth curve of advantages with node scaling, is the key experiment to push this paradigm from "feasible" to "mandatory."

**Sixth, System-Level Closed Loop.** The chip already integrates a switched-capacitor energy harvester, reference source, and LDO (inherited from the solar-powered version of ISSCC 2022 [14], i.e., "23 μW Solar-Powered KWS ASIC"). Composing the energy harvester, single-ended microphone, time-domain FEx, and GRU accelerator into a self-powered complete audio node, and providing measured end-to-end system power consumption (benchmarking against the 100 μW system budget), is the last kilometer for this technology to move from paper to product.

---

**One-Sentence Summary**: This paper uses the migration of the representation domain—"moving information from voltage amplitude to pulse time"—to convert the process scaling deadlocks (headroom, intrinsic gain) of the KWS analog front-end into problems that digital logic gates are naturally good at solving. Using four interlocking circuit inventions: nested FLL linearized VCO, SRO dual-integrator loop BPF, PFD addition rectification, and SRO-based ΔΣ TDC, it delivered a scorecard of 23 μW, 12.4 ms, 12-class 86.03%, and analog FEx Schreier FoM 93.11 dB on 65 nm silicon—it proves not the abstract proposition that "time-domain is better," but a complete engineering path where every design decision is backed by mathematical form and measured data.
