## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is a persistent entry point for intelligent voice assistants: devices must accurately determine "whether the audio in this second is a wake-word" from a continuous audio stream. Meanwhile, the recognition process must be efficient, the model memory footprint must be small, and it must remain robust across users with different accents and speaking habits (the paper describes these three product constraints for the KWS task in Section 1). Recent mainstream approaches use deep networks such as DSCNN, ResNet, RNNs with multi-head attention, and Transformers, training centrally on public benchmarks like Google Speech Commands—i.e., aggregating raw recordings from all users onto a single server to train the model.

The pain point here is not accuracy, but data ownership. Voice recordings are highly sensitive privacy data: they carry biometric and demographic features such as speaker identity, accent, region, and language habits. Moreover, wake-word detection inherently requires devices to listen continuously, generating data continuously. Uploading such data in its original form to the cloud is simply not feasible in many compliance scenarios. Federated Learning (FL) offers a training paradigm where data stays local: clients only update models locally, and the server only aggregates and returns parameters.

However, bringing KWS into the federated framework collides with one of the most severe forms of data heterogeneity in FL. The paper decomposes the non-IID nature of the KWS scenario into three types of skew (Section 1): quantity skew (some users contribute very few samples), label distribution skew (users prefer different words), and feature distribution skew (users have accents). These three skews are the norm, not the exception, in KWS. Their severity can be read directly from Table 1: for the 12-class task, splitting the training set by user yields 2,234 clients and a total of 45.6k training samples, with an average of 20.4 samples per user and a maximum of 141 samples for the user with the most data; for the 35-class task, there are 2,434 clients, 105.5k samples, an average of 43.3 samples per user, and a maximum of 316 samples. An average local data volume of just over 20 samples means that most clients cannot even cover all 12 classes—each user is both a "data source" and a "data silo."

This data distribution leads to two fatal consequences (Abstract and Section 1): first, overfitting to user-specific information. The paper provides two very specific failure examples—the feature extractor treating a specific user's accent as a key discriminative factor, or the classification layer biasing towards words commonly used by that user; second, weight divergence, where local update directions deviate severely from the global direction, causing distortion in the parameter averaging aggregation on the server side (Section 3, citing reference [12]). In other words, the problem is not "slow convergence of federated training," but rather "each client is pulling the model towards itself, resulting in a global model that resembles no one."

Another easily overlooked structural amplifier is the ratio of local training steps to local data volume. The federated experiments in the paper uniformly set the number of local training steps to E=50 and batch size to 32 (Section 5), meaning each selected client consumes approximately 1,600 sample points per round. Based on the average sample sizes in Table 1, for the 12-class task, the average client repeats its 20.4 samples about 78 times per round (50×32/20.4), and for the 35-class task, about 37 times; even for users with the most samples (141/316 samples), they repeat the data 11 to 5 times per round. This calculation shows that under the real data partitioning of FedKWS, local "over-training" is not a hyperparameter mistake, but an inevitable behavior under the default FedAvg protocol—overfitting to user-specific information is guaranteed by the protocol structure. This is the starting point for understanding the entire design of this paper.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper systematically reviews available tools in Sections 2 and 3 and points out gaps one by one:

- **FedAvg Series (including existing FedKWS work)**: FedAvg uniformly assigns E steps of local training to all selected clients, completely ignoring data quality. Reference [17] (Leroy et al., ICASSP 2019) extensively validated FedAvg on the Hey Snips dataset and adopted adaptive average aggregation (i.e., the FedOpt route); Reference [18] (Hard et al., Interspeech 2020) studied data augmentation and distillation in FedKWS to address resource constraints and sample labeling issues. Neither of these lines treats "local models overfitting user-specific information" as the core attack target.
- **FedOpt**: Only replaces simple parameter averaging with an adaptive optimizer on the server side. It treats the "aggregator" but does not cure the root cause: "local updates themselves are contaminated."
- **FedRS**: Designed for label shift, it uses a constrained softmax, covering only one of the three skews, and is powerless against feature skew caused by accents.
- **FedProx / FedMMD / FedDyn / MOON (family of regularization methods)**: They add regularization terms during local training to pull local models towards the global model, acting at the level of parameter space or intermediate feature space. The paper points out two defects: First, such regularization only limits the "drift amount" and never specifies "which components are user-specific and should be removed," representing a passive distance constraint rather than active information discrimination. Second, based on conclusions from references [27, 28, 29] (research on feature transferability, personalized federated learning, etc.), feature distribution skew mainly erodes the lower layers of the network, while label distribution skew mainly erodes the upper layers. FedKWS suffers from both skews simultaneously—bias is dispersed throughout the network, making regularization targeting specific layers or parameter space structurally insufficient.
- **System Scheduling Level**: Prior to this paper, no FedKWS work adaptively allocated local computation based on client data quality.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the paper aims to answer three questions simultaneously: First, how to explicitly identify and suppress "user-specific information" (accents, preference for common words) in local training, rather than broadly limiting local model drift—requiring regularization to act on the entire prediction function level rather than specific layers. Second, how to reallocate local training steps among clients with dual imbalances in data volume and class distribution, allowing clients with abundant and balanced data to contribute more, while clients with poor data contribute less to aggregation, while keeping the total computation comparable to FedAvg. Third, how to make the method independent of specific network architectures, ensuring universality across DSCNN, MHAttRNN, ResNet, and Transformer. The method name FedKWS-UI (Federated KWS with User-Invariant information) points directly to the goal: enabling the federated model to learn user-invariant information.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

FedKWS-UI fully preserves the communication skeleton of FedAvg (downloading the global model, multiple local update steps, server parameter averaging, iterating for T rounds), on top of which it overlays two orthogonal strategies:

- **ALO (Adversarial Learning against Overfitted models)**—learning user-invariant information "explicitly" from the loss function level;
- **ALT (Adaptive Local Training)**—learning user-invariant information "implicitly" from the system scheduling level (original words from the abstract: explicitly and implicitly).

This "one algorithm, one system" dual-path design is itself the core architectural judgment of the paper: user-specific information enters both in "how the loss is calculated" (the loss of a single client is dominated by its accent and common words) and in "who trains more" (poor-quality clients and high-quality clients receive the same number of training steps), so surgery on a single level is not thorough.

The carrier of ALO is the private-shared models framework (continuation of references [30] FedPHP, [31]): each client maintains not only the shared global model $f$ participating in aggregation but also a private model $f_p$ that is never uploaded. The private model is deliberately trained to overfit using pure cross-entropy, serving as a "detector of user-specific information"; the global model is trained on the same data to move in a direction "away from this detector." The shared part learns the common discriminative patterns, while the private part absorbs user-specific biases, and the two meet with opposite signs in the loss function.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The basic optimization objective of FL (Section 3): $\min_\psi \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k L(D_k; \psi)$, where $\psi$ is the global parameter, and $p_k$ is the weight of the $k$-th client. FedAvg solves this alternately through local/global two processes: the selected client set $S_t$ downloads the global model $\psi_t$, performs local multi-step updates to obtain $\hat{\psi}_t^k$, and the server performs parameter averaging $\psi_{t+1} \leftarrow \frac{1}{|S_t|}\sum_{k\in S_t}\hat{\psi}_t^k$, iterating for T rounds.

The training loss of ALO consists of three equations. First, the private model is trained to overfit on client $k$ using standard cross-entropy:

$$L(D_k;\psi_p^k) = \mathbb{E}_{x_i,y_i\sim D_k}\left[-\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathbb{I}\{y_i=c\}\log[f_p(x_i)]_c\right]$$

Then, the downloaded global model is trained using equations (1)(2)(3):

$$L_{ls} = \mathbb{E}_{x_i,y_i}\left[-\sum_{c=1}^{C}\big[(1-\mu)\mathbb{I}\{y_i=c\} + \mu/C\big]\log[f(x_i)]_c\right] \tag{1}$$

$$L_{adv} = -\mathbb{E}_{x_i,y_i}\left[\sum_{c=1}^{C}\underbrace{-[f_p(x_i)]_c}_{negative}\log[f(x_i)]_c\right] \tag{2}$$

$$L(D_k;\psi^k) = L_{ls}(D_k;\psi^k) + \lambda L_{adv}(D_k;\psi^k) \tag{3}$$

Equation (1) is label smoothing with coefficient $\mu$: the target for the true label is softened from one-hot to a peak of $(1-\mu)$ plus a uniform base of $\mu/C$. Equation (2) is the most critical step in the paper, termed "negative distillation": it formally follows the cross-entropy form of knowledge distillation [32, 33, 34], but the teacher distribution is globally negated—$-[f_p(x_i)]_c$ is a set of non-positive numbers summing to -1 over $c$. Minimizing equation (2) is equivalent to minimizing $\sum_c [f_p(x_i)]_c \log[f(x_i)]_c$, i.e., requiring the global model to output low probability where $f_p$ probability is high (overfitted regions). Ordinary distillation is "moving towards the teacher," here it is "moving away from the teacher": pushing the global model's predictions away from overfitted regions. Why is it called regularization at the function level: equation (2) does not involve any parameter distance terms nor align any intermediate features; it directly constrains the behavior of the output function on the data distribution—this precisely bypasses the structural difficulty of "bias dispersed across different layers."

The mathematics of ALT (latter half of Section 4): Let the $k$-th client have $n_k$ training samples and class distribution $q_k\in\mathbb{R}^C$ ($\sum_c q_{k,c}=1$, $q_{k,c}\ge 0$). Define normalized sample size $\bar{n}_k = n_k/\max_{j\in[K]}n_j \in [0,1]$ and normalized class distribution entropy $e_k = \left(-\sum_{c=1}^C q_{k,c}\log q_{k,c}\right)/\log C \in [0,1]$. The client utility is the harmonic mean of the two: $r_k = 2\bar{n}_k e_k/(\bar{n}_k + e_k) \in [0,1]$. The $k$-th client performs $r_0\cdot r_k\cdot E$ steps of gradient updates per round, where $r_0$ is determined by total conservation: $\sum_{k=1}^K r_0 r_k E \approx K\cdot E$, solving for $r_0 = K/\sum_k r_k$. The necessity of normalization lies in pulling "sample count" (dimension is tens to hundreds of samples) and "entropy" (dimension is 0 to 1) into the same [0,1] interval to combine them; using $\log C$ as the denominator makes entropy comparable between the 12-class and 35-class tasks.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: ALO—Reverse Navigation using an "Overfitted Teacher"

Every step in the design chain of ALO has a clear "why." **Why create an overfitted private model first?** Because user-specific information is implicit; there are no labels saying "this feature is an accent." The only reliable carrier is a model trained to the limit on that user's data: when $f_p$ learns to fit the user $k$'s 20 samples with near-perfect accuracy, what precipitates in its prediction distribution is "everything that can be memorized in this batch of data," among which user-specific components (accents, preferred words) are fully amplified. Overfitting here transforms from a defect into a tool for the first time—it is a differentiable probe for user-specific information. **Why use negated distillation instead of generative adversarial min-max games?** The opponent is frozen: after $f_p$ is trained in advance, it remains fixed during global model training. The "adversarial" aspect manifests as a constant pushing-away direction, avoiding剧烈 oscillations of dynamic dual-model games under small sample sizes, resulting in much higher training stability than true adversarial training. **Why must label smoothing of equation (1) be superimposed?** Because the negative teacher is not a pure detector of user-specific information—$f_p$ also learns true discriminative patterns. Moving away blindly would destroy discriminative information along with it. Equation (1) anchors the discriminative signal of the task with true labels (softened), allowing the global model to deviate from the teacher under small weight $\lambda$. The paper itself points out the independent role of equation (1): preventing the global model from being overly confident about data from a specific user (Section 4). **Why must $\lambda$ be small?** Ablation provides quantitative boundaries (Figure 3 right, 12-class ResNet): at $\lambda=0$, 85.29; at $\lambda=1e-4$, 86.07; at $\lambda=1e-3$, 86.23 (optimal); at $\lambda=1e-2$, drops to 82.79; at $\lambda=1e-1$, collapses to 76.99. The adversarial term is a navigation fine-tuning, not the main loss. Increasing the weight by one order of magnitude starts to become harmful, and by two orders of magnitude directly destroys training—this indirectly confirms the judgment that "discriminative information is mixed in the negative teacher."

The cost at the implementation level also needs to be noted: each client must additionally maintain and train a private model in advance, nearly doubling local computation and storage. The private model is not uploaded (aggregation only involves $\hat{\psi}_t^k$), so communication volume remains unchanged, but the paper does not report specific numbers for local overhead; how many training steps constitute "sufficient overfitting" for the private model, and whether its training occurs every round or only at the initialization stage, are not detailed in the paper (the text only says "after abundant training steps").

### Key Technical Innovation 2: ALT—Reallocating Training Steps Based on Data Quality

ALT answers the scheduling question: FedAvg gives every selected client the same E steps, on what basis? On KWS data with dual imbalances in quantity and class distribution (Figure 1 right scatter plot: the entropy of client class distribution for task 12 ranges from close to the theoretical upper limit of uniform distribution $\log 12 \approx 2.49$ to about 0.21; for task 35, from close to $\log 35 \approx 3.56$ to about 0.15—i.e., there exist users who almost only say one or two words), "treating everyone equally" means letting a small user with 100 skewed samples pull the global model with the same intensity as a large user with 316 balanced samples. Figure 1 right also uses color depth to mark the value of $r_0\cdot r_k$ for each client: the color scale maximum is approximately 3.49 and 4.70 for the two tasks, approaching the upper limits of r0 (3.5 and 5.0), while the minimum drops to the magnitude of 0.15 to 0.21—the utility gap between high-quality and low-quality clients is an order of magnitude.

**Why use harmonic mean instead of arithmetic mean or simple weighting by sample size?** The harmonic mean has a短板 (short-board) effect: if either $\bar{n}_k$ or $e_k$ is close to 0, $r_k$ is pressed close to 0. This precisely captures the semantics that "both types of qualification are necessary conditions"—users with large data volume but severely skewed class distribution (the model will be pulled towards their preferred words) should not train more, and users with uniform class distribution but only a few samples (bound to overfit) should not train more. Under arithmetic mean, these two bad cases can both receive medium utility; weighting by sample size completely ignores class imbalance. **Why enforce total conservation?** $\sum_k r_0 r_k E \approx K E$ ensures that the expected total number of training steps for all clients is the same as FedAvg. Performance improvements cannot come from secretly spending more money; ablation and comparative experiments are then fair. Inferring from the given r0 ($r_0=K/\sum_k r_k$, taking 3.5 and 5.0), the average utility $\overline{r_k}$ for the two tasks is approximately 0.29 and 0.20 respectively—the utility of the vast majority of clients is low, which is precisely the reason why the 35-class task requires a larger r0 compensation. **Implicit semantics of ALT**: Letting low-utility clients train less is equivalent to weakening the negative impact of unqualified clients on aggregation; letting high-utility clients train more is safe because their data distribution is closer to the global distribution, the bias introduced by more training is small, and shared information is greater. The paper admits a boundary: client computing power heterogeneity is not included in $r_k$, left for future work (end of Section 4).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Vs. FedProx / FedDyn / MOON**: These three pull the local model towards the global model (parameters or intermediate features), following a "limit amplitude" logic; ALO introduces an external reference frame (overfitted private model) to "push away," actively discriminating information to be deleted rather than broadly limiting drift, and acting at the function level rather than parameter/feature level (positive comparison in the last paragraph of Section 3).
- **Vs. FedRS**: FedRS only treats label shift; ALO bypasses the problem of "which layer is eroded by which skew" at the function level, being effective against both feature skew and label skew.
- **Vs. Distillation in Reference [18]**: That is forward distillation for knowledge aggregation, direction is "moving towards"; ALO is negative distillation for information removal, direction is "moving away," giving opposite optimization semantics to the same mathematical form.
- **Vs. FedOpt**: FedOpt changes the server-side aggregator, while FedKWS-UI changes the client-side loss and scheduling; the two are orthogonal. In experiments, FedOpt collapses on the 35-class Transformer (54.56, Figure 2), indicating that treating only the aggregator is insufficient.
- **ALT Dimension**: Introducing "data quality-aware computing power reallocation" into FedKWS; previous related work (adaptive averaging in [17], augmentation and distillation in [18]) did not touch the system scheduling level.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Experiments were conducted on Google Speech Commands (using the federated partition configuration recommended by FedScale [35]), with two tasks: 12-class (10 words + silence + unknown) and 35-class (35 words). The training set is split into clients according to the user IDs provided in the dataset, with each user forming one client; the test set is not split, specifically used to evaluate the generalization ability of the aggregated global model. Table 1 gives the scale: 12-class task K=2,234 clients, N=45.6k training samples, average 20.4 samples per client, max 141 samples, test samples M=4.9k; 35-class task K=2,434, N=105.5k, average 43.3 samples, max 316 samples, M=11.0k. Features are 40-dimensional MFCCs (30ms window, 10ms step), and standard dataset augmentations are followed: random time shift ±100ms, and mixing in background noise of 0.1 volume with 0.8 probability (Section 5 Datasets paragraph). Notably, the feature extraction and augmentation strategy of this partition is exactly the same as centralized training, so the performance gap between federated and centralized training can only be attributed to the training paradigm and data heterogeneity itself, not differences in the feature pipeline.

On the network side (Section 5 and Table 2), four architectures are examined, deliberately controlling parameter counts to be at the same level: DSCNN (172 channels, 169K/173K parameters), MHAttRNN (4 heads, 80 hidden neurons, 228K/232K), ResNet (15 layers, 45 channels per basic block, 238K/239K), Transformer (4 layers, model dimension 96, 232K/234K). Centralized training serves as the performance upper bound: DSCNN 97.19/96.95 (12-class/35-class), MHAttRNN 97.31/97.05, ResNet 97.89/97.31, Transformer 96.21/97.14 (Table 2). The paper explicitly states the reason why Transformer did not reach SOTA: only 4 layers and 0.23M parameters were used, whereas the original Keyword Transformer paper [8] has networks up to 5.4M— all comparisons are conducted at the "small model" tier, which aligns with the actual budget for edge-side KWS.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The primary metric is global test set accuracy: recorded every 3 communication rounds, with the average of the last 5 rounds taken as the final score (Figure 2 caption), to eliminate random fluctuations from single-round aggregation. Auxiliary metrics are False Acceptance Rate (FA) and False Rejection Rate (FR) (Table 3): for the 12-class task, the 10 words are treated as positive classes, their FA is averaged, and silence and unknown are treated as negative classes; decision thresholds are not adjusted, and FA/FR under raw predictions are reported directly. The rationale is product-oriented: in KWS deployment, FA corresponds to "false wake-up" and FR corresponds to "failure to wake up," both directly determining user experience and being closer to real pain points than accuracy; not adjusting thresholds avoids the space for manipulating single metrics by changing the operating point. Centralized accuracy (Table 2) serves as a third reference to quantify the accuracy cost paid by federating itself.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Federated settings are unified: batch size 32, local steps E=50, 300 rounds. Baselines are FedAvg ([10], [18] adopted), FedProx, FedMMD, FedOpt ([19], [17] adopted), and hyperparameter scans were performed on the baselines (FedProx/FedMMD regularization coefficients {1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2}; FedOpt global optimizers {SGD, Adam} and corresponding global learning rates) before taking the optimal results. The comparison is thorough. FedKWS-UI hyperparameters: $\mu=0.2$, $\lambda=0.001$, $r_0=3.5$ (12-class) / 5.0 (35-class).

Figure 2 (average accuracy of the last 5 rounds, by 12-class/35-class × four architectures, total 8 groups, order: FedAvg / FedProx / FedMMD / FedOpt / FedKWS-UI):

- 12-class DSCNN: 82.85 / 85.51 / 83.74 / 85.50 / **87.29** (leading best baseline by +1.78)
- 12-class MHAttRNN: 86.20 / 86.39 / 85.92 / 86.38 / **86.57** (+0.18)
- 12-class ResNet: 83.48 / 83.93 / 83.90 / 81.30 / **86.23** (+2.30)
- 12-class Transformer: 71.17 / 72.15 / 72.71 / 71.12 / **79.06** (+6.35, improvement specifically cited in the paper text)
- 35-class DSCNN: 82.59 / 81.44 / 82.79 / 84.23 / **84.51** (+0.28)
- 35-class MHAttRNN: 79.80 / 83.10 / 83.64 / 82.83 / **84.60** (+0.96)
- 35-class ResNet: 79.05 / 75.78 / 80.07 / 79.33 / **80.50** (+0.43)
- 35-class Transformer: 70.24 / 70.07 / 66.78 / 54.56 / **74.75** (+4.51)

Five levels of conclusions can be read from this set of numbers. **First, the cost of federating is precisely quantified**: the best results of all baselines on the 12-class task are only 86.39 (MHAttRNN + FedProx), differing from the centralized ResNet's 97.89 by 11.5 points (original words in Section 5 text), and FedKWS-UI's best 87.29 still differs by 9.9 points—the damage caused by non-IID is far from healed; this paper is stopping the bleeding, not curing the root cause. **Second, the ranking of architectures under federated learning differs from centralized**: MHAttRNN is the most stable, Transformer is the worst. The paper's guess (explicitly marked as a guess in the original text) is that MHAttRNN directly calculates sequence information and attention can precisely capture important signals, making it more robust to random time-shift augmentation; Transformer lacks the inductive bias of convolution/recurrence, making it more brittle on small-data clients (the latter half is a reasonable inference, not expanded in the paper). **Third, FedKWS-UI wins all eight groups**, and the advantage is significant on DSCNN/ResNet/Transformer in the 12-class task, verifying the universality claim of method-architecture decoupling. **Fourth, the baseline camp is internally unstable**: FedProx on 35-class ResNet (75.78) is actually lower than FedAvg (79.05); FedOpt catastrophically collapses on 35-class Transformer to 54.56, 15.68 points lower than FedAvg. The paper does not explain the reason for FedOpt's collapse; a reasonable hypothesis is that the adaptive server optimizer amplifies the aggregation direction already polluted by non-IID, and noise is further scaled on the weakest architecture—this is an inference of this note, not a conclusion of the paper. These counterexamples collectively show that the two existing routes of "pulling parameters/features together" and "server adaptation" are not reliable under the dual skew of KWS. **Fifth, FA/FR improve synchronously** (Table 3, DSCNN, 12-class, final aggregated model, no threshold adjustment): On FA, FedAvg 0.27, FedOpt 0.32, FedKWS-UI 0.23; On FR, 5.03, 3.19, 2.78. Note that FedOpt is a trade-off relative to FedAvg (FR drops but FA rises), while FedKWS-UI dominates both metrics—dominating both metrics without moving the threshold indicates that the improvement comes from the model's discriminative boundary becoming cleaner, not from moving the operating point.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

**Using only ALT, removing ALO entirely ($\mu=0$ and $\lambda=0$, 35-class, Figure 3 left)**: DSCNN 82.27, MHAttRNN 84.73, ResNet 74.10, Transformer 70.57. Compared to the complete FedKWS-UI's 84.51/84.60/80.50/74.75, the increments from ALO are +2.24 / −0.13 / +6.40 / +4.18. The conclusion is fine-grained: ALT alone can sustain MHAttRNN and even slightly exceed the complete version, and Transformer is basically usable, but it is clearly insufficient on DSCNN and especially ResNet—the paper judges ALO as indispensable based on this (Section 5 Ablation paragraph). This differentiation is very informative: architectures with strong temporal modeling (MHAttRNN) are inherently more robust to user bias, so improvements at the scheduling level are sufficient; purely feedforward architectures (ResNet/DS CNN) absorb bias deeper, requiring explicit adversarial cleaning at the loss function level.

**The role of two hyperparameters within ALO (Figure 3 middle, right)**: Label smoothing coefficient $\mu$ scanned on 12-class DSCNN as {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5}, yielding 86.55 / 87.24 / 87.29 / 87.21 / 86.96—adding label smoothing almost always yields benefits, $\mu \approx 0.2$ is the sweet spot, and the curve is flat (only 0.08 points difference between 0.1 and 0.3), friendly to tuning. Adversarial weight $\lambda$ scanned on 12-class ResNet as {0, 1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1}, yielding 85.29 / 86.07 / 86.23 / 82.79 / 76.99—the effective interval is only one order of magnitude wide, $\lambda=0.001$ is recommended, and at 0.1 it directly loses about 9 points. Combining the two scans gives a manual for using ALO: label smoothing is a broad-spectrum beneficial item, negative distillation is a narrow-spectrum high-sensitivity item.

**Visualization Verification (Figure 4, 35-class)**: First, centrally train a model $\theta_0$ with 93.0% test accuracy; then fine-tune for 20 epochs on data from "low-quality users" (only 100 samples, imbalanced class distribution, i.e., the type of client in the bottom right of Figure 1) and "qualified users" (about 250 samples, relatively balanced class distribution, top right of Figure 1), obtaining $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$; plot the generalization terrain in the interpolation space $\theta_0 + \gamma_1(\theta_1-\theta_0) + \gamma_2(\theta_2-\theta_0)$, $\gamma\in[-0.1, 1.1]$. The left figure shows: moving along the qualified user direction ($\gamma_2$ axis) causes little drop in generalization, while moving along the low-quality user direction ($\gamma_1$ axis) causes rapid collapse—this provides geometric justification for "ALT lets qualified users train more and low-quality users train less." The right figure further applies ALO to low-quality users: using $\theta_1$ as the overfitted teacher to retrain $\hat{\theta}_1$, $\hat{\theta}_1$ itself performs better, and the interpolation terrain along the low-quality direction becomes smoother. Since federated aggregation is essentially a weighted average of multiple local solutions (i.e., a point in the interpolation space), the "deep pits" caused by local updates on the interpolation terrain directly harm aggregation quality—ALO fills the pits, geometrically explaining why it is "beneficial to the aggregation process" (Section 5 Visualization paragraph).

## Main Contributions

1. **Problem Reframing**: Reframes the performance degradation of FedKWS from the broad "non-IID difficulty" to "local models overfitting user-specific information," and provides an analytical framework of triple skew (quantity/label/feature) plus dual erosion (lower layers eroded by feature skew, upper layers by label skew), pointing out that layer-by-layer regularization is structurally unsuited for KWS.
2. **ALO**: Proposes function-level negative distillation adversarial regularization—using a deliberately overfitted private model as a probe for user-specific information to push the global model away from overfitted regions, combined with label smoothing to anchor discriminative signals. This is a new usage of "distillation form, opposite semantics," forming methodological complementarity with the pulling-type regularization of FedProx/FedDyn/MOON.
3. **ALT**: Proposes a data quality-aware local step reallocation mechanism (harmonic mean utility of normalized sample size and class distribution entropy + total conservation), extending FL optimization from the "algorithm level" to the "system scheduling level," a dimension that was blank in previous FedKWS work.
4. **Large-scale Empirical Evidence**: Achieves first place in all 8 settings across 2,234/2,434 real user clients, two task granularities, and four architectures. The maximum improvement on Transformer is +6.35 (72.71→79.06, 12-class), and simultaneously reduces FA (0.23) and FR (2.78) without adjusting thresholds (Table 3); ablation and interpolation visualization verify the necessity and mechanism of the two components respectively.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Doubling of Local Overhead**: ALO requires each client to additionally maintain and train a private model in advance, nearly doubling local storage and computation. For edge-side KWS scenarios that are already constrained by memory budgets, this is a realistic pressure. The private model is not uploaded, so communication does not increase, but the paper does not report specific overhead (training steps, parallel/serial timing with the global model).
- **Lack of Theoretical Guarantees for Negative Distillation**: The paper provides no theoretical analysis of why "moving away from the overfitted teacher" exactly removes user-specific components while preserving discriminative components, only empirical boundaries—the $\lambda$ effective window is only one order of magnitude (1e-4 to 1e-3, Figure 3 right), and at $\lambda=0.1$ it collapses by 9 points, indicating that the signal-to-noise ratio of this pushing-away direction is fragile, and hyperparameter transferability across datasets/architectures is questionable.
- **Prerequisite of Teacher Quality**: The effectiveness of ALO depends on $f_p$ truly overfitting, and "sufficient overfitting" lacks an operational criterion (the text only says "abundant training steps"); if local data is extremely small (e.g., single-digit samples), the private model may not even be able to fit user-specific information, causing the probe itself to fail.
- **$r_k$ is Heuristic**: The combination of harmonic mean and entropy lacks axiomatic derivation. The paper does not perform ablation comparisons of $r_k$ with alternative utilities such as "using only sample size," "using only entropy," or "arithmetic mean," making it impossible to exclude the possibility that other utility measures are equally effective or even superior.
- **ALT Assumes Single-Dimension Controllability**: Step allocation only reflects data quality, ignoring client computing power and availability heterogeneity, which the paper admits is left for future work. Additionally, letting users with large data volumes train more implies the assumption that "more training is harmless," but whether extreme large users would pull the global model towards their sub-distribution is not stress-tested in the paper.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Single Dataset**: All experiments are based on Google Speech Commands, not covering other KWS benchmarks like Hey Snips (the direct comparison object [17] uses Hey Snips), so cross-dataset conclusions are unknown.
- **Extrapolability to Extreme Small Client Scenarios**: The average of 20.4/43.3 samples per client (Table 1) is an extreme setting of "one end per user"; whether the method yields equal benefits in silo-style federated learning (where each institution has sufficient data but skewed distribution) is not reported; intermediate form experiments are missing.
- **Weak Transformer Configuration**: 4 layers, 0.23M parameters (as stated in the paper, compared to 5.4M in [8]), it is questionable whether conclusions about Transformer (worst under federated, largest improvement with FedKWS-UI) can be extrapolated to mainstream scales.
- **Statistical Robustness Not Reported**: No variance or standard deviation from multiple independent repeated experiments is reported; it is impossible to judge whether some small leads (e.g., only +0.28 for 35-class DSCNN, only +0.18 for 12-class MHAttRNN) are significant; the client sampling ratio $|S_t|$ per round is also not reported, making reproduction and computing power accounting difficult.
- **Narrow Coverage of FA/FR**: Only reported for one setting: DSCNN, 12-class (Table 3); false wake-up/false rejection behaviors for other architectures and the 35-class task are unknown.
- **Absence of System Cost**: No quantitative comparison of wall-clock time, total communication, or additional computing power for the private model; for a work highlighting a "system scheduling" contribution (ALT), the lack of end-to-end system cost accounting is a significant gap.
- **Lack of Comparison with Personalization Routes**: The paper's goal is global model generalization, while another main line in the FL community is local personalization (including the authors' own FedPHP [30]); in scenarios where "every user has a different accent," personalization might be a natural solution, but the paper does not discuss the trade-offs between the two routes.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Directions Proposed by the Paper**: Extension to streaming KWS ([6]), and combination with differential privacy [36] to meet stricter privacy requirements (Section 6).
- **Lightweight ALO Teacher**: Use early stopping snapshots, exponential moving average copies, or local models from previous rounds to approximate the overfitted teacher, avoiding double local training; or let the private model retain only the classification head (last layer privatized, echoing the "where to privatize" question in [31]), minimizing probe costs.
- **Multi-dimensional Utility Function**: Incorporate client computing power, online duration, and data timeliness into $r_k$, moving from two-dimensional harmonic mean to multi-dimensional scheduling, aligning with the system reality of cross-device FL.
- **Orthogonal Superposition with FedOpt**: ALO/ALT treat the local side, FedOpt treats the server side; the paper does not report the performance of combining the two, which is an experimental gap that can be filled with low cost.
- **Verification in Real Product Scenarios**: In custom wake-word scenarios, label skew is more extreme than in GSC (most users almost never say non-wake-words), and the mechanism of the class entropy term deserves separate testing; further simulate real pickup distributions by combining simulated far-field/noisy data.
- **Theoretical Makeup**: Establish analysis of "user-specific information separability" for negative distillation (e.g., arguing under feature decoupling or causal representation frameworks that the overfitted teacher mainly carries user-specific components under certain conditions), turning the effective window of $\lambda$ from an empirical rule into a derivable magnitude.
