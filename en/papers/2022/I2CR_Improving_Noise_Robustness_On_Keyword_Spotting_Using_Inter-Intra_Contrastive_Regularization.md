# I2CR: Improving Noise Robustness on Keyword Spotting Using Inter-Intra Contrastive Regularization

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Dianwen Ng, Jia Qi Yip, Tanmay Surana, Zhao Yang, Chong Zhang, Yukun Ma, Chongjia Ni, Eng Siong Chng†, Bin Ma† (Alibaba Group Singapore; School of Computing, National University of Singapore; School of Electronic and Information Engineering, Xi’an Jiaotong University; † denotes co-senior authors)
- **Date**: September 2022 (arXiv:2209.06360v1, submitted 2022-09-14)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.06360
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, noise robustness, contrastive learning, supervised contrastive regularization, data augmentation, low SNR, out-of-domain generalization

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) refers to detecting preset keywords within a continuous speech stream. It serves as the standard trigger entry for personal voice assistants such as Siri, Google Assistant, and Amazon Alexa, where users activate devices hands-free using wake words like "Hey Siri," "Ok Google," or "Alexa." In recent years, a number of high-precision KWS modeling works have emerged (citations [1]-[5], including Keyword Transformer, MatchboxNet, ConvMixer, etc.). However, the vast majority of these works focus on clean recordings or close-talking audio, largely ignoring strong noise environments—specifically scenarios with Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNR) below 0 dB. This neglect is fatal from an engineering perspective: KWS systems are always-on, and users do not select their speaking environment. Taking a voice assistant to the middle of a street, a subway platform, or a moving vehicle means traffic noise will continuously flood into the microphone. The paper asserts that there are few models in the existing literature that can maintain robustness under such noisy conditions.

A deeper pain point is that noise robustness is more stringent for KWS than for general speech tasks: First, KWS models are typically deployed on edge-side small models (the paper explicitly mentions that low-complexity KWS models require a smaller model footprint), leaving no extra capacity to "remember" various noise conditions. Second, false alarms and missed detections directly determine product experience; the degradation of feature embedding quality at low SNR simultaneously increases both types of errors.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper’s criticism of existing approaches focuses on three lines:

- **Multi-conditioning training treats symptoms rather than the root cause.** The conventional approach to building noise-robust KWS involves repeatedly conditioning the model on noisy data across various SNR levels. However, the paper points out that without additional noise augmentation computation, feature representations remain severely affected by low-SNR noise. Multi-conditioning training heavily relies on the network to "adapt and generalize" to the specific set of SNR noise environments selected during training. In other words, the model learns "how to remedy after seeing these specific noises" rather than "how to ignore the noise itself at the representation level." Consequently, performance inevitably degrades for noise types and SNRs outside the training range, a problem exacerbated by smaller model footprints. The fundamental design flaw here is that supervised cross-entropy only requires "classifiability," not "noise-free representations"; the network can completely reduce training loss by memorizing noise conditions.
- **Two-stage contrastive pre-training is unsuitable for KWS scenarios.** The speech recognition community has used contrastive learning for noise-robust representations: SPIRAL [8] and Wav2vec-switch [9] employ contrastive learning with different noisy views during the pre-training phase, encouraging the model to learn context-aware representations robust to noise, followed by fine-tuning on labeled data. However, this approach requires large-scale external unlabeled corpora for pre-training—the paper explicitly states that no other large external datasets are available, and two-stage training "would not be as meaningful as [8][9]," thus it is not considered. Furthermore, self-supervised contrastive learning itself faces optimization difficulties (the paper points out dimensional collapse) and training resource overheads.
- **Traditional supervised learning lacks geometric constraints on latent representations.** The paper makes a clear diagnosis at the beginning of the methodology: conventional supervised learning only focuses on associative patterns for distinguishing multiple classes, offering no additional constraints on latent representations to prevent the model from absorbing nuisance factors such as gender, speaking style, or background noise. The root cause of performance degradation in noisy KWS is that these nuisance features are not suppressed—background noise can dominate the embedding space, squeezing class information to the margins. Contrastive regularization research in the image domain (domain generalization [10][11][12], semi-supervised pseudo-label contrastive regularization [13]) has proven that "pulling same-class samples from different domains closer in the embedding space" leads to better domain generalization. However, no one has brought this idea to KWS noise robustness; the only related work, CLAR [14], combines self-supervised contrastive loss with cross-entropy for KWS but is only validated in clean settings, with positive pairs consisting solely of two augmented views of the same sample.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved

The engineering problem the paper aims to answer is: **Can we make small-model KWS stronger on noises seen during training and robust to unseen noise types and SNRs, solely by modifying the training objective, without changing the model architecture, adding any inference overhead, or relying on large-scale unlabeled pre-training corpora?** This breaks down into three sub-challenges: (1) How to transform existing label information in supervised training into resources for contrastive positive pairs, allowing contrastive constraints and cross-entropy to coexist in a single-stage end-to-end manner rather than two stages; (2) When training from scratch, network weights are randomly initialized, and early instance representations are nearly random vectors; it is extremely difficult to impose cross-sample similarity constraints at this stage, so how to stabilize training; (3) How to design augmented views so that contrastive learning truly aligns "noise invariance" rather than aligning augmentation artifacts. To the authors' knowledge, this is the first work using contrastive learning to address KWS noise robustness.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall framework (Fig. 3) is very simple: a standard supervised classifier (encoder + classification head), with a contrastive regularization term added to the cross-entropy loss during training. The regularization term completely disappears during inference, and the model structure is identical to the baseline. This design motivation of "decoupling the regularizer from the architecture" is one of the paper’s core selling points—the benefits must come from the training signal rather than architectural changes to claim generality across any backbone.

To verify backbone independence, the paper selected three feature encoders for experiments: EfficientNet-B0 [20], ResNet-18 [21], and Keyword Transformer (KWT-1) [1]. The first two are convolutional encoders, which are SOTA in a wide range of tasks such as image and audio event classification; KWT-1 is a transformer architecture specifically designed for keyword spotting. This selection implies a capacity spectrum: the paper later admits that ResNet-18 is the largest among the three (thus the strongest baseline), while KWT has the smallest footprint (thus the weakest at low SNR). The logic of this selection is to allow direct observation of whether "regularization benefits depend on model capacity."

The input features are 64-dimensional log Mel filter banks (FBanks), with a 25 ms window length and 10 ms frame shift. The resolution is fixed at 1 second, resulting in a 98×64 time-frequency map; utterances shorter than 1 second are zero-padded on the right, and those longer are cropped. This is the standard configuration on GSC, ensuring comparability with existing works.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The total loss function is a weighted sum of two terms:

$$L = L_{CE} + \alpha L_{I2CR}$$

where $L_{CE}$ is the supervised cross-entropy, $L_{I2CR}$ is the contrastive regularization term, and $\alpha$ is the regularization weight. The contrastive term is:

$$L_{I2CR} = \sum_{i \in I} \frac{1}{|P(i)|} \sum_{p \in P(i)} -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_i, z_p)/\tau)}{\sum_{n \in P(i)} \exp(\text{sim}(z_i, z_n)/\tau)}$$

where $P(i)$ is the set of indices of all positive pairs for anchor $i$, $\text{sim}(z_i, z_p)$ is the dot product of normalized vectors $z_i$ and $z_p$ (i.e., cosine similarity), and $\tau$ is the temperature parameter (the paper does not report its value). Formally, this is the notation for Supervised Contrastive Loss (SupCon, Khosla et al. [16]).

Translated into action: for each anchor in the batch, the numerator is the similarity between the anchor and a specific same-class sample, and the denominator is the sum of similarities between the anchor and all samples participating in the contrast within the view. Minimizing this negative log-likelihood means **pulling same-class samples to the nearest positions and pushing other samples away within the batch view**. The term $1/|P(i)|$ averages over multiple positive samples for each anchor, preventing classes with many samples in the batch from dominating the gradient. The key difference from the self-supervised version (Fig. 1’s N-pair views: anchor + single augmented positive sample + negatives from the batch or memory queue) lies in the construction source of $P(i)$—this is the innovation of the next section.

Why this constraint is noise-resistant: Noisy augmentation creates multiple views of "the same semantic content + different noise realizations." If these views are required to cluster in the embedding space, the only stable solution for the model is to encode the common factor (keyword content) into the representation and squeeze out the noise-varying factor (the noise itself). Geometrically, this forces class clusters to be more compact and inter-class separation to be greater, preventing noise from tearing apart class clusters via continuous drift.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Inter-Intra Dual-Source Positive Sample Construction

Conventional contrastive schemes use only one positive pair: augmenting the anchor with different perturbations once (the paper calls this intra-view contrast, referring to the practice of two views of the same sample, see citation [18]). The paper points out that this is insufficient and provides a two-step reasoning:

- **Problems with using only intra-view**: Different augmentations of the same sample share several unfavorable factors—typical examples include speaker style. These factors remain unchanged in both views, so contrastive learning cannot distinguish "what should remain invariant." Consequently, nuisance attributes like speaker style are encoded into the embedding, introducing irrelevant bias.
- **Motivation for introducing inter-view**: The essential goal of the method is to teach the model to encode basic features that contain "no attributes other than class information." Therefore, positive pairs should come from **different samples of different speakers within the same class group**—the only common factor between two different recordings is the word itself, forcing the model to use class information as the basis for clustering. Since labels are freely available in supervised training, positive pairs (inter-intra view) can be formed directly in the minibatch based on identical target labels, requiring no external resources. Fig. 2 illustrates this pairing framework.

The paper also cites [19] to support the judgment that "the more positive sample references, the better the model’s inference of class representations." The deep value of this design lies in upgrading contrastive learning from "instance discrimination" to "class discrimination": intra-view is responsible for noise invariance (views of the same sample with different noises must still be similar), while inter-view is responsible for removing redundancy (same-class samples from different speakers must also be similar). Together, they compress the representation into the subspace of "pure class factors."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Curriculum Warm-up Scheduling for Regularization Strength $\alpha$

The paper frankly admits a training difficulty: inter-view contrast is extremely difficult to impose in the early stages of training. The reason is that the model trains from scratch with randomly initialized weights, and instance vector representations are essentially random; at this point, forcibly imposing a constraint to "maximize the similarity of two random vectors"—even if they come from the same class—is non-trivial, leading to chaotic gradient directions.

The solution is a simple but crucial schedule: **set $\alpha = 0$ for the first epoch** (pure cross-entropy warm-up), after which $\alpha$ increases at a linear rate (current epoch number / total epoch number), with the maximum value truncated at 0.5. The rationale for this design is: cross-entropy first quickly organizes the representations onto a manifold that is "roughly separable," causing same-class samples to naturally begin to cluster; the contrastive constraint then performs geometric refinement on this already structured manifold, so the two no longer conflict. The truncation at 0.5 prevents the regularization weight from overpowering the supervised signal itself. Furthermore, the paper emphasizes that to ensure fairness between the baseline (pure supervised) and the regularized version, **all hyperparameters for all experiments are kept fixed**—the baseline also uses the same augmentation and training configuration.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Augmentation Pipeline Design for Contrastive Representations

The paper cites [22] (What makes good views for contrastive learning?) to emphasize that the effectiveness of contrastive representation learning heavily depends on view design. Therefore, four augmentations are carefully combined, and **every augmentation view undergoes the full set**:

1. **Noise Augmentation**: A random segment of noise is selected from a noise dataset (FSD50K) and added to the keyword audio. The SNR is randomly determined between (-10, 30) dB. The training noise environments are limited to three common outdoor scenes (traffic, metro, car), with an overall training SNR range of -10 dB to 30 dB. This forms the basis for creating "same content, different noise" positive pairs.
2. **Time-Frequency Masking (SpecAugment [23])**: Randomly select small blocks of the time-frequency map and set the signal values of several frequency channels and several time steps to zero. The number of masks in both time and frequency directions is fixed at 2, with maximum mask sizes of 25 (frames) for time and 7 (channels) for frequency.
3. **Time Shifting**: The audio is randomly shifted forward or backward. Samples shifted out of the end are wrapped around to the beginning (circular shift). The shift magnitude and direction are randomly determined within (-100 ms, 100 ms).
4. **Speed Perturbation [24]**: Change the speech speed via resampling, using the `speed` function in PyTorch Sox, with the rate randomly varying between 90% and 110% of the original speed.

The intent of this combination: Noise augmentation provides the "noise perturbation" desired by the contrastive objective; time-frequency masking and shifting are responsible for erasing local time-frequency shortcuts (preventing the model from relying on specific frequency bands or time positions for decisions); speed perturbation expands the diversity of temporal deformations within the class. Together, these four make the constraint of "similarity across views" satisfiable only through semantic content.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Contrastive Multi-conditioning Training**: Multi-conditioning training leaves noise robustness to the network to implicitly absorb from massive noisy replicas, where representations may still be dominated by noise; I2CR uses explicit geometric constraints (intra-class clustering) to directly suppress noise factors and does not optimize for specific noise types, thus enabling extrapolation to unseen noises and SNRs (the experimental designs in Table III and Table IV are intended to verify this).
- **Contrastive Two-stage Self-supervised Pre-training** (SPIRAL [8], Wav2vec-switch [9], and the series [6][16][17]): Does not require large-scale unlabeled pre-training corpora; single-stage end-to-end. Additionally, the presence of supervised signals provides strong guidance for optimization, which the paper believes can counteract the dimensional collapse common in self-supervised contrastive learning, stabilizing training.
- **Contrastive CLAR [14]**: CLAR combines self-supervised contrastive loss with cross-entropy, with positive pairs consisting only of "two augmented views of the same sample," and is only validated on clean KWS; I2CR expands positive pairs to "same-class different samples + self-augmented views," with the main battlefield being noisy KWS, and additionally addresses the scheduling problem of imposing inter-view constraints during training from scratch.
- **Contrastive Image Domain Contrastive Regularization** ([10][11][12] domain generalization, [13] semi-supervised): The underlying idea is the same (pulling same-class samples from parallel domains closer), but applying it to KWS requires redesigning noise augmentation views and $\alpha$ warm-up, representing a migration rather than a direct copy.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

- **Speech Data**: Google Speech Commands V2 [25], consisting of 105,000 utterances, 35 words, each 1 second long, sampled at 16 kHz. Two common subsets are used: the full 35-class set, and a 10-class subset ("up", "down", "left", "right", "yes", "no", "on", "off", "go", "stop"). The train/val/test split follows the 80/10/10 division from [25][26], facilitating comparison and reproduction.
- **Noise Data**: Extracted from FSD50K (Freesound) [27], an open, manually annotated sound event dataset. 905 instances are extracted from the official train set to form the training noise pool, and 487 samples are taken from the official eval set for testing—this means that even for "in-domain" evaluation, the noise instances used for testing were not present during training; only the noise types (traffic/metro/car) were seen. Out-of-domain evaluation uses the background noise provided with Google Speech Commands.
- **Training Noise Conditions**: Three outdoor noises (traffic, metro, car), with SNRs ranging from -10 dB to 30 dB.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric is **top-1 classification accuracy binned by noise type × SNR** (as shown in Tables I-IV). The rationale for this choice: First, accuracy is the common currency on GSC, allowing direct comparison with [25][26] and existing architectures in Table V; second, reporting accuracy binned by SNR is equivalent to drawing a robustness curve, which can expose collapse points at low SNRs, providing much more information than a single average accuracy. The paper does not report false alarm/missed detection rates, AUC, or wake-up operating points commonly used in KWS engineering, nor does it report parameter counts, FLOPs, latency, or training overhead (model size is only described qualitatively as "largest/smallest"). The introduction mentions that this method encourages faster training, but training time figures are not reported.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

**In-domain Noise Main Results (Table I, 35 classes; Table II, 10 classes)**. The three rows of ablation are Base (pure supervised), Intra Reg (using only same-sample augmented views as positive pairs), and I2CR Reg (inter-class + intra-class). The test noises are traffic/metro/car at -10/-5/0/20 dB. Key points:

- At the baseline level, ResNet-18 is consistently the strongest across both subsets, achieving at least 84% accuracy for 35 classes and at least 87% for 10 classes at -10 dB (Table I/II). The paper attributes this to it being the largest model among the three; KWT-1 has the smallest footprint, with the lowest accuracy of about 74% or above for both subsets at -10 dB (e.g., 0.743 for car noise in Table II).
- I2CR provides **consistently positive gains** across all three backbones, three noise types, four SNR levels, and both subsets, with gains amplifying as SNR decreases. Typical numbers (Table I, 35 classes, -10 dB): EfficientNet-B0 traffic noise 0.840→0.867 (+2.7 points), ResNet-18 car noise 0.841→0.862 (+2.1 points), KWT metro noise 0.807→0.825 (+1.8 points). Gains are larger for the 10-class subset (Table II): KWT car noise at -10 dB improves from 0.743 to 0.782, which the paper calls "up to a 4% absolute improvement"; ResNet-18 car noise at -10 dB improves from 0.872 to 0.904 (+3.2 points).
- Gains narrow at high SNRs: At 20 dB, improvements are mostly between 0 and 0.3 points (e.g., Table I ResNet-18 traffic 20 dB 0.971→0.974). The reason is straightforward: at 20 dB, the noise energy itself is weak, and nuisance factors are few, so the geometric constraint has little to clean up; the method is positioned as a low-SNR enhancer.
- Convergence levels: The 10-class model reaches about 98% at 20 dB, and the 35-class model reaches about 97% (as stated in the paper).

**Beyond Training SNR Range (Table III, -30/-20/-15 dB and clean)**. All models collapse significantly at -30 dB (the 35-class KWT baseline drops to only 0.196), with the 35-class set suffering the heaviest degradation; however, the I2CR version is significantly better—the paper reports average absolute gains at -30 dB: 4% for the 10-class subset and 2.4% for the 35-class subset. Gains become more obvious as the SNR approaches the training range; for example, EfficientNet-B0 10-class at -15 dB improves from 0.754 to 0.806 (+5.2 points). Worth highlighting separately is the clean column: the model never saw clean audio during training, yet the I2CR version is comprehensively better, with KWT 10-class clean improving from 0.945 to 0.968 (+2.3 points). This indicates that the constraint learned a purer class representation rather than "overfitting to noise."

**Out-of-Domain Noise (Table IV, GSC background noise, SNR range same as training)**. All models drop in performance on unseen noise types (as expected, since they were not conditioned on these noises), but I2CR is consistently superior. The paper reports average absolute gains at -10 dB: 5.7% for the 10-class subset and 3.6% for the 35-class subset. The single largest example is KWT 10-class at -10 dB: 0.482→0.577, an absolute improvement of 9.5 points—the smallest model benefits the most from out-of-domain noise regularization. Even at 20 dB, gains remain between 0.5 and 1.3 points.

**Comparison with Clean Domain SOTA (Table V, 35-class clean test)**. The comparison targets are existing architectures "trained and tested on clean data": TDNN(+xvector)-SpeechBrain [29] 0.974, Conformer-ESPnet [30] 0.975, Audio Spectrogram Transformer [2] 0.981, Branchformer [31] 0.973. The I2CR models in this paper, which never saw the clean domain, achieve EfficientNet-B0 0.974, ResNet-18 0.977—slightly lower than AST but comparable to or better than the rest. The paper explicitly states this training setting difference in the table notes (others trained/tested on clean domain, this paper never saw clean domain); the implication is that contrastive regularization forces the model to encode higher-quality class representations, so "not seeing clean data" does not put it at a disadvantage.

**Visual Evidence of Representation Quality (Fig. 4, t-SNE)**. The latent representations from the bottleneck layer before the classifier softmax are taken from the KWT 10-class test set under 0 dB noise and projected to 2D using t-SNE [28]. The class clusters for I2CR are more prominent, have clearer boundaries, less overlap, and more concentrated sample points within clusters compared to the baseline. The paper argues that I2CR learned richer class representation embeddings insensitive to interfering noise—this is a direct visual verification of the mechanism claim that "geometric constraints change representation structure."

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The core ablation is embedded in the three rows of Table I/II (Base vs Intra Reg vs I2CR Reg):

- **Intra-view regularization itself is effective**: Using only "two augmented views of the same sample" as contrastive regularization already surpasses the pure supervised baseline at low SNRs (Table I EfficientNet-B0 traffic -10 dB: 0.840→0.858; Table II KWT traffic -10 dB: 0.765→0.793). This confirms the value of view consistency constraints, consistent with CLAR’s conclusions in clean settings.
- **Further improvement with the addition of inter-view**: I2CR surpasses intra-only in almost all settings (the same two examples continue to rise to 0.867 and 0.805). Mechanism explanation: The two views of the intra positive pair share nuisance factors like speaker style, allowing the model to take shortcuts via "instance-invariant" features, resulting in incomplete inter-class separation; introducing same-class different samples leaves only class information as the common factor, forcing the constraint to act on the correct subspace.
- **Ablations not performed**: The $\alpha$ scheduling strategy (e.g., constant $\alpha$ or no warm-up), temperature $\tau$ values, and the impact of batch size on the number of positive pairs are not reported in ablation studies—the paper only provides design motivation for $\alpha$, and does not even give specific values for $\tau$. Strictly speaking, although the gain of I2CR relative to intra-only is consistent, the magnitude is small (mostly around 1 point), and without variance reports, this incremental gain requires cautious interpretation.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed I2CR (Inter-Intra Contrastive Regularization)**, which, to the authors' knowledge, is the first work using contrastive learning to address KWS noise robustness. Methodologically, it constructs $P(i)$ of the supervised contrastive loss as the union of "same-class samples in the batch + self-augmented views," combined with a curriculum schedule of $\alpha=0$ for the first epoch and linearly increasing $\alpha$ truncated at 0.5, making cross-sample contrastive constraints stable and feasible when training from scratch.
2. **Proved consistent backbone-independent gains**: Three architectures (EfficientNet-B0, ResNet-18, KWT-1) covering convolutional and transformer families, and large, medium, and small capacities, all achieved consistent improvements across three noises, four SNR levels, and two subsets. This shows it is a general training strategy rather than a specific architecture trick; moreover, it incurs zero additional overhead and zero architectural changes during inference.
3. **Validated generalization to unseen conditions**: For out-of-domain noises (GSC background noise) and extreme SNRs (-30/-20/-15 dB) never conditioned during training, I2CR provides average absolute gains of 5.7%/3.6% (10/35 classes, out-of-domain noise) at -10 dB, and 4%/2.4% (10/35 classes, out-of-range SNR) at -30 dB; models that never saw clean audio also comprehensively surpass baselines on clean data and match SOTA trained on clean domains (Table V).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Collapse at extreme SNRs remains**: At -30 dB, the best I2CR model achieves only 0.411 (EfficientNet-B0, 10-class, Table III), and for 35 classes, all models are below 0.33. Contrastive regularization acts at the representation level, suppressing noise factors in the embedding; when the speech itself is submerged at the signal level, representation learning has no material to work with. Its capability boundary is "representation denoising," not "signal enhancement."
- **Diminishing returns at high SNRs**: Improvements at 20 dB are generally only 0–0.3 points (Table I/II); the method is essentially a low-SNR specific enhancer, so do not expect it to improve performance in quiet environments.
- **Positive pair construction depends on batch label distribution**: Inter-view positive samples all come from same-label samples within the minibatch. With a batch size of 128 for 35 classes, this means an average of only about 3-4 samples per class. The number of positive pairs is strongly constrained by batch composition and class imbalance. The paper does not perform batch size sensitivity analysis; this implicit constraint would be more severe on larger vocabularies.
- **Training overhead is not quantified**: Contrastive loss requires computing the pairwise similarity matrix of samples in the batch, with computational cost growing quadratically with batch size; the paper’s claim of "no additional complexity" only holds for the inference architecture, while the additional overhead on the training side is not reported.
- **Does not solve capacity gaps**: I2CR provides similar magnitudes of gain for the three backbones, but KWT-1’s absolute accuracy remains the lowest under all conditions—modifying the training objective cannot replace model capacity. The level of 74%→78% for small edge models at -10 dB is still far from the accuracy required for product-level wake-up.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Only classification accuracy is reported**, without false alarm/missed detection rates, AUC, or operating point curves that are truly relevant to wake-word systems; there is an entire layer of engineering involving detection windows and post-processing smoothing between GSC’s 1-second isolated word classification and streaming continuous detection that is not addressed.
- **Single noise model**: Only additive noise is used, without reverberation, far-field, or channel distortion—the paper itself cites ConvMixer [4], which is designed for far-field noisy KWS, while I2CR’s experiments do not cover this more difficult main line.
- **No statistical significance control**: There are no repetitions with multiple random seeds and no variance reports; gains of 0.1-0.3 points at 20 dB are difficult to distinguish from noise without variance information.
- **Key hyperparameter ablations are missing**: There are no control experiments for $\alpha$ warm-up scheduling, temperature $\tau$, or batch composition sensitivity; there is also a lack of horizontal comparison with "two-stage contrastive pre-training" (even a small-scale comparison under resource constraints) or stronger front-end enhancement methods.
- **Limited comparability of Table V**: The models in this paper never saw the clean domain, while the comparison methods were trained and tested on the clean domain. Although the authors honestly state this in the table notes, this is essentially an asymmetric comparison, serving only as evidence that it "does not drop in performance" rather than evidence of surpassing. Model parameter counts/FLOPs are not reported in the text, and the qualitative description of "largest/smallest footprint" cannot be quantitatively verified.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Complementarity with pre-training paradigms**: When external unlabeled corpora are available, perform noise-augmented contrastive pre-training first, then fine-tune with I2CR, to test whether the gains from single-stage regularization and two-stage pre-training are additive.
- **Expansion of acoustic conditions**: Introduce reverberation and far-field simulation, multi-channel front-ends, to push "additive noise robustness" to real smart home and in-vehicle acoustic scenarios.
- **Deployment-oriented evaluation**: Report false alarm/missed detection operating points, latency, and memory usage within a streaming KWS framework, grounding the conclusions in edge-side constraints.
- **Semi-supervised extension**: Drawing on the pseudo-label contrastive regularization idea from [13], incorporate large amounts of unlabeled noisy speech into positive pair construction to alleviate dependence on labeled batch samples.
- **Systematic hyperparameter study**: Investigate the form of $\alpha$ scheduling, temperature $\tau$, and the impact of batch size and class balance on positive pair quality; as well as hard negative mining for confusable word pairs (homophones, near-homophones), concentrating contrastive pressure on the true confusion boundaries.

## Terminology Quick Reference

- **Contrastive learning**: A representation learning method that pulls positive pairs closer and pushes negative pairs apart; positive pairs are typically two augmented views of the same sample.
- **Positive/Negative pairs**: Sample pairs requiring similar representations / sample pairs requiring distant representations; negative samples are often taken from the mini-batch or memory queue.
- **Intra-view / Inter-view**: Contrast between augmented views of the same sample / Contrast between different samples of the same class; I2CR’s $P(i)$ includes both.
- **Multi-conditioning training**: Training the model on noisy data at multiple SNR levels, the traditional approach for noise-robust KWS.
- **Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)**: The energy ratio of signal to noise, expressed in decibels; lower SNR means stronger noise; -10 dB means noise is 10 dB higher than speech.
- **Supervised Contrastive Loss (SupCon) [16]**: A contrastive loss that uses labels to treat all same-class samples in the batch as positives; the formal source of the I2CR objective function.
- **Dimensional collapse**: A failure mode of self-supervised contrastive learning where representations collapse to a low-dimensional manifold, losing discriminative power.
- **t-SNE [28]**: An algorithm for projecting high-dimensional embeddings to 2D for visualization; Fig. 4 uses it to display class cluster structure.
- **SpecAugment [23]**: A data augmentation method that performs time/frequency masking on time-frequency maps.
- **GSC V2 / FSD50K**: Google Speech Commands Version 2 (105,000 utterances, 1 second, 35-word command dataset) / Freesound’s open sound event dataset; used in this paper as sources for speech and noise, respectively.
