# CaTT-KWS: A Multi-stage Customized Keyword Spotting Framework based on Cascaded Transducer-Transformer

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Zhanheng Yang, Sining Sun, Jin Li, Xiaoming Zhang, Xiong Wang, Long Ma, Lei Xie (Audio Speech and Language Processing Group, School of Computer Science, Northwestern Polytechnical University; Tencent Technologies Co., Ltd., Joint Work)
- **Date**: July 2022 (arXiv:2207.01267v1, submitted July 4, 2022)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.01267
- **Keywords**: customized keyword spotting, transducer, transformer, multi-stage detection, force alignment, multi-task learning, false alarm suppression

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The task of Keyword Spotting (KWS) is to detect predefined keywords from a continuous audio stream, serving as the core entry point for hands-free experiences in smart speakers, in-car assistants, and wearable devices. The specific scenario targeted by this paper is more stringent than "single wake-word" detection: command-control and privacy-sensitive applications require always-on local KWS running on the edge side, simultaneously detecting dozens or even hundreds of command words online. A typical solution cascades a wake-word detection module with a window-based command recognition module; once the wake-word is triggered, another keyword detection module activates only within a short time window to accept user commands. Such always-on systems have hard requirements for three things simultaneously: the model must be small (due to limited edge-side resources), the response must be fast (streaming low latency), and it must be robust against both False Alarms (FA) and False Rejections (FR). The first two are old constraints in this field, but the third is the target of this paper: when the number of keywords expands from one to dozens or hundreds, false alarms become extremely difficult to control, and the cost of each false trigger is the system executing commands or lighting up interactions "unprompted" when the user is not speaking, which directly damages the user experience.

Why does an increase in the number of keywords make false alarm control difficult? Mechanistically, it is easy to understand: the larger the command set, the more valid paths exist in the decoding search space, and the higher the prior probability that any unrelated speech (broadcast, chat, driving noise) "happens" to enter a specific command path. A single wake-word system has only one target path that can be falsely triggered, while a system with twenty-nine commands theoretically amplifies this opportunity for false triggering by nearly thirty times. The abstract explicitly lists "severe false alarms when detecting dozens to hundreds of keywords" as the core problem to be solved, and the experimental section validates this using a car dataset with twenty-nine Mandarin commands (Section 3.1).

Another long-standing pain point is customization cost. Traditional neural network KWS methods with fixed vocabularies require collecting training data and training models for specific keyword sets. Adding a new command to the system means a new round of data collection and model retraining, which is almost unacceptable under the pace of product iteration. The ideal state is: adding new keywords requires no additional data collection, no model retraining, and can go online just by changing a configuration.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing work into three types and points out their deficiencies one by one:

**Neural network methods with fixed vocabularies.** Early small-model KWS based on DNN/CNN/attention treated keyword recognition as a closed-set classification problem. Performance relied on training data collected for specific vocabularies, requiring retraining when the vocabulary changed (citations [1]-[7]). These methods are mature in single wake-word scenarios but cannot meet the customization needs of "adding words at any time" in command word scenarios.

**Bias customization routes based on transducer.** RNN-T is naturally streaming with low latency, has achieved success in speech recognition, and can use sub-word modeling units like phonemes, opening up space for keyword customization. Existing work mainly biases the search towards predefined keyword transcriptions using attention bias: He et al. ([12]) used an attention mechanism to bias the search process; Liu et al. ([13]) further improved attention-based bias and added auxiliary losses during training; Tian et al. ([14]) explored CTC joint training, stateless prediction networks, and various training data configuration strategies to avoid overfitting in transducer KWS. However, this route has two clear gaps: first, previous research basically only considered wake-word scenarios targeting a single wake-word at runtime, and command recognition scenarios detecting multiple keywords simultaneously have not been explored; second, transducer methods have high wake-word rates, but false alarm problems are equally prominent in the literature ([13][14]), and the problem is further amplified in command scenarios facing dozens to hundreds of keywords.

**Multi-Level Detection (MLD) statistical verification methods.** Multi-level strategies have been adopted previously ([13][17][18][19]): the first level is a lightweight always-on detector, and after detecting candidates, they are sent to subsequent levels for verification. The MLD method most relevant to this paper (Liu et al., [13]) is designed as follows: the detection level calculates posterior probabilities and confidence within a sliding window, ignoring phoneme order, to detect keywords with minimal computational cost; subsequent verification levels calculate edit distance probability confidence and approximate likelihood confidence for further verification. The paper's criticism hits the nail on the head: MLD is a **statistical verification method that relies solely on transducer outputs**—all confidence scores are derived from the same transducer output, with a single source of evidence. If the transducer itself produces systematic bias for a certain audio segment (e.g., overestimating phoneme posteriors for noise frames), all subsequent statistics will go wrong together, because they are not independent second opinions but different transformations of the same output. The ceiling of verification from a single information source is the fundamental reason why FA cannot be suppressed in this route.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the engineering problem this paper aims to answer is: how to build a multi-keyword simultaneous detection edge-side customized KWS framework that simultaneously satisfies four mutually constraining conditions—(1) command recognition accuracy must be high, and FR-caused "user shouted but no response" cannot significantly deteriorate; (2) false alarm rate must be suppressed to an extremely low level (the paper finally achieves 0.13 times per hour, a reduction of more than 90% relative to the detection level); (3) zero data collection and zero model retraining for new keywords, only changing the search graph; (4) the overall model has approximately 3.8M parameters, fitting into edge-side devices. The tension among these four conditions lies in: the most direct way to suppress FA is to tighten thresholds, but that harms accuracy; the most direct way to add verification modules is to stack models, but that harms size and latency. The paper's solution is to use "cascading + shared encoder + event-driven computation" to resolve this simultaneously—the verification modules run only briefly when triggered, and the always-on overhead is only the first level.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

CaTT-KWS (Cascaded Transducer-Transformer KWS) consists of three modules: a transducer-based keyword detector, a force alignment module based on frame-level phoneme predictors, and a transformer-based decoding verifier. The overall system is shaped into a multi-task learning framework, with the encoder shared among all modules (Figure 1). The data flow is: the streaming transducer detects keyword candidates in the audio stream and provides rough time boundaries; the force alignment module uses the frame-level posteriors of the phoneme predictor for Viterbi alignment, completing the first round of keyword verification and correcting time boundaries; the transformer decoder receives the encoder outputs for the corresponding candidate intervals and completes final verification using beam search.

The "whys" behind several architectural decisions are worth breaking down one by one:

**Why use transducer instead of a closed-set classifier for the first level.** Besides streaming low latency, the key lies in the ownership of the customization mechanism: the transducer's modeling units are phoneme-level sub-words, so keyword constraints can be entirely placed in the WFST search graph on the decoding side (composition of lexicon L and grammar G), without changing model weights. The first level can therefore be "intentionally" made high-recall: the paper explicitly states that this stage "tries to recall more cases on keywords, but inevitably produces a large number of false alarms" (Section 2.2), leaving accuracy issues to the subsequent levels. This is the standard division of labor in cascaded systems—the recall of the first level is the ceiling of the system's recall; it is better to relax it than to cut off true commands at the first gate.

**Why verification evidence must be independent of transducer output.** This is a direct response to the single-source evidence defect of MLD. Force alignment uses the frame-level posteriors of the phoneme predictor (an independent linear head attached to the shared encoder), and the transformer final review uses another set of self-attention parameters—both do not share parameters with the transducer's joint network, have different inductive biases, and have uncorrelated error patterns. Only when all three lines of evidence (transducer decoding path, phoneme predictor alignment likelihood, transformer rescor-ing) point to "is a keyword" is it triggered, naturally compressing FA exponentially.

**Why share the encoder.** Size and training efficiency. Detection, alignment, and final review use the same acoustic representations, so the encoder does not need to be copied three times; multi-task supervision (transcription loss + frame-level phoneme cross-entropy + transformer loss) acts on a single encoder simultaneously, also serving as regularization.

**Why verification modules can be relaxed without fear of computational power.** The latter two levels are event-driven: only brief segments triggered by the first level flow through alignment and transformer; for most wall-clock time, there is no trigger, and the always-on computation is only the tiny transducer of the first level. This explains why the transformer can occupy a place in the final overall budget of 3.8M.

Model configuration details (Section 3.2): Input is 40-dimensional PNCC features, 25ms window length, 10ms frame shift. The shared encoder consists of a temporal downsampling layer with a rate of 4, a 128-dimensional LSTM layer, and six 512-dimensional FSMN layers (with 320-dimensional linear projection); the left context for all FSMN layers is 8, and the right context per layer is [2,2,1,2,2,1]. The transducer decoder is a one-dimensional causal convolution with kernel width 2 (i.e., a stateless prediction network, [23]); the joint network is a single-layer feedforward network with hyperbolic tangent activation and 100 units; the output is 210 context-independent (CI) phonemes plus blank. The phoneme predictor has only one linear layer plus softmax. The transformer decoder consists of several 512-dimensional self-attention blocks (default 1 block, the effect of block number is explored in Section 3.4). The overall has approximately 3.8M parameters. This configuration follows the author team's previous tiny transducer ([11], ICASSP 2021), which is itself a compression and anti-overfitting design for edge-side deployment—DFSMN encoder plus causal convolution stateless prediction network is an extension of the cFSMN small model route in the transducer era.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Multi-task joint training loss (Equation 1):**

$$L = \alpha L_{Transducer} + \beta L_{CE} + \gamma L_{Transformer}$$

The paper empirically sets α=1.0, β=0.8, γ=0.5 (Section 2.1). The logic of weight allocation: the transducer loss is the main task, dominating detection capability, so it has the highest weight; frame-level phoneme cross-entropy CE is dense supervision—every frame has phoneme labels, whereas the transducer loss only carries gradient signals at the boundaries of emitted tokens; dense frame-level labels can accelerate encoder convergence and regularize encoder training (the paper's original words: predicting frame-level phoneme labels can "accelerate and regularize encoder training"), so it is given 0.8; the transformer loss is a verification auxiliary task, 0.5. Two sets of labels are required during training: transcription text and frame-level phoneme alignment, i.e., generate frame-level alignment using an external process first, then train jointly (the paper does not report the specific generation method of alignment labels, nor details on training epochs, optimizers, etc.).

**Decoding graph composition (Equation 2):**

$$LG = \min(\det(L \circ G))$$

The lexicon WFST (L) maps phoneme strings to words, and the grammar WFST (G) is the grammar network of the predefined command set. After composing the two, determinization (det) and minimization (min) are performed to obtain the final decoding graph. During inference, the transducer uses greedy search, and only the posterior probabilities of non-blank outputs are sent to the WFST decoder (i.e., the "skip blank" technique in literature [11]—skipping the dominant blank emissions, searching only for valid tokens), using the token passing algorithm to find the most likely triggered keyword and provide a rough time boundary for the keyword. The secret of customization lies entirely in this equation: changing the keyword set is equivalent to changing a G graph and recomposing it; the acoustic model is completely unaware of the vocabulary change.

**Force alignment confidence (Equation 3):**

$$S_1 = -\frac{\sum_{l \in f, t \in T} \log(p_l^t)}{T}$$

where f is the result of frame-level force alignment, $p_l^t$ is the posterior of the phoneme predictor for label l at frame t, and T is the precise time span obtained in this stage. Essentially, this is the **average frame-level negative log-likelihood** over the alignment interval—dividing by T performs length normalization, making S1 comparable between keyword candidates of different lengths. S1 is compared with a preset threshold τ (experimentally set to 1.5); passing if below the threshold. Why normalization is necessary: command word lengths vary from six or seven phonemes to over ten; without normalization, the likelihood sum for longer words is naturally larger, leading to systematic discrimination.

**Transformer final review confidence (Equation 4):**

$$S_2 = -\sum_{l \in B} \log(p_l)$$

where B is the phoneme sequence triggered in the beam. Note that S2 is a raw sum, without dividing by length—this is not an oversight: beam search is forcibly truncated at step M (M is the phoneme sequence length of the candidate keyword), so all hypotheses participating in comparison have exactly length M, making the sum naturally length-comparable, and the normalization factor is a constant, not affecting ranking. S2 is compared with a preset threshold υ (experimentally set to 5); only if smaller is it finally triggered. The decision logic is actually two gates: if no output sequence in the beam matches the candidate phoneme sequence, it is directly rejected; if there is a match, it enters the S2 threshold comparison.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Search Graph Level Keyword Customization—Change Words Without Retraining

Moving "customization" from the model layer to the decoding layer is the foundation of the framework's customizability in this paper. Traditional closed-set classifiers burn the vocabulary into the output layer; changing the vocabulary means changing the model structure. The transducer output in this paper is 210 CI phonemes (Section 3.2), and the phoneme set covers the entire open vocabulary, with keyword knowledge entirely externalized in the LG composed graph. The engineering action to add a new command is: add the new command's phoneme sequence to G, re-compose LG, and update the search graph—zero data collection, zero model retraining. The authors also admit that this idea is isomorphic to the classic hybrid framework (DNN-HMM + WFST, [20][21]) (original words in Section 2.2: "This is similar to the hybrid framework widely adopted in KWS"). The innovation does not lie in WFST customization itself, but in grafting this mature customization mechanism onto a streaming end-to-end transducer and equipping it with two-level neural network verification.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Frame-Level Phoneme Predictor and Viterbi Force Alignment—Boundary Correction and First-Level Verification

This module solves a famous pathology of streaming transducers: **emission delay**. The right context of streaming models is limited (the right context of FSMN in this paper is only [2,2,1,2,2,1]), so the actual emission time of labels is systematically later than the true start time of phonemes, causing the keyword start point given by token passing to be biased late. The paper quantifies this deviation with data (Table 2): the detection level start point error is 0.29 seconds on the clean set and 0.44 seconds on the noisy set. A start point deviation of 0.29 to 0.44 seconds is fatal for subsequent verification—cutting the encoder output according to this boundary would chop off the head of the keyword, feeding a naturally defective candidate segment to the transformer, garbage in garbage out, inevitably damaging final review accuracy.

The countermeasure is divided into three steps (Figure 1(d)): First, empirically **push back** the start point by td frames (experimentally set to td=15, Section 3.3; the paper does not report the milliseconds corresponding to this frame count, but estimating at a 40ms frame rate after 4x encoder downsampling, it is about 0.6 seconds), ensuring the keyword falls completely into the window; Second, construct a **linear WFST graph** using the phoneme sequence of the candidate keyword, and make a clever small design—insert an extra garbage symbol (g) before the phoneme sequence, used to absorb outputs pushed back into the window that do not belong to the candidate keyword. Why g is indispensable: without it, non-keyword frames pushed back into the window would be forced to align to keyword phonemes, distorting the alignment path and lowering likelihood, causing normal true keywords to be easily falsely killed—g provides a legal exit for "extra frames"; Third, perform Viterbi alignment on this graph using the frame-level posteriors of the phoneme predictor, obtaining the candidate's precise start point tr and alignment likelihood S1 (Equation 3), completing the first-level verification.

The cost-performance ratio of this module is extremely high because it is a **one-tool-two-use** design: during training, the frame-level CE loss of the phoneme predictor is an auxiliary task to accelerate and regularize encoder training (the β term in Equation 1); during inference, the same head becomes an aligner providing boundary correction and S1 confidence. No additional always-on computation is added for alignment.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Transformer Final Review with Length Prior Forced Truncation

After force alignment obtains precise boundaries, the output of the shared encoder in the corresponding interval h_enc is cut out and fed to the transformer decoder (Figure 1(c)), performing beam search autoregressively to generate an N-best list. The core design is **forced truncation**: do not wait for <EOS> to appear, forcibly stop at step M, where M is the phoneme sequence length of the candidate keyword (Section 2.4). The rationale for this design lies in the nature of the task: this level does "verification" not "generation"—the length of the answer is a known prior, and the discriminative information of the sequence must all unfold within M steps. The benefits are threefold: computation has an upper bound (M is usually six to over ten steps, fixing the final review overhead); hypotheses in the beam have consistent lengths, so raw summation of S2 is comparable (Equation 4 requires no normalization factor); incidentally avoiding the risk of the autoregressive decoder trailing off with meaningless tokens on short sequences.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Multi-task One-Time Training, Three-Level Synergy Formation

Three heads (transducer joint network, phoneme predictor linear layer, transformer decoder) are hung on the same encoder and trained jointly using the weighted loss of Equation 1 (Section 2.1). This avoids the representation mismatch problem of training levels separately—if the transformer verifier is trained using outputs from another encoder, there will be distribution drift when connected to the detection encoder during inference. The shared encoder allows the three levels to adapt to each other during training: the acoustic representation shaped by the transducer loss is simultaneously polished by phoneme CE and transformer loss, with dense frame-level supervision compensating for the sparse gradients of the transducer loss.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the **attention bias series** ([12][13][14]): different customization mechanisms—they pull the search towards the target keyword transcription by biasing attention, so keyword knowledge enters the model computation graph; in this paper, keyword knowledge is externalized in the WFST search graph, and changing words does not touch the model. Different target scenarios—they focus on runtime single-keyword wake-up, while this paper targets command recognition scenarios with simultaneous multi-command detection.

Compared to **MLD** ([13]): the independence of verification evidence is the essential difference. The posterior confidence of MLD's subsequent levels (sliding window posterior sum, edit distance probability, approximate likelihood) are all derived from transducer outputs, being statistical transformations of the same information source; the verification heads in this paper are new models with independent parameters, and the phoneme predictor and transformer do not share parameters with the transducer joint network, with heterogeneous evidence and uncorrelated errors. The performance gap is directly reflected in Table 1: at a similar accuracy level, the complete framework of this paper has 0.13 false alarms per hour, while MLD reproduction has 0.27 (S3 vs S4). The ROC curve (Figure 2) shows that in the product-level interval of less than 0.15 false alarms per hour, MLD's FR rate deteriorates sharply while this paper's method remains flat.

Compared to **traditional hybrid WFST systems** ([20][21]): structurally similar (acoustic model plus WFST decoding), but the acoustic model is replaced by a streaming end-to-end tiny transducer, and there are two levels of neural network verification after decoding—the WFST scoring of traditional systems is the end point, while the WFST in this paper is only the first baton of a three-level pipeline.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

**Training Data**: 23,000 hours of Mandarin ASR corpus, from real data of Tencent's in-car voice assistant product (Section 3.1). This is a considerable amount of training data, and also the material basis for the command word task to be customized without collection—phoneme-level modeling units paired with 23,000 hours of general speech, the acoustic model has seen enough phoneme context variants, and new commands are just new arrangements of old phonemes. The development set was randomly shuffled and split from the training set (the paper does not report the specific scale of the development set).

**Test Data**: 6,000 keyword sentences, covering 29 Mandarin commands, each command containing more than six phonemes. The paper gives a specific example: the command to start GPS navigation, with the phoneme sequence "d a3 k ai1 d ao3 h ang2" (i.e., "open navigation"). Test sentences are divided into two groups of 3,000 sentences each according to collection conditions: a relatively clean highway driving environment (clean set) and a noisy urban driving environment (noisy set).

**False Alarm Evaluation Data**: 84 hours of audio, covering broadcasts, casual chats, and driving noise (Section 3.1). All data are anonymized and manually transcribed.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Metrics at three levels: **Command Accuracy** (reported separately for clean and noisy sets), measuring the proportion of true commands correctly detected, corresponding to the opposite of FR; **False Alarms per Hour** (FA per hour), calculating the false trigger frequency on the 84-hour negative sample set into hours; **ROC Curve** (Figures 2, 3, 4), depicting the trade-off surface between FR and FA—when drawing the curve, CaTT-KWS fixes the transformer level threshold and dynamically adjusts the force alignment level threshold, while MLD only adjusts the last level threshold. Another mechanistic metric: **Start Point Time Error** (seconds, Table 2), comparing the average offset of the command phrase start point given by the detection level and force alignment level relative to manual annotations.

The rationale for metric selection is product logic: the experience of always-on systems is dominated by false alarms (false triggers interrupt users), but accuracy cannot collapse (users shouting with no response is even more intolerable), so both ends must be reported simultaneously along with a trade-off curve, rather than single-point numbers. Start point error is a direct verification of "whether boundary correction is truly effective"—it translates the mechanistic claims of Section 2.3 into measurable numbers. The paper does not report key metrics for edge-side deployment such as inference latency, Real-Time Factor (RTF), decomposition of computational overhead per level, and power consumption, which is a significant omission in the experimental design.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Core comparisons are all in Table 1 (Accuracy % and False Alarms per Hour, two test sets: clean/noisy):

| Experiment | Framework Configuration | Accuracy (Clean) | Accuracy (Noisy) | False Alarms/Hour | FA Relative Reduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | Transducer + WFST (Baseline) | 96 | 87.65 | 1.47 | - |
| S1 | + Transformer Decoder (Two-stage) | 94.31 | 85.89 | 0.23 | 84.35% |
| S2 | + Force Alignment (Two-stage) | 95.48 | 87.34 | 0.5 | 65.98% |
| S3 | + Alignment + Transformer (Complete CaTT-KWS) | 94.20 | 85.69 | 0.13 | 91.15% |
| S4 | Transducer + MLD ([13] Reproduction) | 94.41 | 86.24 | 0.27 | - |

Comparison 1: **Profile of the baseline itself.** S0 shows that the tiny transducer detection level has an accuracy as high as 96% on the clean set, but 1.47 false alarms per hour—this is the quantitative profile of "high wake-up rate accompanied by high false alarms" (the pain point repeatedly emphasized in the abstract and introduction is verified on its own baseline).

Comparison 2: **Complete framework vs. baseline.** Relative to S0, S3 reduces false alarms from 1.47 per hour to 0.13 per hour, a relative reduction of 91.15% (abstract wording "more than 90%"), and accuracy drops from 96% to 94.20% (clean), 87.65% to 85.69% (noisy), with drops at both ends less than 2 percentage points (abstract wording "accuracy drop less than 2%"). This is the core number of the paper: exchanging less than 2 percentage points of accuracy cost for a 90% reduction in false alarms.

Comparison 3: **Vs. MLD.** The paper reproduced MLD (S4) according to the experimental configuration suggested in literature [13], obtaining accuracy 94.41/86.24, false alarms 0.27 per hour—accuracy is similar to this paper, but false alarms are more than double that of this paper's complete framework (0.27 vs 0.13). The more critical difference is in the ROC curve (Figure 2): in the interval of less than 0.15 false alarms per hour (the strict interval exactly required for actual product operation), this paper's method is significantly better than MLD, while MLD's FR rate deteriorates sharply in this interval. That is to say, MLD is not bad at all operating points, but cannot hold up at the most valuable operating point of "suppressing false alarms to the extreme"—this exactly corroborates the analysis of the "ceiling of single information source verification" in the previous text.

Comparison 4: **Mechanism verification of boundary correction** (Table 2). Detection level command start point error: clean set 0.29 seconds, noisy set 0.44 seconds; after force alignment: clean set 0.11 seconds, noisy set 0.23 seconds. Under both conditions, the start point error is reduced to less than half of the original (clean set drops to 38%, noisy set drops to 52%). This table is direct evidence for the mechanistic claim that "force alignment corrects emission delay," and also explains why the three-stage (S3, false alarms 0.13) is better than the two-stage (S1, false alarms 0.23): accurate start points allow the transformer to receive complete and clean candidate segments, making verification more reliable—the paper explicitly attributes the difference between S1 and S3 in Table 1 to "accurate time boundaries of commands can improve the performance of transformer decoder verification."

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

**Finding 1: Role division and sequential coupling of two-level verification** (Table 1). Adding only transformer final review (S1): false alarms drop from 1.47 to 0.23 (down 84.35%), but accuracy drops by 1.69 percentage points (96 to 94.31); adding only force alignment (S2): false alarms only drop to 0.5 (down 65.98%), but accuracy only drops by 0.52 percentage points (96 to 95.48). The role division read out is: force alignment is the "cheap accuracy keeper"—it kills two-thirds of false alarms at the cost of almost not hurting accuracy; transformer is the "ultimate false alarm killer"—it has the strongest false alarm suppression power but hurts accuracy when used alone (because boundaries are inaccurate, feeding defective candidate segments). Only combining them in the order of "alignment first, final review later" (S3) simultaneously achieves 0.13 false alarms and 94.20 accuracy. This order is not an interchangeable decoration, but a necessary dependency chain: the output of alignment (precise boundaries) is the prerequisite for the input quality of the transformer.

**Finding 2: Transformer decoder capacity is not always better when larger** (Figure 3). Adjusting decoder scale by increasing the number of self-attention blocks: 1 block 0.9M, 2 blocks 1.7M, 4 blocks 3.2M (Figure 3 legend). Conclusion (Section 3.4): increasing the decoder **does not** bring accuracy benefits at similar false alarm levels, and performance is even worse in the low false alarm interval—because more parameters exacerbate overfitting. This negative result provides practical guidance for engineering practice: the capacity of the final reviewer has an optimum point, 1 self-attention block (0.9M) is the sweet spot for this setting, and blindly stacking parameters backfires at strict operating points.

**Finding 3: Modeling unit granularity has operating-point-dependent crossover** (Figure 4). Comparing by changing the modeling units of the transformer decoder from CI phonemes to 6,000 common Chinese characters (phoneme system decoder 0.9M, character system 3.8M, Figure 4 legend). ROC curves show: under low false alarm conditions, the phoneme system has a lower FR rate, while under high false alarm conditions, the character system has a lower FR rate (Section 3.5). Mechanistic explanation: character units carry stronger sequence constraints (each token has more information, high word-level discrimination), but the 6,000-class output layer also brings a larger confusion space and a decoder volume 4 times that of the phoneme system; the phoneme system has weak constraints but a small confusion space (210 classes), and is more stable in intervals requiring extremely strict false alarms. The paper thus gives engineering advice for selecting units on demand: use large-granularity units (characters) for scenarios emphasizing FR, and small-granularity units (phonemes) for scenarios emphasizing FA; at the same time, giving volume countermeasures—because larger modeling unit sets expand the input and output layers, one can control model size by **cutting unused input and output nodes** according to the phonemes/characters covered by the predefined keyword set.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed the CaTT-KWS multi-stage customized KWS framework**: A cascaded structure of streaming tiny transducer detection level, phoneme predictor force alignment verification level, and transformer decoder final review level, with a shared encoder, formed by one-time multi-task learning training. This is the complete implementation of the idea of "suppressing false alarms using cascaded heterogeneous verification" in the multi-command customized KWS scenario.
2. **Significant false alarm suppression effect with data backing**: Achieved 0.13 false alarms per hour on a challenging in-car dataset, a relative reduction of 91.15% relative to the transducer detection baseline (1.47 false alarms per hour) (Table 1 S3), with command recognition accuracy dropping no more than 2 percentage points.
3. **Zero-cost keyword customization mechanism**: Customization occurs entirely at the WFST search graph level (composition of LG in Equation 2), adding new keywords requires no data collection, no model retraining, and phoneme/character two modeling unit granularities can be selected according to operating point needs (Section 3.5).
4. **Force alignment module one-tool-two-use**: During training, it is an auxiliary task for regularizing the encoder (frame-level CE loss); during inference, it is a boundary corrector and first-level verifier, correcting the emission delay pain point of streaming transducers (start point error 0.29/0.44 seconds, Table 2) to 0.11/0.23 seconds, and proving that accurate boundaries are a prerequisite for the performance of subsequent verification (S1 vs S3).
5. **Edge-side feasibility**: Overall approximately 3.8M parameters (Section 3.2, Conclusion), combined with event-driven subsequent level computation, possessing conditions for edge-side deployment; and using ablation to calibrate the transformer capacity sweet spot (1 block, 0.9M) to avoid useless stacking.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**Accuracy cost still exists and is not negligible.** The complete framework drops from 96% to 94.20% on the clean set (Table 1), meaning about 1.8 percentage points of true commands are falsely killed by the subsequent levels. For command control systems, false rejection also hurts experience—users shouting "open navigation" with no response, shouting again, the commercial cost of this frustration is not discussed by the paper. Every bit of benefit in suppressing false alarms through cascaded verification is borrowed from the recall side.

**A large number of hyperparameters rely on manual experience settings.** Multi-task loss weights α/β/γ=1.0/0.8/0.5 (Section 2.1), alignment threshold τ=1.5, final review threshold υ=5, boundary push-back frame count td=15 (Section 3.3), all are empirical values. The paper does not report sensitivity analysis of these parameters or whether they need to be re-tuned during cross-domain migration—especially τ and υ directly determine the operating point, and changing a noise environment (from in-car to home) may likely cause threshold mismatch.

**Fixed push-back amount raises doubts about adaptability to latency changes.** td=15 frames is a constant push-back, but emission delay itself varies with acoustic conditions (Table 2 shows noisy set start point error 0.44 seconds is significantly larger than clean set 0.29 seconds). Fixed push-back in scenarios with large latency fluctuations may still fail to fully capture the word head, or push back too much relying on garbage symbols as a fallback. The start point error after alignment on the noisy set is still 0.23 seconds, indicating that the correction did not completely solve the problem.

**S2 lacks cross-keyword length normalization.** Equation 4 is a raw sum; hypotheses in the beam for the same keyword have consistent lengths due to forced M-step truncation, making them comparable; but phoneme lengths of different keywords are different (commands all contain more than six phonemes but the upper limit is not uniform), and the same global threshold υ=5 has inconsistent tightness for long and short commands, with long commands naturally having more sum terms and larger S2, potentially being systematically relaxed. The paper does not report whether thresholds are set separately for each command.

**Volume cost of character units.** The character system transformer decoder reaches 3.8M, 4 times that of the phoneme system (Section 3.5, Figure 4 legend), although the paper proposes a countermeasure of cutting input and output nodes according to keywords, this is an engineering patch at the deployment stage rather than a solution at the model level.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

**Command scale differs from claims.** The abstract claims to face "dozens to hundreds" of keywords, but the actual tested command set is 29 (Section 3.1). The behavior curve of false alarms growing with the number of keywords (e.g., how much FA increases from 0.13 when expanding from 29 to 100) is not reported by the paper; the actual performance in the "over a hundred" scenario is extrapolated rather than tested.

**Single false alarm evaluation domain.** The 84-hour negative samples all come from broadcasts, casual chats, and driving noise (Section 3.1), i.e., mixed within the in-car domain. The paper does not report false alarm performance across domains (quiet home, TV伴音, children's voices, multi-speaker overlap); for edge-side always-on systems, false alarm loss of control when changing domains is a common launch accident.

**Lack of system-level overhead measurement.** The paper does not report inference latency, Real-Time Factor (RTF), decomposition of trigger rate and average overhead per level verification, memory usage, and power consumption. Although the argument of "event-driven" holds in principle, there are no numbers for the always-on overhead of the first-level transducer and the latency increment of the subsequent two levels after triggering; the argument for edge-side feasibility only falls on the single dimension of 3.8M parameters.

**Missing training details affect reproducibility.** Optimizer, learning rate, training epochs, generation method of frame-level phoneme alignment labels, beam width, development set scale, etc., are not reported by the paper. The MLD baseline is reproduced by the authors themselves according to the configuration suggested in literature [13] (S4), not the original numbers, and the fairness of comparison relies on reproduction quality but the paper does not provide reproduction details.

**No report of statistical significance.** 6,000 test sentences, 0.13 false alarms per hour (about 11 false alarm events in 84 hours), whether the difference between 0.13 and 0.27 is stable under this sample size, and how large the variance across random seeds is, are not reported by the paper.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

**Learnable boundary correction.** Upgrade fixed td push-back to latency-aware adaptive push-back, or directly regress keyword start and end points on the encoder (similar to the idea in citation [4] using CNN to precisely detect the head and tail of wake words), replacing the heuristic of "push-back plus garbage symbol fallback" with end-to-end learned boundary prediction.

**Confidence fusion.** CaTT's S1 (alignment likelihood) and S2 (transformer likelihood) are currently two independent threshold gates in series, and statistical quantities like MLD's edit distance probability are also mature evidence. Jointly modeling multiple lines of confidence (learning a fusion scoring or calibration) may squeeze out more margin in the low false alarm interval than hard threshold series.

**Mixed granularity of modeling units.** Phonemes and characters each have their strengths (the crossover phenomenon in Figure 4), and intermediate granularity units like initials/finals, syllables, or BPE-like units, or mechanisms for automatically selecting granularity according to commands, are worth exploring; combined with an automated input and output node cutting process, productizing the manual engineering suggestions of Section 3.5.

**Automatic calibration of thresholds and operating points.** Online adaptation for τ and υ (calibrating according to environmental noise or user feedback), and a threshold system normalized by command length, can alleviate the problems of fixed threshold cross-domain mismatch and inconsistent tightness for long and short commands.

**Larger command sets and cross-lingual validation.** Expanding the command scale to over a hundred, expanding negative samples to multiple domains (home, office, multi-language mixed speech), and testing the false alarm growth curve and the migration of the customization mechanism under foreign language phoneme systems are necessary validations for pushing this framework from paper to product.

**Orthogonal combination with edge-side compression technologies.** This paper's framework is completely orthogonal to quantization, pruning, and distillation (such as BiFSMN-like binarization schemes), and the combination of 3.8M detection level plus event-driven verification can be further compressed; the paper does not report related attempts, leaving significant space.
