## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword spotting (KWS) systems serve as the core voice interface for devices such as smart speakers, wireless earbuds, and wearable devices. Wake-word listening on these devices constitutes an "always-on" workload: the device must run detection continuously for hours or even days on a single charge, making energy efficiency the direct determinant of battery life and thermal performance. The paper addresses a critical pain point: the industry-standard frontend uses forty-channel log-Mel features, and the number of multiplications in the acoustic model is directly tied to the dimensions of the input feature matrix (as noted in footnote 2 of the original text). This means the number of feature channels is an upstream knob for energy efficiency—reducing the channels from forty to single digits drastically reduces the computational load of the acoustic model. However, hand-crafted Mel filterbanks expose two inherent defects when the channel count is reduced to such low numbers. First, the triangular filters of the Mel scale are designed based on the average human auditory perception of pure tones, serving as a general prior for broad speech perception rather than the specific task of "distinguishing this small set of keywords while resisting specific noise types." When channel counts are high, redundancy can mask this mismatch; but when channels are few, frequency resolution collapses, and discriminative cues for adjacent keywords (such as the high-frequency energy distribution of fricatives or the relative positions of different vowel formants) are averaged out in coarse-grained weighting. Second, hand-crafted filterbanks are unaware of the noise spectral characteristics of the training and deployment environments: frequency bands where noise energy concentrates still receive valuable channel resources, effectively forcing scarce channels to encode noise rather than speech. Together, these two points form a compelling hypothesis: if the filterbank itself is learnable and jointly optimized with the task and noise, it should be more competitive than hand-crafted features under conditions of extreme channel scarcity—this is the proposition the paper aims to verify.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

First, end-to-end learnable features have consistently failed to surpass hand-crafted features in keyword spotting. Optimizing the feature extraction process toward task objectives is a general trend in recent deep learning, and the KWS domain is no exception. Mittermaier et al. embedded SincNet-style sinc convolution filterbanks into a convolutional network-based detection pipeline, where filter cutoff frequencies were jointly trained with the acoustic model; however, this work did not provide a formal comparison with hand-crafted features. Peter et al.'s work at ICASSP 2022 filled this gap, but the conclusion was that MFCCs remained superior for keyword spotting. Thus, the hypothesis that "learnable features are better" has repeatedly failed in head-to-head comparisons. Second, the authors' own prior work (an EUSIPCO conference paper) had already explored filterbank learning, finding no statistically significant difference between learned filterbanks and log-Mel features. This led to the "spectral information redundancy" conjecture: recognizing a small set of keywords does not require utilizing the full spectral information (footnote 1 in the original text notes that another team independently proposed the same conjecture in the context of low-bit precision features). This conjecture is crucial; it explains why learned features fail to win under the standard forty-channel setting—the information redundancy levels the performance ceiling for both representations. Third, the energy efficiency dimension has been entirely absent in previous comparisons: prior works assumed a standard channel count of forty, never treating "channel count" as a variable in the comparison coordinate system, and thus never discovering that the true advantage zone for learned features might lie on the side of fewer channels. Fourth, the learnable frontend itself lacks specialized regularization mechanisms: filterbank channels are prone to co-adapting with specific spectral details of the training noise (overfitting to training noise), and channels tend to become dependent on each other, offering no guarantee of generalization to unseen noise.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

Three intertwined problems. First, under what conditions can learned features truly surpass hand-crafted features—this requires breaking the previous conclusion of "tie or worse" and providing boundary conditions for victory. Second, how to mitigate precision collapse when channel counts are reduced to single digits—if fewer channels mean an avalanche of precision loss, the energy efficiency gains cannot be realized in a usable system, and the trade-off is invalid. Third, how to enable the learnable filterbank to generalize to unseen noise types, rather than simply memorizing the long-term spectrum of the training noise. Added to this is an engineering requirement: all conclusions must withstand statistical testing, and energy efficiency gains must have quantifiable hardware basis.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire pipeline is shown in Figure 1: one-second input speech undergoes Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) to obtain a linear power spectrum matrix, which is then passed through a filterbank layer (a learnable matrix multiplication, optionally with random dropout during training). The output undergoes log compression and batch normalization before being fed into a deep residual convolutional acoustic model with dilated convolutions, followed by posterior processing—the word with the maximum posterior probability among all words is judged as detected.

The "whys" behind several design decisions are worth elaborating. First, retaining the STFT and making only the "mapping from linear power spectrum to K-dimensional features" learnable, rather than learning the frontend from raw waveforms as in the SincNet approach, yields direct benefits. The search space shrinks from the entire frontend to a single $F \times K$ matrix (241 by K in this paper), resulting in very few parameters, reduced overfitting risk, and high interpretability of the matrix itself—it can be directly plotted as filter response curves (as done in Figure 3) and compared band-by-band with the long-term noise spectrum, turning "what was learned" into checkable evidence rather than a black box. Second, the acoustic model uses a deep convolutional network with residual connections and dilated convolutions, based on the authors' own review conclusions: state-of-the-art KWS acoustic modeling relies on convolutional networks possessing both residual connections and the ability to capture long-term time-frequency patterns. Residual connections ensure the deep network is trainable, while dilated convolutions expand the receptive field at zero parameter cost, effectively covering long-term context at the keyword level. Third, the decision rule takes the maximum posterior probability over the entire word segment, strictly aligning with the dataset's one-second fixed-length, one-word-per-segment evaluation protocol, avoiding interference from additional decoders and focusing the comparison on the frontend variable alone.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

Equation 1 defines the linear power spectrum matrix: the input speech undergoes STFT and takes the squared modulus, forming a matrix $X$ with $T$ rows and $F$ columns. The element at row $t$ and column $f$ is the power spectrum value of the $f$-th linear frequency bin in the $t$-th frame, where $T$ is the total number of frames and $F$ is the total number of linear frequency bins.

Equation 2 defines the filterbank layer: $Y$ equals $X$ right-multiplied by $g(W)$, where $W$ is a learnable filterbank matrix of size $F \times K$, jointly optimized with the acoustic model via backpropagation; $g$ is the linear rectification function (ReLU) taking the element-wise maximum with zero, used to ensure the non-negativity of the filterbank weights.

Why must a positivity constraint be applied? The physical meaning of filterbank weights is the weighting coefficient of the power spectrum—the output of a channel is a weighted sum of energies from several frequency bins, and energy superposition cannot be negative physically. A stricter constraint is that if negative weights were allowed, subsequent log compression would be undefined when the output is zero or negative. The benefit of using linear rectification is that the constraint is woven into the forward computation, allowing gradients to pass directly through unsaturated weights, which is simpler to implement than projection-based constraints. Log compression is written as the element-wise logarithm of $\max(Y, \eta)$, where the lower bound $\eta$ is set to $e^{-50}$: the logarithmic operation simulates the dynamic range compression of the auditory system, converting multiplicative differences in the spectrum into additive differences for easier processing by convolutional networks, while setting a lower bound prevents divergence when taking the log of zero. The extremely small value of $e^{-50}$ implies a lower bound of approximately -50 in the log domain, sufficient to cover the dynamic range of speech plus noise scenarios. Batch normalization follows the log compression, aiming to pull features with vastly different scales across channels and SNRs back to a unified distribution, stabilizing the training of the subsequent convolutional network. The filterbank matrix $W$ is initialized with a Mel filterbank—fine-tuning from a feasible solution validated by extensive practice avoids pathological convergence when $K$ is very small and ensures that the comparison between "learned features" and "hand-crafted features" isolates the single variable of "whether joint fine-tuning is performed," increasing internal experimental validity. The training objective is to minimize cross-entropy loss, using the Adam optimizer, a batch size of 64, and an early stopping strategy monitoring validation loss with a patience of five epochs; each experiment is repeated five times with different random initializations to support statistical testing. Notably, the only difference between this filterbank learning scheme and the authors' prior work is the optional random dropout during training—this seemingly small increment is the key to overturning previous conclusions.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Pushing Channel Count to the Extreme, Redefining the Comparison Coordinate System

The most core methodological action of this paper is not adding new modules, but changing the problem: instead of comparing learned and hand-crafted features under the standard forty channels (a path proven to only yield ties), the channel count is slashed all the way down to five, turning the allocation of frequency resources into a scarcity constraint. Under the cover of information redundancy, the mismatch between hand-crafted Mel scales and task objectives is masked; once channels are scarce, both defects erupt simultaneously—and the learnable filterbank gains an advantage in two degrees of freedom: it can redistribute the center frequency, bandwidth, and shape of channels entirely (Figure 3 shows that the learned filter shapes are not Mel triangles), allowing it to move to frequency bands richest in keyword discriminative information and avoid bands where noise energy concentrates. This is the winning move for "learned features outperform hand-crafted features," and to the authors' knowledge, this paper is the first to provide proof of this.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Random Dropout in the Filterbank Layer and Autoencoder-style Bottleneck

The only methodological difference from prior work is the introduction of random dropout in the filterbank layer, with a rate of 0.4. Its mechanism (conjectured in Section 4 of the paper) is: random dropout combined with extremely few channels creates an information bottleneck in the filterbank layer—randomly dropping channels during training forces each channel to learn more robust representations that are less dependent on other channels, blocking co-adaptation between channels. This is similar to the phenomenon in autoencoders where the bottleneck layer forces the compressed representation to capture essential structures. The benefits are intuitively presented in Figure 3: the filterbank learned with random dropout is a smoothed version, especially in the low-frequency range—narrow and sharp filters often fit specific spectral details of the training noise, while smoothing implies lower dependence on training noise details and better generalization to unseen noise. It should be noted that the main text does not explicitly state whether random dropout acts on the weight matrix or the output feature tensor, but given the goal is to "enhance the robustness and generalization of individual filter channels," combined with the box position in Figure 1, a reasonable interpretation is channel-level random dropping of the filterbank layer output.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Treating Energy Consumption as a First-Class Evaluation Dimension

The paper uses the number of multiplications per second of input speech in the acoustic model as a proxy for energy consumption, based on hardware measurements by Tang et al.: there is a strong positive linear relationship between the number of multiplications and power consumption in KWS convolutional models, with a coefficient of determination of 0.9641 and a p-value of 0.0001. Due to the presence of residual connections, the number of multiplications in the acoustic model strongly depends on the channel count (Table 1): with $K=40$, there are 895 million multiplications per second; with $K=5$, only 71 million, a reduction of 12.6 times overall—greater than the 8-fold reduction in channel count itself, indicating that the number of multiplications scales super-linearly with $K$ in this architecture. This proxy metric allows the gains from "slashing channels" to be directly converted into energy numbers, upgrading frontend design from a pure accuracy problem to a system-level accuracy-energy trade-off.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the SincNet approach: the latter learns sinc filters from raw waveforms, where each filter has only two parameters (low and high cutoff frequencies), imposing a strong prior of bandpass shape; this paper retains the STFT and learns a full matrix mapping with only positivity constraints, distributing degrees of freedom across all weights, and directly optimizing for few channels and energy efficiency. Compared to the authors' prior work: methodologically, only random dropout was added, but experimentally, the comparison was pushed to the extreme interval of fewer than ten channels, thereby overturning previous conclusions—indicating that the prior "no significant difference" conclusion was due to choosing the wrong coordinate system, not because the method itself was flawed. Compared to Peter et al.'s neural architecture search plus quantization scheme: that work searches for frontend structures and quantizes them, concluding MFCC is superior; this paper does not search for architecture or quantize, but single-pointly proves that learned features win with few channels. The two conclusions are not contradictory because the winning condition depends on the variable of channel count. Compared to the traditional MFCC pipeline: MFCCs undergo discrete cosine transform and cepstral weighting on top of log-Mel; the comparison evidence chain in this paper only covers log-Mel as a baseline, and no direct comparison with MFCC was performed.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Experiments were conducted on a noisy version of the Google Speech Commands dataset, constructed by the authors in prior work. The corpus consists of one-second speech segments, each containing one word, with additive noise from the NOISEX-92 and CHiME-3 noise databases. The noise types for the training and validation sets total eight: in-car, factory 1, bus, pedestrian street, white noise, multi-speaker babble, machine gun, and F-16 cockpit. The test set noise is divided into two groups—seen noise (in-car, factory 1, bus, pedestrian street) and unseen noise (factory 2, F-4 cockpit, café, street corner)—the unseen noise group is the key apparatus for testing generalization. Training and validation SNRs are 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 dB plus clean conditions; test SNRs extend to -10, -5, 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 dB plus clean—the extrapolation of tests to -10 dB is an intentional extreme stress test. Under each combination of noise type and SNR, the training, validation, and test sets contain 3,699, 427, and 497 utterances, respectively; based on this, the training pool comprises approximately 48 combinations, totaling approximately 178,000 utterances. The task is classification into eleven classes: the ten standard keywords of the dataset plus a padding class, with near-balanced distribution per class. Frontend configuration: 30 ms analysis window (480 points at 16 kHz sampling), 10 ms frame shift, resulting in 241 linear frequency bins. Random dropout rate is 0.4 when enabled. The filterbank matrix is initialized with Mel. Each experimental condition is repeated five times with different random initializations.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The primary metric is keyword spotting accuracy (percentage), reported decomposed by SNR and seen/unseen noise dimensions, averaged over five repetitions (Table 1); the "average accuracy" cited in the text and shown in Figure 2 is the average across SNRs. Statistical testing uses two-sample t-tests with a significance level of 0.05; grid points significantly superior to the baseline are marked in bold or underlined in Table 1 (plain text cannot reproduce the markings). The rationale is simple: accuracy differences from a single training run may entirely stem from random initialization; five repetitions plus t-tests are key apparatuses for the credibility of conclusions in a short paper. The energy efficiency metric uses the number of multiplications per second in the acoustic model, justified by the linear relationship with measured power consumption (coefficient of determination 0.9641, p-value 0.0001). Metrics commonly used in deployment, such as false alarm rate, miss rate, ROC curves, actual hardware latency and power consumption, and model parameter count—are not reported in the paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The comparison objects are three frontends: log-Mel (hand-crafted baseline), learned features without random dropout, and learned features with random dropout, with channel counts set to 40, 10, 8, 7, and 5 (Table 1; only these points are reported due to space constraints).

Channel count equals 40 (895 million multiplications per second): no statistically significant difference among the three frontends, consistent with the authors' prior conclusions. Looking closely at the grid points, the no-dropout learned features are even slightly worse under some conditions (unseen -10 dB: 35.41% vs. 37.17%; clean: 95.58% vs. 96.09%), while the dropout version is slightly better (unseen -10 dB: 38.14%, clean: 96.20%), but none are significant—the spectral information redundancy masks the benefits of learning.

Channel count equals 10 (188 million multiplications per second): learned features begin to numerically exceed the hand-crafted baseline at all grid points (seen -10 dB: 47.37% vs. 45.41%; unseen 0 dB: 74.85% vs. 74.34%). The paper judges that "filterbank learning becomes advantageous when the channel count is less than ten, especially for unseen noise."

Channel count equals 8 (141 million multiplications per second): the advantage expands. Seen 0 dB: dropout learned features 78.99% vs. 76.01%, nearly three percentage points higher; unseen 0 dB: no-dropout learned features 72.24% vs. 68.92%, 3.32 percentage points higher; unseen -5 dB: 53.11% vs. 49.94%. The dropout version dominates all grid points on both seen and unseen sides at this channel count.

Channel count equals 7 (118 million multiplications per second): no-dropout learned features lead comprehensively on the unseen side (-10 dB: 30.47% vs. 27.88%; -5 dB: 51.80% vs. 48.20%; 0 dB: 72.12% vs. 68.51%).

Channel count equals 5 (71 million multiplications per second): the paper emphasizes that dropout learned features exceed the hand-crafted baseline under all test noise conditions, which is verified grid-by-grid—seen side ranges from 44.66% vs. 40.12% at -10 dB to 93.91% vs. 92.13% at clean; unseen side ranges from 28.39% vs. 24.52% to 93.81% vs. 92.50%. The most striking is unseen -10 dB: an improvement of 3.87 absolute percentage points, a relative increase of about 16%, indicating that under the harshest conditions, the noise-robustness advantage of learned features is actually the largest.

System-level trade-offs (Observations 3 and 4 in the main text and footnote 3) are the most engineering-valuable numbers in the paper. First group: switching from 40-channel log-Mel (average 81.95% across SNRs) to 8-channel dropout learned features (average 79.11%) results in a relative accuracy loss of only 3.5%, while energy consumption drops from 895 to 141, approximately 6.3 times. As a control, switching from 40 to 8 while keeping hand-crafted features (average 76.75%) results in a relative loss of 6.3% (footnote 3)—meaning learning almost halves the cost of slashing channels. Second group: switching from 8-channel hand-crafted features (average 76.75%) to 5-channel dropout learned features (average 76.86%) results in no accuracy loss, even a slight increase, while energy consumption drops by another factor of two. Combined, there is an unspoken calculation in the paper: switching directly from 40-channel hand-crafted to 5-channel learned features results in a relative accuracy loss of about 6.2%, while energy consumption drops by about 12.6 times. Figure 2 plots these trade-offs as a "multiplications-average accuracy" curve: with multiplications on the x-axis and accuracy on the y-axis, the curve in the low-multiplication region (few channels) is significantly higher than the hand-crafted feature curve, and the two curves tend to coincide when the channel count is greater than ten.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

First ablation: the switch of random dropout (all grid points in Table 1). At channel count 40, the dropout version is almost universally superior to the no-dropout version on the unseen side (-10 dB: 38.14% vs. 35.41%); at channel count 8, the dropout version dominates all grid points on both seen and unseen sides; at channel count 10, both have wins and losses; at channel count 5, the dropout version is better at most grid points. The overall conclusion is that random dropout yields the greatest benefits in the two hardest directions: extremely few channels and unseen noise, supporting the design goal of "enhancing the robustness and generalization of learned filterbanks."

Second ablation (actually evidence analysis): filterbank morphology (Figure 3, channel count 5, average of five repetitions). Two findings: first, the filterbank learned with random dropout is a smoothed version of the no-dropout version, especially in the low-frequency range—this aligns with the bottleneck mechanism explanation, where narrow and sharp filters correspond to fitting training noise details, and smoothing means denoising the fit. Second, learned filterbanks generally suppress frequency components around 2.7 kHz and 4.3 kHz; the authors pin this on the training noise: the long-term spectrum of F-16 cockpit noise happens to have strong peaks at these two frequencies—the filterbank learned to avoid the noise peaks, equivalent to performing implicit frequency-domain noise avoidance at the feature level. Meanwhile, learned filterbanks place more weight on the 5.8 to 7 kHz frequency band compared to the Mel scale (dashed line in Figure 3), because the long-term spectrum of most training noise attenuates in the high-frequency range, and speech information in this band suffers the least distortion—moving channels to the "places with the least noise." This is the direct spectral evidence for the assertion that "learned features adapt to noise spectral characteristics and are more robust to both seen and unseen noise." The authors also note that this analysis holds for channel counts greater than five (similar graphics), but the advantage is masked by information redundancy when the channel count is not less than ten.

The third is an implicit redundancy ablation: in the process of reducing channel count from 40 to 5, the accuracy of hand-crafted features monotonically collapses, while the collapse of learned features is significantly slower (Figure 2), indirectly supporting the spectral redundancy hypothesis proposed independently by the authors and another team—redundancy levels all differences when channels are sufficient, but reveals the disparity in allocation capability when channels are scarce.

## Main Contributions

First, to the authors' knowledge, this is the first proof that filterbank learning can surpass hand-crafted speech features when the number of filterbank channels is drastically reduced. This corrects the narrative of previous works stating "learned features do not surpass hand-crafted" to "conditional victory," with the boundary of victory lying near channel counts less than ten, representing a substantial advancement in the understanding of this direction. Second, it provides translatable system-level trade-off numbers: switching from 40-channel log-Mel to 8-channel learned features results in a 3.5% relative accuracy loss and a 6.3x energy reduction; switching from 8-channel hand-crafted to 5-channel learned features maintains accuracy while reducing energy by another factor of two—this set of numbers has direct reference value for always-on low-resource device deployment. Third, it introduces random dropout into the filterbank layer, using a bottleneck mechanism to enhance the noise robustness and channel generalization of the learnable frontend, with a small methodological increment but clear benefits. Fourth, filterbank visualization evidence: the suppression at 2.7 and 4.3 kHz corresponds to F-16 noise peaks, and the weighting at 5.8 to 7 kHz corresponds to high-frequency noise attenuation, grounding the slogan of "learning to adapt to noise" into checkable spectral evidence. Fifth, a reusable evaluation apparatus: five repetitions plus t-tests plus multiplication-count energy proxy (linear relationship coefficient of determination 0.9641) establishes a clean comparison paradigm for small-footprint frontend research.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

The accuracy ceiling still exists: learned features cannot eliminate the cost of slashing channels; 8 channels still have a 3.5% relative loss. More sharply, the "average 3.5%" masks degradation under extreme conditions—under unseen -10 dB, 8-channel dropout learned features (30.52%) are 6.6 absolute percentage points lower than 40-channel hand-crafted (37.17%), a relative difference of about 18%; the cost of few channels under poor SNR is far greater than the average. The expressive form of the filterbank layer itself is limited: Equation 2 is a linear weighting within one frame, lacking non-linear combination capabilities in the frequency domain and not carrying the bandpass shape prior of SincNet; there is no theoretical guarantee that this matrix can approximate the optimal discriminative subspace under low channel counts. The design space of random dropout is unexplored: the rate is fixed at 0.4, the paper does not report rate ablation, and the position of the acting tensor (weights or features) is not explicitly stated. The energy proxy only counts acoustic model multiplications: the filterbank layer itself performs $241 \times K$ multiplications per frame, and the overhead of STFT, memory access, and batch normalization is not included; in real memory-constrained microcontrollers, memory access energy may exceed multiplication energy. The decision rule takes the maximum posterior over the entire segment: there is no streaming sliding window, posterior smoothing, or false alarm control, leaving one step away from the detection rate-false alarm rate operating point of true concern for always-on deployment.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

The vocabulary is fixed to the ten words of the dataset plus the padding class; it has not been validated on larger vocabularies or multi-word sequence wake-words, so the extrapolation of conclusions to custom wake-word scenarios is unknown. It only compares with one baseline, log-Mel: it does not reproduce SincNet, MFCC, or architecture search plus quantization schemes within the same framework, and the tension with the existing conclusion that "MFCC is superior" is not reconciled by experiments. Channel counts are only reported at five points (40, 10, 8, 7, 5, due to space constraints); more extreme limits (e.g., 3, 4) are not explored, so the inflection point of accuracy collapse and the limits of energy gains are unclear. Noise conditions only include additive noise, no reverberation; while extrapolating training SNRs from 0 to 20 dB to test SNRs of -10 dB is harsh, the training distribution does not cover this region, so the -10 dB results should be understood as out-of-domain stress tests. There are no actual hardware power consumption and latency measurements; the depth, channel configuration, and parameter count of the acoustic model are not reported in the paper. The morphological analysis in Figure 3 remains at the level of visualization and correlation interpretation—for example, the "dip at 2.7 kHz originating from F-16 noise peaks" lacks causal verification experiments (retraining after removing that noise to see if the dip disappears), and there is no corresponding filterbank behavior analysis on the unseen noise side. Additionally, the noisy dataset itself originates from the authors' prior work; while self-built benchmarks facilitate comparison, their value for independent reproduction requires third-party verification.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper states that future work will conduct systematic research to gain deep insights helpful for designing better speech features for robust small-footprint keyword spotting. Following the evidence chain of this paper, several specific directions can be extended: first, joint exploration of structure and constraints—explicitly formalizing the observed mechanisms of "avoiding noise peaks and strengthening high frequencies" into noise-aware regularization, or even combining online noise estimation for runtime channel allocation; second, orthogonal superposition with model compression—the low-bit feature direction and the few-channel direction of this paper are independent, and their combination may yield multiplicative energy efficiency gains; third, advancing the evaluation to streaming detection protocols, adding false alarm rate-miss rate operating points and actual power consumption measurements on real microcontrollers; fourth, expanding noise dimensions to reverberation and multi-noise superposition, and re-validating on custom wake-word corpora; fifth, completing ablations of random dropout rates, positions, and structured variants (e.g., per-channel dropping), as well as limit scans for channel counts less than five, to find the inflection point of accuracy collapse. These directions collectively point to the same goal: realizing the concept that "frontend features are also an optimizable part of the model" into deployable systems under the dual constraints of energy efficiency and robustness.
