# Deep LSTM Spoken Term Detection using Wav2Vec 2.0 Recognizer

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Ján Švec, Jan Lehečka, Luboš Šmídl (Department of Cybernetics, University of West Bohemia in Pilsen, Czech Republic)
- **Date**: September 2022 (Interspeech 2022)
- **Link**: https://doi.org/10.21437/interspeech.2022-10409
- **Keywords**: spoken term detection, Wav2Vec 2.0, grapheme confusion network, out-of-vocabulary retrieval, self-supervised pretraining, deep LSTM, joint embedding space

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points
Spoken Term Detection (STD) is a variant of Keyword Spotting (KWS) applied to large-scale spoken archives: it shifts the time-consuming recognition and indexing work to an offline phase, achieving second-level response times for online queries, trading one-time preprocessing costs for search speed (citation [1]). Unlike edge-side wake-word KWS, which only detects a single pre-trained wake word, STD faces "arbitrary query words provided by users after the fact," which is a typical requirement for oral history archive retrieval. The target scenario of this paper is the USC-SFI MALACH oral history archive (English version LDC2012S05, Czech version LDC2014S04). The most valuable words for retrieval in such archives are precisely proper nouns such as names and places that are out-of-vocabulary (OOV) (citation [7])—the archives themselves record numerous historical details that cannot be covered by standard vocabulary lists.

The traditional STD pipeline involves: a DNN-HMM hybrid recognizer converting audio into word lattices, and then building an inverted index from these lattices. The fatal flaw of this paradigm lies in its vocabulary dependency: out-of-vocabulary words (OOV) simply cannot enter the index, requiring workaround remedies via proxy words (citations [2][3]) or sub-word units (citations [4][5][6]). The accuracy of these remedies is directly constrained by the quality of the pronunciation dictionary. Historically, recognizers for oral history archives have incorporated a large amount of manual domain knowledge into their pronunciation dictionaries and language models (citation [8]). This knowledge is both an asset and a shackle: the asset lies in good domain adaptation, while the shackle lies in high maintenance costs, inherent OOV limitations, and the authors' judgment that hybrid architectures have reached their ceiling. Meanwhile, end-to-end systems have generally surpassed hybrid systems, among which Wav2Vec 2.0 (citation [9]) is particularly attractive for such scenarios: it requires no language model or pronunciation dictionary, directly outputting grapheme-level transcriptions, making it naturally suitable for modeling OOV. From Tab. 1, we can quantify the severity of this problem: the OOV rate for the English test set is 3.2%, and for the Czech test set, it is 2.6%, while the vocabularies of the two recognizers are as large as 243,699 and 252,089 words, respectively—no matter how large the vocabulary, it cannot cover the demand for proper noun retrieval.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods
The paper identifies gaps in existing solutions from four levels. First, inverted index methods (DNN-HMM plus word lattices) are structurally unable to index OOVs; proxy words and sub-word units only alleviate rather than solve the problem, and they rely on the "manual knowledge bottleneck" of the pronunciation dictionary. Second, the authors' own system from the previous year (Interspeech 2021, citation [13]) applied Deep LSTM embedding scoring to the output of a DNN-HMM phoneme recognizer, achieving ATWV scores of 0.5823 for English and 0.7531 for Czech on the test set (Tab. 4). However, the phoneme recognizer itself still requires a pronunciation dictionary, leaving the root cause of the OOV problem unresolved. Third, a raw Wav2Vec grapheme recognizer performs poorly for STD: on in-vocabulary terms, the raw Wav2Vec development set MTWV is only 0.6178 for English and 0.7248 for Czech (Tab. 2), significantly lower than the DNN-HMM baseline of 0.7899 and 0.8760—the reason being that 1-best grapheme transcriptions lack language model constraints, causing homophone competition errors to directly enter the index; even adding an external language model with beam search (using the same language model as DNN-HMM, implemented via HuggingFace Wav2Vec2ProcessorWithLM, citation [20]) can recover scores to 0.8105 and 0.8757 (Tab. 2), it loses the advantage of "not relying on a language model," and OOV remains unsolved. Fourth, from an engineering perspective, Wav2Vec 2.0 is based on Transformer, where self-attention has quadratic complexity for input length computation and memory, limiting manageable input lengths to tens of seconds. Oral archive recordings often exceed one hour, making direct whole-segment inference infeasible. Additionally, CTC training does not naturally guarantee precise alignment between graphemes and acoustic frames, which is an additional complication for STD requiring hit time segments.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved
In one sentence: How to fuse the knowledge of the "OOV-friendly but knowledge-poor" grapheme Wav2Vec recognizer and the "knowledge-rich but vocabulary-limited" DNN-HMM hybrid recognizer into a trainable end-to-end STD system, without relying on pronunciation dictionaries or language models, while solving three engineering obstacles: long audio inference, CTC alignment errors, and training without manual annotations. Specifically broken down into four sub-problems: 1) What retrievable intermediate representation to use instead of word lattices (answer: grapheme confusion network); 2) How to train the scoring network without manual annotations (answer: bootstrap training queries using high-confidence words from DNN-HMM); 3) How to avoid training labels being polluted by boundary noise due to inaccurate CTC alignment (answer: boundary masking); 4) How to handle arbitrary-length inputs (answer: sliding window decoding).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation
The entire system is a "three-piece set" consisting of "two recognizers and one scoring network." The front end is Wav2Vec 2.0 fine-tuned as a grapheme recognizer, with outputs shown in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2: the CTC classification layer outputs a "frame × grapheme" posterior probability matrix, which is first spliced via a sliding window into a complete posterior matrix of arbitrary length, then compressed into a frame-aligned grapheme confusion network (each confusion segment records the 3 most likely graphemes and their probabilities, and segment duration). The back end is the Deep LSTM STD network, composed of two independent processing pipelines (Fig. 2): the green pipeline encodes the feature vector sequence of each confusion segment into an embedding sequence; the yellow pipeline encodes the grapheme sequence of the query word into a few query embedding vectors. The two pipelines have the same structure—each consists of 6 layers of bidirectional LSTM with skip connections, with an output dimension of 300 (concatenation of forward and backward). Finally, dot-product scoring is performed segment by segment. Additionally, there is a small bidirectional LSTM attached to the grapheme embeddings (20 units per direction plus a dense linear output layer) to predict the minimum number of segments the query word should have in the confusion network.

Several design motivations are worth expanding. First, the embeddings output by the two pipelines live in the same mathematical space (the joint embedding space in Fig. 2). Scoring is simply taking the maximum dot product with all query embeddings plus Sigmoid calibration (Eq. (3)). This means the query side and audio side are completely decoupled: audio-side embeddings are independent of the query and can be precomputed offline entirely, leaving only vector dot products for online retrieval, which meets the STD architectural requirement of "heavy offline, light online." Second, the query side uses a shared network plus max pooling to produce multiple query embeddings (three in experiments: one for the first half, one for the middle, one for the second half of the query). The paper explicitly states the reason—to enhance network expressiveness without increasing trainable parameters. Why "one segment to multiple query vectors then take the maximum"? Because the alignment quality of different parts of a query word in the confusion network varies. Multiple local embeddings allow each audio segment to find the query part that best matches it, and max pooling naturally tolerates local misalignment. Third, input features are deliberately kept minimal (segment duration, first three graphemes and their probabilities), following the recipe validated in citation [13] for the phoneme-based system. The benefit is that competition hypothesis information in the confusion network is retained in the features, not just the 1-best, allowing recognition uncertainty to be transmitted to the scoring network. Fourth, scoring uses trainable Sigmoid calibration parameters to compress non-normalized dot product values into calibrated probabilities, giving threshold decisions stable semantics.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms
The construction of the grapheme confusion network is the most mathematically core part of the paper. Let the CTC output be the posterior matrix, where $t$ is the frame index and $y$ is the output symbol table including the blank symbol. Step 1: Generate 1-best grapheme hypotheses and their frame alignment via standard CTC greedy decoding. The alignment must satisfy Eq. (1): the grapheme is the symbol with the highest posterior probability frame-by-frame within its dominated frame interval, followed by several frames dominated by blank symbols. The role of the blank segment is to separate consecutive occurrences of the same grapheme. The paper's example is intuitive: for the word "book," the frame-by-frame optimal sequence is b-b-blank-o-o-blank-o-k-k, so the 1-best hypothesis has the main alignment as ((1,4),(4,7),(7,8),(8,10)). Step 2: Using this 1-best and its frame alignment as the pivot sequence (following the confusion network generation idea in citation [15]), hang alternative graphemes in parallel at each position to obtain the symbol distribution for segment $s$ (Eq. (2)): sum the grapheme posteriors for all frames within the segment, then normalize by dividing by the sum of all non-blank symbol posteriors for all frames within the segment. Intuitively, this redistributes the probability mass occupied by blank symbols to the actual graphemes according to the intra-frame proportion, resulting in a "blank-removed, frame-aligned, competition-hypothesis-inclusive" compressed representation. The paper specifically argues a key premise: although CTC training mathematically treats "the same grapheme sequence with different alignments" as equivalent (which is the source of CTC's alignment-free loss), experimental verification confirms that the grapheme-level alignment obtained by the above greedy decoding is sufficiently effective to support the frame-to-grapheme correspondence required by STD (supporting evidence in Tab. 2's usability).

The scoring formula is Eq. (3): the calibrated hit probability for segment $s$ equals the Sigmoid applied to "the maximum dot product with all query embeddings multiplied by the calibration slope plus the calibration bias," where the slope and bias are trainable parameters. During detection, the sequence is simply thresholded to find peaks, with peak length not less than the minimum number of segments predicted by the query graphemes. The hit score takes the mean of the peak, and the hit time boundaries are calculated directly from the frame alignment of the corresponding segments (more details in citation [13]).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Bootstrapping Query Training from Unannotated Archives (Knowledge Transfer Pipeline)
This is the most important innovation at the conceptual level of the paper (Fig. 1). The scheme is: pass the same batch of unannotated audio through two recognizers—Wav2Vec produces grapheme confusion networks, and the DNN-HMM hybrid recognizer produces word hypotheses with confidence scores; only take high-confidence words with confidence higher than 0.95 as query words. The sequence of corresponding segments in the confusion network for these words serves as the occurrence samples for the query, paired with binary training targets (segment-level presence or absence), to train the Deep LSTM STD network. The cleverness of this design lies in indirect knowledge transfer: the knowledge behind the DNN-HMM recognizer's pronunciation dictionary and language model no longer exists in the retrieval system as explicit resources, but is distilled into the parameters of the scoring network in the form of "teacher annotations"—the network implicitly learns the phonological and syntactic knowledge contained in the hybrid recognizer. There are three layers of engineering benefits: the retrieval system runtime completely does not need a pronunciation dictionary or language model (they only act as teachers once during training); training data can be infinitely extended to the entire unannotated archive (in practice, using unannotated English and Czech data from the Visual History Archive, excluding recordings from development and test set speakers); and no manual annotation is required. The cost is that the system deployment still requires running the DNN-HMM once, but this is a pure offline one-time cost.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Grapheme Confusion Network as a Retrievable Intermediate Representation
Why not use 1-best transcription or word lattices directly? 1-best loses recognition uncertainty, and grapheme competition errors have no way back; word lattices are word-level and depend on the vocabulary. The grapheme confusion network occupies both ends: grapheme-level granularity allows any OOV query to enter the system directly as a grapheme sequence, while the probability distribution on confusion segments retains a certain amount of recognition hypothesis information. Coupled with the blank symbol mass redistribution in Eq. (2) and segment-level frame alignment, this representation simultaneously satisfies the three rigid requirements of STD—retrievable (segment-level features can be pre-encoded into embeddings), scorable (competition hypotheses enter features), and locatable (segment alignment provides hit time boundaries). Additionally, since word separators are merged with blank symbols during training (both recorded as blanks), the entire representation is naturally segmented by word, which meshes with the term filtering rule "query words are not substrings of other words in the dataset or vocabulary words" (Section 4), avoiding substring false alarms.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Sliding Window CTC Decoding
Addressing the quadratic complexity of Transformer self-attention, the paper uses a sliding window with an 18-second window length and 3-second overlap to split long recordings. Each window independently runs Wav2Vec to obtain posteriors. The overlap region is split from the middle: the first 1.5 seconds take the posterior from the left window, and the last 1.5 seconds take the posterior from the right window, splicing out the complete posterior matrix for arbitrary-length inputs, which is then uniformly used to construct the confusion network. The choice to splice at the "posterior matrix layer" rather than the "transcription layer" is deliberate: the confusion network construction algorithm (Eqs. (1)(2)) acts on the posterior matrix. Splicing across windows only requires ensuring the continuity of the posterior matrix time axis, after which the subsequent process is completely unaware; whereas if splicing were done at the decoded text layer, graphemes cut off at window boundaries would be difficult to handle. The 3-second overlap also leaves room for acoustic context at the window edges, reducing degradation of boundary recognition quality.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Boundary Masking to Absorb CTC Alignment Errors
The paper does not shy away from the problem of imprecise CTC alignment; instead, it first quantifies it and then designs a countermeasure. The authors analyzed training queries generated by high-confidence words from DNN-HMM and found that the 1-best graphemes from Wav2Vec deviated from the reference words by at most a few graphemes (corresponding to a few confusion segments), and never observed CTC output shifts exceeding five graphemes. The countermeasure is output masking during training: if the training target undergoes a 0-to-1 or 1-to-0 transition at a certain point, the loss for the segments on both sides of the transition point is not backpropagated, allowing the network to learn the optimal "soft transition" (shown in the top of Fig. 2). The essence of this design is to remove the "alignment uncertainty interval" from the supervision signal and leave it to the model's discretion, avoiding the network being forced to fit noise from essentially random labels at the boundaries. The mask width is optimized as a hyperparameter on the development set according to MTWV, resulting in positive/negative 1 for English and positive/negative 2 for Czech (Section 5). The fact that the optimal widths differ for the two languages also indicates that the alignment error distribution is language-dependent.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods
Compared to traditional inverted index pipelines: the retrieval unit changes from "words in the vocabulary" to "dot-product neighbors in the grapheme embedding space," completely uprooting vocabulary dependency, and OOV changes from "unhandleable" to "using the same scoring mechanism as IV." Compared to heuristic methods (citation [4]) and siamese network methods (citation [18]): those methods separate representation learning and scoring, whereas this paper is a complete framework for joint end-to-end training of embedding extraction and scoring calibration, pulling development set MTWV from 0.4636/0.6225 (English/Czech, Tab. 3) to 0.8308/0.8987. Compared to their own previous year's Deep LSTM plus DNN-HMM phoneme recognizer (citation [13]): the scoring network skeleton and feature recipe are basically inherited, with the revolutionary change at the input end—phoneme posteriors are replaced by grapheme confusion networks, and pronunciation dictionary dependency is downgraded from a system necessity to a training-time teacher resource. This change leaped the development set MTWV from 0.6703/0.7723 to 0.8308/0.8987 (Tab. 3), indicating that the bottleneck was previously in the quality of the phoneme recognizer rather than the scoring framework. Compared to Wav2Vec with external language model and beam search, this method performs better on in-vocabulary terms (0.8398 vs 0.8105, Czech 0.9117 vs 0.8757, Tab. 3 vs Tab. 2), indicating that the trainable scoring network's robustness to recognition errors exceeds "more accurate 1-best plus naive retrieval"—this is a judgment worth paraphrasing: downstream learnable retrieval structures can partially absorb upstream recognizer errors, and optimizing each stage individually does not necessarily mean global optimization.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale
The evaluation data is the English and Czech parts of the MALACH oral history archive. Statistics for the development and test sets are shown in Tab. 1: vocabulary size is 243,699 for English and 252,082 for Czech; 10 speakers each; OOV rate is 0.5% development/3.2% test for English, and 0.3% development/2.6% test for Czech; DNN-HMM recognizer word error rate is 24.10/19.66 development/test for English, and 23.98/19.11 development/test for Czech; in-vocabulary query terms are 597/601 development/test for English, and 1680/1673 development/test for Czech; OOV query terms are 31/6 development/test for English, and 1145/948 development/test for Czech; data duration is 11.1/11.3 hours development/test for English, and 20.4/19.4 hours development/test for Czech. Query terms are automatically divided into IV and OOV based on the DNN-HMM vocabulary from development and test data, and terms that are substrings of other terms or vocabulary words are filtered out, ensuring direct comparability with citations [18][13].

Model scale: The English Wav2Vec starts from the public 2.0 Base checkpoint (fairseq toolchain), fine-tuned on the MALACH training set—long audio is cut to no more than 30 seconds based on speech pauses, non-speech events and punctuation are removed, all converted to lowercase, peak learning rate 8e-5, 80,000 steps update, equivalent to running 86 epochs on 246 hours of training audio. There is no public monolingual pre-trained model for Czech, so the authors trained one from scratch: pre-training data exceeds 80,000 hours of Czech (TV 27k, Parliament 24k, Radio 22k, Sports 5k, Telephone 2k hours and several other domains), all cut to no more than 30 seconds, pre-trained following the steps in citation [9]; fine-tuning data preparation is the same as for English, 87 hours, peak learning rate 2e-5, 80,000 steps update, equivalent to 270 epochs. The model is open-sourced on HuggingFace under the name fav-kky/wav2vec2-base-cs-80k-ClTRUS. The number of output symbols for the CTC classification layer is 53 for English and 51 for Czech, with both models having an output frame length of 0.02 seconds. One Deep LSTM STD network is trained for each language: training data is unannotated data from the Visual History Archive (excluding development and test set speakers), 200,000 steps, batch size 32, ADAM optimizer, inverse square root learning rate schedule with no warm-up; training queries only take words with confidence higher than 0.95. Regarding the parameter count of the STD network, indexing time, retrieval real-time rate, and storage overhead, the paper does not report.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics
The metrics use the standard Term Weighted Value (TWV) family in the STD field (citation [19]): its core is to synthesize miss rate and false alarm rate into a single value weighted by cost, forcing the system to make explicit trade-offs between the two types of errors, rather than tuning thresholds to inflate numbers. The paper reports Maximum TWV (MTWV, i.e., taking the optimal decision threshold on the development set) on the development set, and Actual TWV (ATWV, where the threshold is fixed to the optimal value selected on the development set and applied to the test set) on the test set. The rationale for this arrangement is honest deployment simulation: the threshold must be determined a priori without seeing the test data, so ATWV reflects true online performance; MTWV is used for comparing the upper limits of capabilities between methods and hyperparameter selection (mask width was determined this way). The specific values of the weighting coefficients are not reported in the paper, following the standard settings in citation [19].

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA
The first group of comparisons (Tab. 2, development set, in-vocabulary terms MTWV only) calibrates the recognizers themselves: DNN-HMM hybrid recognizer English 0.7899, Czech 0.8760; raw grapheme Wav2Vec English 0.6178, Czech 0.7248; Wav2Vec with external language model and beam search English 0.8105, Czech 0.8757. The conclusion is clear: the raw grapheme model is indeed weaker (the cost of lacking language model constraints), but adding a language model can catch up to or even surpass the hybrid baseline, proving that the raw posterior quality of Wav2Vec is sufficient and worth pursuing the trainable route.

The second group of comparisons (Tab. 3, development set MTWV, grapheme STD method family) provides a complete ladder of method evolution: heuristic method (citation [4]) English 0.4636, Czech 0.6225; Siamese Neural Network (citation [18]) English 0.5012, Czech 0.6547; Deep LSTM plus DNN-HMM (citation [13]) English 0.6703, Czech 0.7723; This paper's method (with respective optimal masks) English 0.8308, Czech 0.8987. Compared to their own system from the previous year, English has an absolute improvement of 16.05 percentage points and a relative improvement of about 23.9%; Czech has an absolute improvement of 12.64 percentage points and a relative improvement of about 16.4%—the "significant surpassing" mentioned in the paper's abstract refers to this. Breaking it down, this paper's method has in-vocabulary terms English 0.8398, Czech 0.9117, and out-of-vocabulary terms English 0.7027, Czech 0.8784: in-vocabulary is better than the Wav2Vec baseline with language model (0.8105/0.8757), and out-of-vocabulary drops only slightly from in-vocabulary—and out-of-vocabulary terms are inherently unsearchable for all vocabulary-based recognizers, representing a qualitative change from nothing to something here.

The third group of comparisons (Tab. 4, test set ATWV) is the final answer: heuristic method English 0.4435, Czech 0.6564; Siamese network English 0.4956, Czech 0.6873; Deep LSTM plus DNN-HMM English 0.5823, Czech 0.7531; DNN-HMM baseline empowered with language model and vocabulary English 0.7275, Czech 0.8283; This paper's method English 0.7616, Czech 0.9100. Note the significance of the last row: even allowing the old baseline to use the complete arsenal of language model plus vocabulary to search for in-vocabulary words, this paper's dictionary-free system still wins by 3.41 percentage points for English and 8.17 percentage points for Czech, while also being able to search for out-of-vocabulary words. The decay magnitude from development to test set (English drops from 0.8308 to 0.7616, Czech rises slightly from 0.8987 to 0.9100) suggests a larger generalization gap for English, possibly related to the higher OOV rate of 3.2% in the English test set (author's inference, not analyzed in the paper).

### Findings from Ablation Experiments
The paper's ablation focuses on mask width (Tab. 3): English no mask 0.8198, +/-1 mask 0.8308, +/-2 mask 0.7873, +/-3 mask 0.7293; Czech no mask 0.8823, +/-1 mask 0.8888, +/-2 mask 0.8987, +/-3 mask 0.8790. Two findings are worth chewing on. First, masking is generally effective but has an optimum: from no mask to optimal mask, English improves by 1.10 percentage points and Czech by 1.64 percentage points, verifying that CTC boundary noise is indeed polluting the supervision signal; but if the mask is too wide (+/-3), English collapses to 0.7293, which is 9.05 percentage points lower than no mask—masking too many segments is equivalent to throwing away true supervision near the boundaries, and the network loses the chance to learn "precise boundaries," indicating this is a trade-off between bias and variance rather than a monotonic improvement. Second, the optimum is language-dependent (English +/-1, Czech +/-2). Czech requires a wider mask, consistent with its more complex grapheme-to-phoneme mapping and higher morphological variation (author's inference); this also reminds practitioners that this hyperparameter must be re-tuned on the development set for their respective languages and cannot be copied blindly. Additionally, the decomposition of in-vocabulary vs. out-of-vocabulary terms (0.8398 vs 0.7027, English) can be seen as a type of ablation: the performance drop in out-of-vocabulary terms mainly comes from the query side never having appeared in the dictionary form during training, relying only on grapheme generalization, but the drop magnitude is far smaller than the "zero score for OOV" in traditional systems.

## Main Contributions
First, methodologically, it proposes and validates a fully trainable end-to-end STD framework of "grapheme Wav2Vec plus Deep LSTM embedding scoring": the retrieval system itself is completely freed from pronunciation dictionaries and language models, and out-of-vocabulary word retrieval becomes an built-in capability rather than an external patch. Second, it proposes a knowledge transfer pipeline for bootstrapping query training from unannotated archives, distilling the knowledge of DNN-HMM and its underlying pronunciation dictionary and language model into the scoring network in the form of teacher annotations, allowing training data to be unlimitedly extended to the entire archive with zero manual annotation. Third, it provides a complete algorithm for constructing frame-aligned grapheme confusion networks from CTC posterior matrices (Eqs. (1)(2)), and verifies the practical usability of greedy CTC alignment for the STD task. Fourth, engineering-wise, it solves the limitation of Transformer quadratic complexity on input length using 18-second windows, 3-second overlap, and half-split posterior sliding window decoding, and absorbs quantified CTC alignment errors (shifts not exceeding five graphemes) using boundary masking. Fifth, it significantly refreshes its own records on both English and Czech in the MALACH archive (test set ATWV English 0.7616, Czech 0.9100, improving over the old baseline by 17.93 and 15.69 percentage points, respectively), and open-sources the 80,000-hour pre-trained Czech Wav2Vec 2.0 model.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method
First, the system does not truly eliminate the dependency on DNN-HMM, but merely shifts the dependency from the retrieval phase to the training phase: creating training queries still requires a hybrid recognizer with a pronunciation dictionary and language model, so the cold-start cost of the pipeline still exists for languages without such resources. Second, masking is an empirical remedy for CTC alignment errors, with width determined by grid search on the development set. "Shifts not exceeding five graphemes" is an observational conclusion rather than a guarantee, and this assumption may fail when changing models or languages. Third, the posterior at the window splice point in sliding window decoding comes from two different attention contexts. The quality of grapheme posteriors near the 1.5-second split point theoretically degrades, and the paper does not perform boundary error analysis on this. Fourth, out-of-vocabulary term performance is still systematically lower than in-vocabulary terms (English development set 0.7027 vs 0.8398). Pure grapheme representation lacks phonetic-level normalization, and homophones with different spellings and cross-morphological variants (especially the rich inflectional changes in Czech) must be digested by the scoring network itself. Fifth, since word separators are merged with blank symbols, query representation is word-atomic, and the paper does not discuss the extensibility for multi-word phrase queries. Sixth, the metrics most concerned with archive-level deployment—indexing time, retrieval real-time rate, embedding storage volume, and model parameter count—are all unreported in the paper. The fact that Wav2Vec needs to perform a forward pass on the entire archive implies a significant offline computational bill. Seventh, the two pipelines are pure bidirectional LSTMs, which are naturally unsuitable for streaming scenarios; new audio must pass through the entire network to obtain embeddings.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design
The evaluation scale is small: the development and test sets each have only 10 speakers and 11 to 20 hours of data. English OOV query terms are particularly scarce (31 in development, 6 in test), and the statistical confidence of the English out-of-vocabulary conclusion 0.7027/0.7027 is questionable. In contrast, the Czech numbers (1145 development, 948 test out-of-vocabulary terms) are much more solid. No significance tests or confidence intervals were provided for all comparisons; some gaps (e.g., the 0.99 percentage point difference between Czech +/-1 mask 0.8888 and +/-2 mask 0.8987) fall within the range of possible random fluctuations. The domain is singular: all experiments are limited to the MALACH oral history domain (difficult acoustic conditions with high word error rates of 19 to 24), and generalization to other domains such as conferences and broadcasts is unverified. Hyperparameters (mask width, threshold) are tuned on the development set and reported on the test set. While the process is correct, the small development set poses a risk of overfitting to it. Additionally, the paper does not report the word error rate of the Wav2Vec fine-tuned model itself on MALACH, making it impossible to disentangle the sensitivity of the end-to-end STD performance to recognizer quality. There is also a lack of head-to-head comparison with contemporaneous end-to-end open-vocabulary keyword retrieval works (such as the acoustic feature projection scheme in citation [11]).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement
Following the paper's own framework, possible extensions include: replacing the LSTM encoder with Conformer or causal Transformers friendly to streaming, and introducing chunked inference to cover scenarios where new audio is archived near-real-time; introducing phoneme-aware features (concatenation of phoneme embeddings and grapheme embeddings) on the query side to bridge the remaining ~14 percentage point gap between out-of-vocabulary and in-vocabulary terms; upgrading the mask from fixed width to a learnable or soft mask adaptive to local posterior entropy, replacing grid search on the development set; the bootstrap teacher for training queries can also be upgraded to a stronger combination of fine-tuned Wav2Vec plus language model, further raising the upper limit of unannotated knowledge. At the evaluation level, it is worth re-verifying the language-dependence conclusion of mask width on more languages and larger evaluation sets, and supplementing systematic benchmarks for indexing speed, storage, and retrieval latency. At the resource level, the 80,000-hour Czech pre-trained model open-sourced by the authors provides a ready-made foundation for speech self-supervised research in low-resource Slavic languages. Combining it with multilingual pre-training (cross-lingual transfer) is a natural question: whether the representation scheme of grapheme confusion networks plus joint embedding space can become a language-agnostic STD intermediate layer. The paper's bilingual evidence gives a preliminary affirmative answer, but it is far from closed.
