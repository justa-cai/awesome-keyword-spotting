- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.04635
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, linearized convolution, streaming convolution, hardware efficiency, int8 quantization, DSP profiling, always-on inference

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the gatekeeper component for voice assistants: it runs continuously on-device, activating the assistant to listen to full voice commands only after detecting a predefined wake word (Section 1). This "always-on" attribute imposes engineering constraints on KWS that are fundamentally different from general speech tasks—it is the only neural network in the device that consumes power and compute resources 24/7/365. Its computational and memory overheads directly impact battery life and storage budgets. For microcontroller-level devices with limited battery capacity and memory space, an efficient KWS model is a prerequisite for productization.

The paper identifies a more subtle pain point: **the misalignment between theoretical computational cost (MACs) and real-world hardware efficiency**. In academic KWS model optimization, multiply-accumulates (MACs) are conventionally used as the efficiency metric, leading to a trend of endlessly compressing models. However, the authors point out in Section 1 that such theoretical figures "are often not interpretable as real hardware efficiency." The reason is that ML operators exhibit **heterogeneous efficiency profiles** on low-power processors—the same single multiply-accumulate operation consumes different clock cycles on a real chip depending on whether it occurs in a convolution operator or a fully connected layer. Convolution hardware efficiency is often lower than expected, whereas linear operators (matrix-vector multiplication) benefit from decades of manual optimization by processor manufacturers, allowing them to fully saturate the compute units. In some cases, the hardware efficiency of linear operators even surpasses the level implied by their MACs count. This leads to a counter-intuitive phenomenon (Section 1): **an MLP with higher MACs may run faster on hardware than a CNN with lower MACs**. Accepting this fact necessitates rewriting the entire paradigm of edge-side model design based on "MACs supremacy."

The abstract provides two empirical facts from the hardware side as the foundation for the paper's argument: given the same theoretical computational cost, int8 operators consume less compute power than float operators; and linear layers are generally more efficient than other layer types. Combined with the modeling-side fact that "convolutions have strong modeling capabilities," these three facts constitute the entire design starting point of LiCo-Net.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper systematically reviews the shortcomings of four major categories of KWS modeling schemes in Section 1, each pointing to the dilemma where "modeling capability" and "hardware affinity" cannot be achieved simultaneously:

- **MLP / Fully Connected Networks**: Simple model structure and efficient hardware support (Abstract, Section 1), but limited by model capacity, often requiring large input sizes to function barely, resulting in a low accuracy ceiling. Experimental results in Figure 3 later confirm that their detection performance lags significantly.
- **RNN / Recurrent Networks**: Better performance than MLPs, but their dependency on previous time-step states is "non-determined," leading to unstable predictions for always-on streaming data. More critically, non-linear operators within RNNs are inefficient on low-power hardware and are fragile to static int8 quantization (Section 1). For always-on systems, performance collapse after quantization is almost a death sentence.
- **CNN**: Friendly to int8 quantization and high model performance, but the computational overhead is usually too high for always-on KWS (Section 1).
- **Streaming CNNs (including SVDF, S1DCNN)**: This is the closest predecessor route to LiCo-Net. Streaming inference ([13][14]) ensures that the output at each time step depends only on a small segment of new input and historical cache, significantly reducing computation; SVDF uses depthwise separable convolutions and 1D convolutions to further improve efficiency ([15][16]), achieving small footprint and high detection quality in terms of both MACs and model size. However, the paper's core criticism is: **these methods still optimize convolution-type operators**, and convolution is precisely the type of operator that is "less efficient than expected" on DSPs. SVDF blocks still mix various operators such as depthwise convolutions over the memory dimension and bottleneck layers. The overhead caused by operator heterogeneity is not eliminated but merely masked by the MACs figure.

In summary, the shortcomings of existing methods can be summarized in one sentence: everyone is compressing MACs, but no one is facing the hardware fact that "operator type determines the real number of cycles per MAC."

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The paper aims to have its cake and eat it too: **CNN-level modeling capability (accuracy) + pure linear operator-level hardware efficiency (cycles)**, while satisfying three hard constraints: First, the transformation must be mathematically strictly equivalent rather than approximate; any loss of accuracy would render the "two-stage" design meaningless. Second, the model must operate in a streaming paradigm (internal state + small chunk of new input), because always-on KWS does not allow recalculating the entire overlapping audio segment for each sliding window. Third, the choice of inference stride $s$ involves a trade-off between "computation frequency vs. decision latency," and the architecture must allow free selection along this trade-off axis without collapsing accuracy. The essence of the challenge is to losslessly rewrite an operator class originally designed for "modeling expression" (convolution) into another operator class designed for "hardware throughput" (linear layers) at deployment time, and to build a provably equivalent bridge between them.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The top-level design of LiCo-Net is a **dual-phase system**: running in streaming convolution form during the training phase, and equivalently linearizing all convolutions into int8 linear operators during the inference phase (Abstract, Section 2.2). The design motivation stems from the hardware observations at the beginning—convolution parameterization is strong in modeling (weight sharing over time, expressing temporal translation equivariance, mature training infrastructure), while linear operators are fast on chips (full SIMD filling, regular memory access). Thus, let each phase use its optimal form, with mathematical equivalence ensuring the function remains exactly the same in the middle.

The main network consists of $L$ LiCo-Blocks stacked sequentially, followed by a linear classifier (Sections 2.2, 3.2). The stacking rules follow Proposition 1 later: the first block uses stride $s_1 \ge 1$, and all remaining blocks have stride 1; in practice, streaming convolution is adopted, and the chunk size is forced to $t = s_1$ to achieve equivalent linearization (Section 2.2). Inside each LiCo-Block is a bottleneck structure composed of three 1D convolution layers: the first layer has a kernel width greater than 1 and expands channels by an expansion factor $e > 1$, followed by two pointwise (1x1) convolutions, with ReLU introducing non-linearity between layers (Sections 2.2, Figure 2); when $s = 1$, residual connections are additionally introduced to improve gradient propagation (Section 2.2).

Specific configurations given in the experimental section (Section 3.2): The large model stacks 5 blocks, with $e = 6$, kernel width $K = 5$, and channel width $w = 32$, resulting in 96.6K parameters; the small model also stacks 5 blocks, with $e = 4$, $K = 4$, $w = 16$, resulting in 28.4K parameters. The role of the expansion factor is to control the inner diameter of the expanded representation within the block: first, the kernel convolution expands 32 channels to a high-dimensional space like 192 (for the large model) for non-linear transformation, and then the pointwise convolution compresses it back to the bottleneck width, buying sufficient expressive power with very few parameters—this is the meaning of "bottleneck between the layers to further boost modeling effectiveness" (Section 2.2). Residual connections are added only when $s = 1$, which makes sense from the perspective of shape alignment: the first block with stride greater than 1 downsamples the time dimension, so the number of input and output frames is no longer equal, and residual shortcuts cannot be directly established; when the stride is 1, the shapes are naturally aligned, making residuals possible.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

All mathematics do one thing: prove that "streaming 1D convolution" can be **exactly rewritten** as a "linear layer" under specific conditions, and clarify those conditions.

**Step 1: Formalization of Streaming Convolution (Section 2.1, Equations 1-3)**. Input $X \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T}$ ($C$ features, $T$ frames), convolved with kernel $W \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times C \times K}$ and stride $s$ as a 1D convolution:

$$Y_{d,i} = \sum_{c=0}^{C-1}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1} W_{d,c,k}\, X_{c,\,s\times i+k}$$

Real-time temporal audio does not arrive all at once. The $T$ frames of input are cut into $N$ chunks of size $t$ arriving sequentially (Section 2.1). Since causal kernels are used, a segment of output can be calculated incrementally with each new chunk; if $t$ is a multiple of $s$, each chunk produces exactly $t/s$ output elements. To align the streaming output with non-streaming calculation point-by-point, the $j$-th chunk is concatenated with the most recent $K-s$ frames of history:

$$Y^{(j)} = \mathrm{conv}(X^{(j)}, W, s),\quad X^{(j)} = [\,X_{:,\,jt-(K-s):jt},\; X_{:,\,jt:(j+1)t}\,]$$

where $Y^{(j)} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times t/s}$, and the complete output is the concatenation of each chunk $Y = [Y^{(0)}, \dots, Y^{(J-1)}]$ (Equation 3). This history $X_{:,jt-(K-s):jt}$ is maintained as an **internal state**. Figure 1 intuitively shows the waste saved: in non-streaming sliding window inference, there is significant overlap of window - shift between adjacent input windows that repeatedly participates in calculation; streaming convolution equivalently produces output using "small chunk of new content + cached historical state," completely eliminating redundant calculations of overlapping parts.

**Step 2: Linearization Conditions (Section 2.2, Equation 4)**. The key observation of the paper is: **when the input length is exactly equal to the kernel width, a standard 1D convolution is equivalent to a linear layer**. Reshape the kernel $W \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times C \times K}$ into $\tilde{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{CK \times D}$, and flatten the input chunk $X^{(j)} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times K}$ into $\tilde{X}^{(j)} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times CK}$, then:

$$Y^{(j)} = \mathrm{linear}(\tilde{X}^{(j)}, \tilde{W})$$

The streaming scenario provides a natural match: when the chunk size $t$ equals the stride $s$, the input of each block consists of $K-s$ frames of history plus $s$ frames of new data, with a length of exactly $(K-s)+s = K$, which is exactly equal to the kernel width—the convolution produces only one output position on each block, thus "convolution" degenerates into a pure matrix-vector multiplication.

**Proposition 1 (Section 2.2)**: A streaming CNN can be equivalently converted to an MLP if and only if: (1) the stride $s$ of the first 1D convolution layer equals the chunk size $t$; (2) the stride of all other layers is 1. The two conditions serve their respective purposes: Condition 1 ensures that the input length of the first layer is locked to the kernel width $K$, triggering linearization; Condition 2 ensures that subsequent layers do not change the frame rate, so the input of each block is always "history + 1 frame of new data." Pointwise convolutions (kernel width 1) are naturally linear layers at any input length, and layers with kernel width greater than 1 can also be linearized because the input length is always equal to the kernel width (text in Figure 2: "Given an input of length K, each convolution layer can be equivalently linearized").

It is worth emphasizing the **nature** of this transformation: it is merely a reshaping of the weight tensor and flattening of the input, introducing no approximation and losing no accuracy. The deployed linear network and the training convolution network are the same function mathematically, which fundamentally distinguishes it from "lossy compression" routes like distillation, pruning, and low-rank approximation.

The stride $s$ has clear engineering semantics in this framework: it is the inference frequency (Section 2.2)—with features arriving every 10ms, $s=1$ means inference per frame, and $s=3$ means inference every 30ms. Larger $s$ means lower inference frequency and less compute power, but may bring higher latency. This trade-off axis is proven to be almost lossless in later experiments.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Operator-Level Hardware-Aware Architecture Design Philosophy

The first innovation is less about a specific module and more about a **design stance that treats "real cycles per MAC" as a first-class citizen**. The paper does not invent new convolution variants to compress MACs, but first profiles on a real DSP to obtain the empirical fact that "int8 linear operator efficiency surpasses convolution" (Abstract, Section 1), and then lets the architecture design obey this fact: **only linear operators are allowed to appear in the inference graph**. This explains why LiCo-Net dares to accept MACs similar to SVDF (Table 1: large model 95.3K vs. 90.7K) while claiming significantly faster measured speed—it bets not on computational volume, but on the exchange rate of computational volume on the chip. The data in Table 1 later fully cashes in on this positioning: calculated from Table 1, for the large model with stride=1, SVDF takes 152,458 cycles for 90.7K MACs (approx. 1.68 cycles/MAC), LiCo-Net takes 93,622 cycles for 95.3K MACs (approx. 0.98 cycles/MAC), and MLP takes 48,891 cycles for 96.3K MACs (approx. 0.51 cycles/MAC)—for the same multiply-accumulate, the number of cycles exchanged differs by more than 3 times depending on the operator, which is the most convincing set of evidence in the paper.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: LiCo-Block Bottleneck Structure

LiCo-Block (Figure 2) is the minimal modeling paradigm tailored for "linearizability": three 1D convolution layers, the first layer has a kernel width greater than 1 and performs channel expansion ($e > 1$), followed by two pointwise convolutions, with ReLU sandwiched in between, and residual connections added when $s=1$. Every cut of the design has a clear "why":

- **Why only the first layer uses a large kernel**: Mixing of temporal context only needs to happen once. Placing it at the entrance encodes $K$ frames of acoustic context into a single-frame representation, allowing subsequent layers to use pointwise convolutions with kernel width 1 (pure channel mixing), naturally satisfying Condition 2 of Proposition 1, while concentrating kernel parameters where temporal receptive field is most needed.
- **Why the expansion factor $e$**: Pointwise convolutions have extremely high parameter efficiency but limited single-layer expressiveness. First expanding the representation width with $e>1$ (large model 32→192 channels) and then performing non-linearity is equivalent to increasing the hidden layer width within the bottleneck budget, which is a transplant of mobile convolution design (similar to MobileNet inverted bottleneck idea) to the KWS streaming scenario.
- **Why residual connections only when $s=1$**: As mentioned earlier, blocks with $s>1$ downsample the time dimension and have shape misalignment; blocks with $s=1$ add residual connections to improve gradient propagation (Section 2.2), which is necessary for training stability of deep networks stacked with 5 blocks.

The block design also implies a beautiful unified perspective (Section 3.2): the baseline MLP takes 21 frames as input with two hidden layers, which is exactly "equivalent to a single LiCo-Block with kernel width 21." That is, **MLP is a degenerate special case of LiCo-Net at $L=1, K=21$**, and LiCo-Net is a strict generalization of MLP along the direction of "multi-block, small kernel, streamable"—this provides internal consistency for the three to share the same linearization theory.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Dual-Phase Deployment Paradigm

Separating the training state (streaming convolution) from the inference state (int8 linear operator chain) is the most pragmatic engineering contribution of this paper. The training side continues to use the mature convolution training stack (weight sharing brings better gradient statistics and generalization priors, residual and bottleneck structures are still usable, 8-GPU Adam large-batch training requires no special handling, Section 3.2); the deployment side rewrites the entire network into a pure GEMV operator chain, directly hitting a type of kernel that is most polished on DSPs. The two states are connected by the lossless transformation of Proposition 1, requiring only one weight rearrangement in the model file. This paradigm decouples "modeling-friendly" and "hardware-friendly" from mutually exclusive options into two orthogonal degrees of freedom.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Vs. SVDF**: Each SVDF block consists of an SVDF layer plus a bottleneck layer (Section 3.2), and the operator composition is still a hybrid of "matrix decomposition + memory-dimension depth convolution + bottleneck"; LiCo-Net has no convolution operators in the inference state, only linear layers. Table 1 shows that under similar parameter counts (92K vs. 96.6K), LiCo-Net is 1.5~1.8 times faster on HiFi4 (Section 4.2), and the gap comes entirely from the operator exchange rate.
- **Vs. MLP**: The inference graph is also pure linear operators; MLP even halves the cycle count (Section 4.2: LiCo-Net spends about 2 times more cycles than MLP, because MLP has "far fewer operators, especially linear operators," with only three large matrix multiplications per inference and very low fixed overhead); but MLP's capacity bottleneck causes its accuracy to lag significantly (Figure 3). LiCo-Net buys CNN-level accuracy through convolution-state training, paying only the lowest hardware cost of the linear operator chain.
- **Vs. Streaming CNNs (S1DCNN, etc.)**: Both perform streaming inference and similarly eliminate repeated calculations of overlapping windows (Figure 1), but retain convolution operators; LiCo-Net pushes "streaming" one step further to "linearization," which is the essential divide between the two at the hardware level.
- **Vs. Quantization/Compression Routes**: Binarization (e.g., BiFSMN), pruning, and low-rank decomposition all work on "compressing values/parameters" and inevitably introduce accuracy loss and retraining costs; LiCo-Net takes an architecture-level route of "rewriting operator classes," with zero accuracy loss, and is orthogonal to quantization and can be superimposed (all models in the experiment are already int8 quantized).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The experiment uses an internally aggregated and de-identified dataset (Section 3.1), with the wake word being the generic phrase "hey operator." Positive samples were recorded by crowdsourced workers, totaling 95K utterances; split in a speaker-independent manner, the training set has 84,354 utterances / 10,047 speakers, and the test set has 10,765 utterances / 1,202 speakers. Negative samples use multilingual non-wake-word corpora: 306 hours on the training side and 194 hours on the test side. Background noise and speed perturbation augmentations are applied to the training data to improve diversity. Acoustic features are 40-dimensional Log-Mel Energy (LMEL), extracted with a 25ms window and 10ms step, followed by global normalization (Section 3.1). Choosing 40-dimensional LMEL instead of MFCC is a common practice in edge-side KWS—retaining energy amplitude information and skipping DCT is more friendly to small networks. It should be noted that this dataset is Meta's internal data and is not publicly available, which constitutes a reproducibility issue for later discussion.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Detection Performance**: DET curve (Figure 3), with the horizontal axis being the number of False Alarms per Hour (#FAs/Hr) and the vertical axis being the False Rejection Rate (FRR). The rationale is rooted in product semantics: an always-on system may produce several false triggers per hour of operation, and FA/Hr directly corresponds to the user's perceived frequency of "the speaker waking up by itself"; FRR corresponds to the frustration of the user shouting the wake word but getting no response (Section 1 explicitly states that failing to wake up is a frustrating experience). The decision score comes from an external decoder similar to [19], which aggregates smooth frame-level posteriors within a 1.1s window into a final detection score between 0 and 1 (Section 3.2)—this layer of smoothing also explains why large strides hardly hurt accuracy.
- **Hardware Efficiency**: Cycles per DSP inference and MIPS (Section 3.2). All models are uniformly quantized to 8 bits, and profiled using the toolchain-provided cycle-accurate instruction set simulator on the Cadence Tensilica HiFi4 DSP core (Reference [20], NXP i.MX RT600 crossover MCU); all operators use optimized kernels (e.g., depthwise convolution implemented with SIMD) to ensure fair comparison. The semantics of MIPS can be inferred from Table 1 data: features arrive every 10ms, so with stride=1, there are 100 inferences per second. For the large LiCo-Net model, 93,622 cycles × 100 = 9.36 MIPS, which matches exactly with the value listed in Table 1; with stride=3, there are 33.3 inferences per second, and 94,556 × 33.3 ≈ 3.12 MIPS also matches. That is, **MIPS = Cycles per Inference × Inference Frequency**, directly corresponding to the continuous load of always-on operation (and thus related to power budget).
- **Training Protocol (Section 3.2)**: The target is 11 classes—9 sub-words of the wake word, SIL, and FILLER; labels are generated by forced alignment with a pre-trained acoustic model; batch size 256, 8 GPUs, Adam optimizer, learning rate 0.01, $\beta=(0.9, 0.98)$. The number of training epochs is not reported in the paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The comparison objects are three types of models: LiCo-Net, SVDF, and MLP, each with large and small versions (Section 3.2): Large LiCo-Net (5 blocks, $e=6/K=5/w=32$, 96.6K), Small LiCo-Net ($e=4/K=4/w=16$, 28.4K); Large SVDF (5 blocks, each block one SVDF layer + bottleneck layer, $K=5/256$ filters/bottleneck 32, 92K), Small SVDF ($K=4/128$ filters/bottleneck 16, 26.9K); Large MLP (21 frames input, two hidden layers 80/320 neurons, 96.7K), Small MLP (40/320, 50.3K). Parameter counts are deliberately aligned within the same version (except for Small MLP, 50.3K is close to twice that of the other two, which the paper explicitly acknowledges in Section 4.2). Each type of model is experimented with under two settings for the first layer stride: $s=1$ and $s=3$.

(Original data from Table 1) Core conclusion: Under similar MACs and strides, LiCo-Net is 1.5~1.8 times faster than SVDF, and spends about 2 times more cycles than MLP (Section 4.2). The "40% reduction in cycles on HiFi4" claimed in the abstract calculates from Table 1 as: Large model stride=1 is $(152,458-93,622)/152,458 \approx 38.6\%$, stride=3 is $44.4\%$; Small model stride=1 is $33.0\%$, stride=3 is $39.4\%$—consistent with the 40% magnitude in the abstract. Another observation calculated from Table 1: The cycles per inference for LiCo-Net and MLP hardly change with stride (93,622 vs. 94,556; 48,891 vs. 49,258), because stride only reduces inference frequency and does not change the calculation amount of state plus new chunk per inference; MIPS therefore decreases inversely by approximately 3 times (Large LiCo-Net 9.36→3.12). This precisely locates the benefit of stride in the "continuous load/power" dimension rather than the single-inference latency dimension. There is also an anomaly in the table not explained by the paper: SVDF's MACs actually increase when stride=3 (Small model 26.3K→36.5K, Large model 90.7K→111.2K), while LiCo-Net and MLP's MACs remain unchanged; combined with the reminder in Section 4.2 that "operator implementation efficiency highly depends on hardware design and specifications," this implies that SVDF may require heavier state processing under large strides, the mechanism of which is not reported in the paper.

**Accuracy Side (Figure 3 DET Curve)**: The large version of each model outperforms the small version; the gap between the two LiCo-Nets narrows significantly when stride=3. SVDF and LiCo-Net have comparable performance under most settings, with the Small LiCo-Net surpassing the Small SVDF when stride=3—improving FRR from 2% to 1.2% at the 0.025 FA/Hr operating point (Figure 3, Section 4.1), indicating that LiCo-Net has stronger modeling power under small parameter counts. Both SVDF and LiCo-Net significantly outperform MLP (Section 4.1), confirming the judgment that "pure MLP capacity is insufficient." The most practical finding is: **large strides have almost no impact on the performance of all models** (Section 4.1)—this is an attractive property of "exchanging larger strides for better computational efficiency without sacrificing model performance" (original wording), the mechanism of which can be explained by the 1.1s external decoding smoothing window: a 30ms decision granularity is dense enough relative to the hundreds of milliseconds duration of keywords.

Synthesizing both dimensions, Small LiCo-Net + stride=3 is the most cost-effective point: 61,154 cycles / 2.01 MIPS (Table 1), with accuracy still better than SVDF under the same setting (Figure 3).

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have an independent ablation table; the de facto ablation consists of two sets of controls:

- **Stride Ablation (s=1 vs. s=3, Figure 3 + Table 1)**: As mentioned above, performance is almost lossless, and MIPS drops by about 3 times. This is a direct verification of the architecture's freedom in Proposition 1 ("first layer stride is adjustable").
- **Model Scale Ablation (Large vs. Small, Figure 3)**: The large model dominates comprehensively, but the gap between the large and small versions of LiCo-Net narrows significantly when stride=3 (Section 4.1), suggesting that under large strides, the small model is already approaching the performance saturation zone of this architecture.

Formal ablation of key architecture hyperparameters—number of blocks $L$, expansion factor $e$, kernel width $K$, presence/absence of residual connections, scanning of chunk size—**is not reported in the paper**. Verification of numerical consistency between the output of the training-state convolution network and the inference-state linear network (e.g., maximum error per layer) is also **not reported in the paper**, although Proposition 1 guarantees equivalence mathematically; whether quantization rearrangement introduces numerical bias at the engineering implementation level remains an empirical blank.

## Main Contributions

1. **Provided the equivalent transformation path from streaming CNN to MLP and its mathematical conditions** (First item in the contribution list in Section 1, Proposition 1): Proved that as long as the stride of the first layer equals the chunk size and the stride of all other layers is 1, the entire streaming convolution network can be losslessly rewritten as a pure linear operator network. This is both the theorem of this paper and a general modification criterion that can be reused by any streaming model.
2. **Proposed LiCo-Block and LiCo-Net** (Second and third items in the contribution list): Achieved linearizable high-capacity modeling with a bottleneck sandwich structure of "one large-kernel expansion convolution + pointwise convolution," which, when stacked into a network, is measured to be 1.5~1.8 times faster than SVDF on HiFi4 with comparable detection performance (Table 1, Figure 3).
3. **Confirmed the assertion that "MACs does not equal hardware efficiency" with systematic experiments** (Section 1, Table 1): Quantitatively demonstrated the difference in cycle exchange rates of more than 3 times among different operator classes under the same MACs, providing a methodological correction from an operator-level perspective for edge-side model design.
4. **Dual-phase (convolution-state training / linear-state inference) deployment paradigm**: Decoupled modeling-friendliness and hardware-friendliness, with zero accuracy loss in transformation, and can be promoted as a general efficient architecture to other always-on speech tasks with strict latency and power budgets (original text in Section 5 conclusion).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Strong hardware binding of conclusions**: All cycle counts come from the simulator of a single HiFi4 core (Section 3.2), and the paper itself acknowledges in Section 4.2 that "operator implementation efficiency highly depends on hardware design and specifications, and the gap between SVDF and LiCo-Net may be different on another chip." Before cross-platform data such as ARM Cortex-M and HiFi5 are available, "linear operators are faster" should be viewed as an empirical conclusion on HiFi4 rather than a universal law.
- **Architecture freedom locked by Proposition 1**: Equivalent linearization requires all strides to be 1 except for the first layer, which means that common temporal modeling methods such as multi-scale downsampling stacking, pooling, and dilated convolutions are either unavailable or need to be modified first to enter this framework; the paper does not discuss the cost of this constraint on the design space.
- **Cost of maintaining consistency of dual-phase artifacts**: The deployed linear network is a rearranged product of the training convolution network, and the two must be strictly synchronized in version; whether quantization before and after rearrangement introduces numerical differences is not verified in the paper. In addition, it is not reported whether 8-bit quantization uses post-training quantization or quantization-aware training, which directly affects reproducible accuracy and cycle counts.
- **Still a 2x cycle gap relative to MLP** (Section 4.2): In extreme power scenarios, if MLP-level accuracy loss can be accepted, pure MLP is still the more power-saving choice; LiCo-Net's positioning is the "optimal compromise when accuracy-sensitive," not the absolute lowest power architecture.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Single wake word, single internal dataset**: Only the word "hey operator" and Meta's internal 95K utterance corpus (Section 3.1), not validated on public benchmarks such as Google Speech Commands, making it impossible for external researchers to reproduce or directly compare with SOTA numbers in the literature.
- **Narrow baseline coverage**: Only compared SVDF and MLP (Section 3.2), while S1DCNN mentioned in the introduction, as well as contemporaneous stronger streaming CNNs (TC-ResNet, MatchboxNet, BC-ResNet series) did not enter the field; it is questionable whether "better than SVDF" can be generalized to "better than the streaming CNN family."
- **Weak accuracy quantification**: Performance evidence is the DET curve plus a single operating point reading (FRR 2%→1.2% at 0.025 FA/Hr, Figure 3), with no AUC, no multi-operating-point numerical table, no statistical significance, and no variance across multiple random seeds, which is not reported in the paper.
- **Lack of real-machine metrics**: No measured power consumption, no measured latency, no memory/peak activation usage (for MCU deployment, SRAM is often a harder constraint than cycles), all relying on instruction set simulators, which is not reported in the paper.
- **Unequal parameters in the small model group**: Small MLP has 50.3K parameters, approximately twice that of Small SVDF/Small LiCo-Net (Section 3.2, Section 4.2), which, although acknowledged by the paper and used as an argument for "MLP has fewer operators so it is faster," makes the three-way comparison in the small model group not clean enough.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Cross-chip and real-machine verification**: Re-measure cycles and power consumption on more cores such as Cortex-M series MCUs and HiFi5 to test the platform robustness of the linear operator advantage, which is the necessary path for the paper's conclusions to move towards engineering consensus.
- **Operator fusion and mixed precision**: The inference state is already a pure linear chain; further operator fusion of adjacent linear layers and ReLU can be performed, and mixed precision int8/int4 can be done based on layer sensitivity to further compress cycles per MAC and weight volume; such optimizations are orthogonal to and can be superimposed on LiCo's architecture route.
- **Completing hyperparameter ablation**: Systematically scan the number of blocks $L$, expansion factor $e$, kernel width $K$, presence/absence of residuals, and chunk/stride to find the Pareto frontier of accuracy-cycles, and expand "stride is almost lossless" from a single experimental point to a complete curve.
- **Task extrapolation**: The paper's Section 5 has already self-identified that LiCo-Net, as a general efficient architecture, can be migrated to other always-on speech tasks with strict latency and power budgets (such as edge-side VAD, acoustic event detection); the linearization conditions of Proposition 1 are equally applicable to streaming CNNs for these tasks, which is worth verifying one by one.
- **Combination with compression routes**: Combined with knowledge distillation (using a large convolutional network to teach a small LiCo-Net) and structured sparsity (linear layers are naturally suitable for row pruning), it may further raise the accuracy ceiling under the same cycle budget.

Appendix: Terminology Quick Reference—LMEL (Log-Mel Energy, 40-dimensional acoustic features commonly used on the edge); SVDF (Singular Value Decomposition Filter, a baseline architecture for streaming KWS using low-rank decomposition plus memory-dimension convolution); DET Curve (Detection Error Tradeoff curve, horizontal axis FA/Hr, vertical axis FRR); MIPS (Million Instructions Per Second, here equal to Cycles per Inference × Inference Frequency); chunk (the new input block arriving each time in streaming inference, size $t$); FRR (False Rejection Rate, the proportion of times the wake word is said but not triggered).
