# Keyword Spotting with Hyper-Matched Filters for Small Footprint Devices

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Yael Segal-Feldman, Joseph Keshet (Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Technion – Israel Institute of Technology); Ann R. Bradlow, Matthew Goldrick (Department of Linguistics, Northwestern University)
- **Date**: August 2025 (arXiv:2508.04857v1, submitted 2025-08-06)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.04857 (Code and trained models: https://github.com/YaelSegal/HyperSpotter)
- **Keywords**: open-vocabulary keyword spotting, hypernetwork, matched filter, Perceiver, cross-attention, small-footprint device, speech encoder

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the task of identifying a predefined set of target keywords from continuous speech. It serves as the core entry component for voice assistants on small-footprint devices such as smartphones and small smart speakers (the paper specifically mentions Amazon Alexa, Apple Siri, Microsoft Cortana, and Google’s Hey Google). Traditional KWS follows a closed-vocabulary paradigm: a fixed set of wake words is defined at the factory, and model training, quantization, and deployment are all centered around this specific set. The first pain point of this approach is the high iteration cost—if users or manufacturers want to add a new command word or wake word, they must retrain or maintain multiple models, preventing a "plug-and-play" experience. To address this, researchers have turned to open-vocabulary KWS: detecting words, terms, or phrases that were never seen in the training data, allowing users to add new keywords without retraining the model.

The second pain point is the resource budget of small-footprint devices. These devices have extremely limited storage and computing power, with on-device model parameters typically capped at the magnitude of millions (M). However, high-performance open-vocabulary KWS models in the literature are generally too large—for instance, AdaKWS (Navon et al., ICASSP 2024), the strongest baseline in this paper, requires storing 13.5M parameters on the device (Table II), which clearly exceeds the limit for small-footprint devices. The contradiction between accuracy and model size is the central battlefield of this paper.

The third pain point is out-of-domain generalization in real-world deployment environments. Training corpora often consist of read-aloud, studio-quality native speech, whereas real-world users include many non-native (L2) speakers, accented English, additive noise, and languages never seen during training. A KWS model that performs well on in-domain metrics may degrade significantly when faced with L2 speakers. The background of the paper’s collaborators from the Department of Linguistics (Bradlow and Goldrick from Northwestern University) also explains why the paper places L2 speaker evaluation at the core.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing work into four technical lines and points out their respective shortcomings:

- **Closed-vocabulary deep model line**: From early CNN and RNN encoders to TDNN+HMM, CNNs with residual connections, depthwise convolutions, multi-scale time-domain convolutions, sinc convolutions, slimmed CNNs, contextual biasing of ASR models, and wake word hot-fixing via model reprogramming. These methods have continuously pushed the accuracy-size frontier of small-footprint KWS but are all limited to a predefined keyword set, making the vocabulary non-expandable.
- **Query-by-Example (QbE) line**: Users register several audio samples of the target keyword, and the system builds a reference model based on this to detect keywords in new audio via similarity to the registered samples. QbE does not require text but is highly sensitive to changes in recording conditions—performance collapses quickly when the registration environment differs from the detection environment. Using TTS to synthesize variants of reference audio is similarly constrained by the mismatch between synthetic audio and real recordings.
- **ASR/CTC transcription line**: First, an acoustic encoder is trained using CTC loss, followed by full transcription and keyword phoneme sequence matching using minimum edit distance; or, an RNN-T encoder is used with "keyword start/end" marker tokens to locate keywords. The paper points out that performing full transcription just to detect a few keywords is an "overly complex" solution, with computational overhead mismatching the task scale.
- **Embedding space and hypernetwork line**: One class of methods (CED, EMKWS, CMCD, etc.) maps audio and keyword character/phoneme embeddings into a shared embedding space, using distance metrics or fusion networks to determine if a keyword is present; another class uses hypernetworks—where a main network receives phoneme or grapheme representations of keywords to generate weights for another network. Bluche et al. (Interspeech 2020) used an LSTM hypernetwork to generate weights for the last CNN layer of an audio encoder; while the model is indeed small (210K parameters on-device, Table VI), performance drops significantly on challenging target keywords. Navon et al.’s AdaKWS uses a hypernetwork to generate weights for the normalization layers of a complete two-layer Transformer, achieving excellent performance but resulting in a model that is too large for small-footprint devices.

In summary, two specific gaps in existing work are: (1) methods designed for small footprints ([20]-[22], [24]) lack accuracy on difficult keywords; (2) [25] (AdaKWS) has sufficient accuracy but exceeds size limits, and its original model and source code are not public, making community reproduction and verification difficult. Furthermore, most works do not systematically validate generalization on out-of-domain datasets, unseen languages, or L2 speakers.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

How to build an open-vocabulary KWS model with detection accuracy reaching or exceeding that of the 13.5M SOTA model, while keeping on-device parameters in the 4-6M range. Constraints include: new keywords are input as pure strings, requiring no retraining of the entire model; the keyword encoder itself does not need to be deployed on the device (keyword weights are compiled offline); and robustness is maintained under out-of-domain datasets (Speech Commands, low-resource languages in FLEURS), languages unseen during training, non-native speakers (L2 subset of Wildcat Diapix), and additive noise (white noise, pub noise). Essentially, this is a joint optimization problem of "openness, accuracy, size, and out-of-domain robustness," where previous works typically satisfied only two or three of these simultaneously.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

HyperSpotter consists of three sub-modules (Figure 1): a speech encoder, a target keyword encoder, and a detection network. Their roles are: the speech encoder converts audio into a general speech representation; the keyword encoder "compiles" character-form keywords into a set of convolutional weights for the detection network; and the detection network uses these two inputs to determine if the keyword is present in the recording.

**Speech Encoder**. The input features are 80-channel log-magnitude Mel-spectrograms, with a 25 ms window length and 10 ms frame shift; the speech signal $x$ consists of $T$ acoustic feature vectors, sampled one frame every 10 ms. The paper implements two encoders:

- **Tiny Whisper Encoder (HyperSpotter-w)**: Takes the encoder from the smallest "tiny" variant of the pre-trained Whisper family: 4 Transformer layers, 6 attention heads per layer, feature dimension $M=384$, outputting one frame every 20 ms, totaling approximately 7.6M parameters. Motivation: Whisper is trained on 680,000 hours of audio using large-scale weak supervision, ensuring robust and generalizable representations; after freezing and reusing it, the downstream task only requires training a lightweight detection head.
- **Tiny Conformer Encoder (HyperSpotter-c)**: 6-layer encoder, 4 attention heads, kernel size 3, feature dimension $M=144$, outputting one frame every 40 ms, totaling 3.7M parameters. Motivation: 7.6M is still too large for small-footprint devices; the Conformer’s hybrid structure of convolution and self-attention can provide effective representations even at small capacities. This encoder is not off-the-shelf—the authors first performed ASR pre-training on VoxPopuli and LibriSpeech train-clean-100/360 using CTC loss (250 epochs, Adam optimizer, learning rate 0.0001, batch size 96), along with three augmentations: frequency masking, time masking, and MUSAN additive noise (SNR randomly sampled between 5-15 dB, implemented in TorchAudio).

In the main experiments (VoxPopuli adaptation), both speech encoders are frozen, and only the detection network and keyword encoder are trained. Why freeze? First, to reuse knowledge from large-scale pre-training; second, to keep the training data requirements and parameter statistics for small-footprint scenarios cleaner.

**Target Keyword Encoder (Hypernetwork)**. The input is the character sequence $k$ of the target keyword. The structure is: character embedding layer (embedding dimension 161) → 4-layer LSTM (hidden dimension 256) → 2 linear layers projecting the LSTM hidden states to the convolutional dimensions of $W_k$, totaling 2.7M parameters. The key design is that its output is not a vector embedding of the keyword, but a complete set of convolutional layer weights $W_k$ ($|W_k|=1024$). Why not output an embedding vector? The paper’s core argument is that these weights can be viewed as a keyword-specific matched filter, with the design goal of maximizing the output signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for the target signal (here, the text-form target keyword); embedding vectors can only be "compared" in a shared space, whereas weights directly "participate in and alter" the computation of the detection network itself. From a deployment perspective, there is also an asymmetric advantage: small-footprint devices use a predefined keyword set, so the $W_k$ for a new keyword $k$ can be generated offline. Thus, the keyword encoder (2.7M parameters) does not need to be deployed on the device; each keyword on the device only occupies 1024 convolutional weights.

**Detection Network (Perceiver)**. Why choose Perceiver over a standard Transformer? Perceiver’s unique mechanism is cross-attention, which compresses high-dimensional inputs into a fixed-dimension latent bottleneck, followed by Transformer-style self-attention only on the bottleneck. This allows it to achieve Transformer-level modeling capabilities with far fewer parameters, fitting the needs of small-footprint devices. The original Perceiver’s mapping is two-stage: first projecting representations from high dimension $M$ to low dimension $N$ ($N \ll M$), then mapping variable length $B$ elements into fixed length $S$ vectors (independent of $B$). This paper’s mapping is three-stage, adding one step: (1) Project speech representation $z$ from $M$ (Whisper 384 / Conformer 144) to $N=64$; (2) Apply keyword-specific convolution (Conv component in Figure 1b); (3) Map variable length $B$ to fixed length $S=16$. The convolution configuration is kernel length 16, dilation 1, stride 1; both Whisper (20 ms resolution) and Conformer (40 ms resolution) versions use the same configuration; depthwise convolution is adopted, where each input channel has its own filter bank, so $|W_k| = 16 \times 64 = 1024$. Why use depthwise? To minimize the number of convolutional weights (standard convolution would be on the order of $16 \times 64 \times 64$) while retaining the effectiveness of convolution in capturing target keyword patterns. The number of layers in the detection network is a scalable knob: each Perceiver layer = one layer of cross-attention + one latent Transformer. The paper systematically evaluated 1 to 5 layers, and all result tables are labeled by layer count (e.g., HyperSpotter-w (5) denotes Whisper encoder + 5-layer Perceiver).

The overall data flow can be summarized in one sentence: Audio Mel-spectrograms pass through the frozen speech encoder to get $z$, which is down-projected to 64 dimensions and then scanned by keyword-specific matched filter convolution kernels; the convolution responses guide the Perceiver’s cross-attention to steer representations toward the target keyword, finally compressing into 16 fixed-length vectors to output the probability of keyword presence.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Signal and Mapping Chain**: $x \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times D}$ (one frame every 10 ms) → Speech Encoder → $z \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times M}$ → Linear Projection → $\mathbb{R}^{B \times 64}$ → Depthwise Convolution (weights $W_k \in \mathbb{R}^{1024}$) → Perceiver Cross-Attention + Latent Transformer → $\mathbb{R}^{16}$ Fixed-length Vector → Binary Classification Probability $\hat{y}$. $B$ is the number of frames output by the encoder (varying with audio length); the fixed-length output of $S=16$ allows the same network to handle inputs of arbitrary length.

**Matched Filter Perspective (Origin of the Paper’s Title)**: In classical signal detection theory, a matched filter is a linear filter that maximizes the output SNR under additive noise, with its impulse response determined by the known target signal (the paper cites Therrien’s textbook [47]). HyperSpotter migrates this idea to open-vocabulary KWS: the "target signal" is no longer a waveform template, but the text keyword itself—the hypernetwork learns a set of convolutional kernels $W_k$ from the character sequence $k$. When these kernels slide over the audio representation sequence, they produce strong responses at time positions where the keyword appears. The paper validates this explanation directly using the cross-attention heatmap in Figure 3: for the target keyword "legal basis", the high-probability blocks in the heatmap (vertical axis: Perceiver fixed dimension $S$, horizontal axis: time frames at 20 ms resolution) precisely align with the two occurrences of the word in the recording. In plain terms: this model does not "understand the whole sentence and then find the word"; instead, it customizes a sliding template detector for each keyword. Where the template response lights up, the word is located.

**Loss Function (Eq. 1)**: Both model variants are trained using Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE):

$$ L_{BCE} = -\frac{1}{E} \sum_{i=1}^{E} [ y_i \cdot \log \hat{y}_i + (1 - y_i) \cdot \log(1 - \hat{y}_i) ] $$

where $E$ is the batch size, $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ indicates the presence or absence of the keyword in the sample, and $\hat{y}_i$ is the probability output by the detection network. During training, the gradient of BCE backpropagates through the detection network and the target keyword encoder (the speech encoder is frozen). This means the "template" of the matched filter is learned end-to-end, without any hand-designed template priors.

**Negative Sample Construction (Following Navon et al. [25])**: Open-vocabulary training lacks a fixed vocabulary to enumerate positive and negative pairs. The paper dynamically generates negatives in four ways within each batch: (1) Randomly select another target keyword from the batch as a negative keyword; (2) Concatenate two different target keywords to form a negative example; (3) Replace one character in the target keyword with another (minimal contrast negative); (4) Select the "nearest" negative keyword from the batch. Why this design? The first two cover simple negatives, while the last two specifically create hard negatives—the character swap negative forces the model to remain sensitive to fine character differences (otherwise, minimal pairs like "cat"/"cap" cannot be distinguished), and the nearest neighbor negative tightens the decision boundary. This evaluation stage uses the same negative sampling technique but removes the nearest neighbor negative. This design is equivalent to encoding "vocabulary openness" into the sampling process, which is also the root cause of the inability to distinguish IV/OOV later.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Hyper-Matched Filter

Representing keywords as weights of another network (hypernetwork paradigm) is not new, but this paper explicitly anchors the role of these weights to matched filters and makes them the "guiding signal" for the entire detection process. Compared to embedding vector representations, weight representations change keywords from "objects to be compared" to "part of the computation": changing a keyword changes the convolutional kernels of the detection network, which in turn alters the behavior of subsequent cross-attention. This design brings three layers of benefits: first, the detection mechanism is interpretable (Figure 3’s heatmaps directly show keyword localization); second, it naturally supports open vocabularies (any character sequence can be compiled into a set of kernels); third, the on-device storage cost for keywords is only 1024 weights.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Keyword Weight Injection into Perceiver Cross-Attention

The usage position of $W_k$ is the key differentiator between this paper and Bluche [24]: Bluche’s hypernetwork-generated weights only act on an isolated single-layer CNN detection network, and insufficient capacity leads to performance drops on difficult keywords. HyperSpotter places keyword-specific convolutions inside the Perceiver, where convolution responses guide cross-attention weights, followed by a complete Perceiver attention mechanism taking over for sequence-level reasoning. This is equivalent to serializing "template matching" (matched filter convolution) and "sequence modeling" (attention) into an end-to-end pipeline, where the former is responsible for locating and amplifying keyword signals, and the latter integrates context for final decision-making.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Offline Keyword Compilation and Deployment Asymmetry

The 2.7M parameter keyword encoder exists only during the training/compilation phase. When reporting parameter counts for all models, the paper only counts parameters that need to be stored on the device, thus excluding the keyword encoder parameters for this paper and other similar methods (this is a fair metric). The user-side experience is: adding a new keyword requires no retraining and no updating of the model on the device; it only requires running the keyword encoder offline to generate new $W_k$ and download it to the device. This pushes "open vocabulary" from a research concept to an engineeringly deployable form.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Dual Encoder Variants and Differentiated Fine-tuning Strategies

HyperSpotter-w and HyperSpotter-c share the same keyword encoder and detection network design, forming a controlled encoder ablation experiment. Fine-tuning strategies are also differentiated: when fine-tuning on LibriPhrase, all components of HyperSpotter-c (including the speech encoder) are fine-tuned; HyperSpotter-w only fine-tunes the detection network and keyword encoder, keeping the Whisper encoder frozen. Why treat them differently? The paper’s hypothesis is that the Whisper encoder has already been trained on sufficiently large and diverse data (680,000 hours) and does not need additional training; whereas the Conformer’s pre-training data volume is much smaller (VoxPopuli plus LibriSpeech clean subsets), allowing full fine-tuning to adapt to the downstream distribution. Experimental conclusions (Section VII) also confirm the complementarity of the two variants: the c-series is stronger on in-domain datasets trained/fine-tuned on, while the w-series is more stable in out-of-domain scenarios.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Vs. QbE methods**: No need for users to register any audio; keywords are input as strings; avoids QbE’s strict requirements for consistency in recording conditions.
- **Vs. ASR/CTC transcription methods**: No full transcription is performed; the detection network directly outputs keyword presence probability, matching computational scale to task scale.
- **Vs. Embedding space methods (CED/EMKWS/CMCD)**: Keywords do not enter a shared space as a vector to be measured; instead, they are compiled into weights that directly shape the detection computation. Under similar parameter scales, performance is superior: HyperSpotter-c (1) (4.2M, comparable to EMKWS/CED’s 3.7M) exceeds both on all metrics in LibriPhrase (Table IV).
- **Vs. Bluche Detection Filters [24]**: Both use hypernetworks to generate convolutional weights, but Bluche’s detection network has only a single CNN layer, whereas this paper’s weights guide cross-attention within the Perceiver and have a complete attention mechanism taking over. On Speech Commands, even after fine-tuning, Bluche’s FRR@FAR5% is around 25, far worse than all models in this paper (best 5.064, Table VI)—though the authors admit this advantage is partly due to a magnitude difference in parameters.
- **Vs. AdaKWS [25]**: AdaKWS uses a hypernetwork to generate weights for the normalization layers of a complete two-layer Transformer, requiring 13.5M parameters on-device; it has good accuracy but exceeds size limits. HyperSpotter uses the Perceiver bottleneck to shrink the detection network; HyperSpotter-c (4) at 5.5M achieves F1 94.88 on VoxPopuli vs. AdaKWS-tiny*’s 91.93 (Table II), with less than half the size and superior accuracy.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

The paper uses six datasets (Table I provides an overview):

- **VoxPopuli**: Approximately 1800 hours of multilingual transcribed speech, covering 16 languages. Used for both training and evaluation; training keywords are marked as "open" (not fixed), with 13,745 test keywords. KWS adaptation adopts the four negative sampling schemes from [25].
- **LibriSpeech train-clean-100 + train-clean-360**: Used only for ASR pre-training of the Conformer encoder. The authors deliberately avoided train-others-500, because the LibriPhrase test set is constructed from train-others-500, to prevent training/testing leakage.
- **LibriPhrase**: A KWS benchmark based on LibriSpeech, containing Hard (LH) and Easy (LE) subsets, with phrase lengths from 1 to 4 words. The original authors only released the test set; the code to generate training/validation sets lacked parameters, so the authors could only generate approximately 200K samples (original 800K); used for both training and evaluation, with 4,003 test keywords.
- **Speech Commands V1**: Evaluation only; 10 keywords selected following [20]’s settings, consisting of short audio clips of approximately 1 second.
- **FLEURS**: Evaluation only; 2,045 test keywords. The paper selected four low-resource languages—Icelandic, Maltese, Swahili, Uzbek—for out-of-domain language testing—both datasets unseen during training and languages not present in the training data, constituting a double out-of-domain scenario.
- **Wildcat Diapix**: Evaluation only. This is spontaneous English dialogue corpus from a two-person collaborative "find the difference" image interaction task. 20 keywords are derived from 10 differences in the images. Sessions are segmented according to transcription alignment; segments shorter than 0.5 seconds are discarded, and segments longer than 30 seconds are split in two, resulting in 7,745 audio clips, including 5,948 from L2 (non-native) English speakers and 1,797 from L1 (native) speakers; two pairs of speakers lacking corrected transcription alignment were excluded. This is the first time this corpus is used for a KWS task; the paper promises to release evaluation scripts for future research to compare L1/L2 performance.

Training settings: Both KWS models are trained with BCE for up to 250 epochs, early stopping on validation loss (patience 40), Adam optimizer, batch size 96, learning rate 0.0001.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper reports standard KWS metrics: F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and recall, suitable for imbalanced positive/negative samples), AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve, measuring threshold-independent overall discriminative power), EER (Equal Error Rate, the operating point where false acceptance rate equals false rejection rate), and FRR@FAR5% (False Rejection Rate at a fixed False Alarm Rate of 5%). The rationale for choosing FRR@FAR5% is engineering-oriented: wake word systems deployed in practice first define a false alarm budget and then set the operating point; the miss rate at a fixed FAR is closer to real-world usage than metrics at arbitrary thresholds. The parameter counting metric is also worth noting: only parameters that need to be stored on the device are counted; all methods using the keyword encoder architecture (including this paper) exclude this part—this is a fair "size" metric for small-footprint scenarios.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Baselines include CED, EMKWS, CMCD, Detection filters (Bluche [24]), and the SOTA method AdaKWS. Since AdaKWS’s original model and code are not public, the authors reproduced and trained it within their experimental framework, denoted as AdaKWS-tiny*; on LibriPhrase, they additionally cite numbers reported by the original authors, denoted as AdaKWS-tiny.

**In-Domain Main Results on VoxPopuli (Table II)**: HyperSpotter-c (4) is comprehensively best—AUC 98.90, F1 94.88, EER 4.918, with only 5.5M on-device parameters; AdaKWS-tiny* achieves AUC 98.01, F1 91.93, EER 6.724, with 13.5M parameters. All ten HyperSpotter configurations have F1 scores falling in the 94.00-94.88 range (including the smallest c (1) at 4.2M: AUC 98.66 / F1 94.52 / EER 5.308), all exceeding the 13.5M AdaKWS-tiny*. The claim in the abstract is thus substantiated: the 4.2M minimum model matches or exceeds models several times larger. Two counter-intuitive observations: (1) The larger w-series (8.1-10M) is slightly inferior overall to the c-series (4.2-6M); (2) w (3) (9M, AUC 98.55/F1 94.34) and w (5) (10M, 98.54/94.27) have almost identical performance, wasting 1M parameters. Regarding reproduction quality, AdaKWS-tiny*’s F1 of 91.93 is slightly lower than the original authors’ reported 92.8; the authors admit they did not fully reproduce it but believe it is close enough.

**Language-Specific Breakdown (Table III)**: F1 performance is consistent across 16 languages (CS/DE/EN/ES/ET/FI/FR/HR/HU/IT/LT/NL/PL/RO/SK/SL), with no single model leading comprehensively; English reaches the highest at 97.83 (c (1)). Lithuanian (LT) and Estonian (ET) are significantly lower—the lowest points are 71.73 (w (4) for LT) and 69.57 (w (2) for ET), attributed to scarce training samples: LT has approximately 500 clips, ET has approximately 800 clips, while other languages all exceed 10,000 clips. This indicates that the model’s multilingual capability is largely data-driven, and the architecture itself does not fail for specific languages.

**LibriPhrase Fine-tuning Results (Table IV)**: After pre-training on VoxPopuli, only an additional 1 epoch of fine-tuning is performed. On the hard subset LH, HyperSpotter-c (4) is best (AUC 96.07 / EER 10.45), exceeding AdaKWS-tiny* (94.46 / 12.107) and the original authors’ AdaKWS-tiny (93.75 / 13.47); on the easy subset LE, HyperSpotter leads across the board (c (3) AUC 99.91 is highest, w (5)/w (1)/c (1) all 99.90). Comparisons at similar scales are more persuasive: c (1) at 4.2M is comparable to EMKWS/CED’s 3.7M, but exceeds both on all metrics (e.g., LH AUC 95.96 vs. EMKWS 84.21, CED 92.7). An honest disclosure: AdaKWS-tiny outperforms the reproduced AdaKWS-tiny* on LE, but reverses on LH; the authors attribute this to not having access to the exact training code and weights, suggesting potential differences in training settings.

**FLEURS Low-Resource Language Out-of-Domain (Table V)**: This is one of the few scenarios in the paper where results are not uniformly best. In Overall F1, HyperSpotter-c (4) ranks first with 67.16 (Icelandic 68.29 / Maltese 65.30 / Swahili 69.32 / Uzbek 67.45), with AdaKWS-tiny* closely following at 66.85—AdaKWS performs surprisingly well here, contrasting with its performance on previous datasets. More notably, the w-series degrades significantly across the board except for w (5) (66.40), with the lowest being w (3) at only 54.96; conversely, the smallest w (1) (62.84) is relatively robust. The authors admit this result is "unexpected" (one would expect the Whisper pre-training with more data to be more stable) and provide no mechanistic explanation. Another conclusion: larger Perceiver depth does not necessarily yield better performance.

**Speech Commands Out-of-Domain (Table VI)**: Since this dataset’s audio is approximately 1 second long, the paper evaluates using the LibriPhrase fine-tuned models (also short audio). HyperSpotter-w (5) is the best overall: AUC 99.05, EER 5.056, FRR@FAR5% 5.064; the best in the c-series is c (5) (97.75 / 8.554 / 10.91). The trend is opposite to VoxPopuli/LibriPhrase—the w-series surpasses the c-series. Comparing baselines: Detection filters [24] (210K parameters) has FRR@FAR5% around 36, dropping to around 25 after fine-tuning; CED (3.7M) has AUC 93.94 / EER 13.45. All HyperSpotter models exceed both; the authors acknowledge that leading Detection filters by a magnitude in parameters is not surprising, but the lead over CED is substantial given they are at a similar scale.

**Wildcat Diapix Spontaneous Speech L1/L2 (Table VII)**: On the L1 subset, HyperSpotter-w (5) is best (AUC 98.11 / EER 6.962 / FRR@FAR5% 8.933); on the L2 subset, w (5) still takes the best AUC and EER (96.58 / 9.545), while w (2) has the best FRR@FAR5% (12.63). The c-series is generally lower (L2 lowest is c (1) with AUC 93.76 / EER 14.15 / FRR 20.43). Two key points: (1) There is no L2 English speech in the training data; L2 shows only a slight drop relative to L1, with overall accuracy remaining high, proving out-of-domain generalization; (2) This dataset is used for KWS for the first time, so there are no results from other methods for comparison.

**Noise Robustness (Figure 2)**: Evaluated on VoxPopuli with white noise and pub noise added according to Whisper’s recipe, across multiple SNR levels for AUC and EER. HyperSpotter-c (4) is best in AUC and EER across all SNR levels and both noise types, with w (5) second. Another counter-intuitive finding: the c-series outperforms the w-series, despite w’s encoder being trained on much more data; the authors remind that the c-series Conformer pre-training included MUSAN additive noise augmentation, which may be the reason. Specific values for each SNR level are only shown in the figure, not listed in a table.

**Cross-Attention Visualization (Figure 3)**: On the Perceiver cross-attention heatmap for the target keyword "legal basis", high-probability scores precisely align with the two time positions where the word appears (horizontal axis: time frames at 20 ms resolution), directly validating the mechanistic claim that "keyword-specific convolution acts as a matched filter to locate keywords."

**IV/OOV Analysis**: The paper specifically discusses the distinction between in-vocabulary (IV) and out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words, concluding that it cannot be evaluated under this paper’s settings—during training, negatives are dynamically generated and positive keywords are randomly selected, making it impossible to determine afterwards which keywords entered the training set; fortunately, the generation process for evaluation keywords mirrors training, and the selection of positive/negative keywords for evaluation is deterministic. This is the evaluation cost brought by the method design (dynamic open-vocabulary sampling).

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Table VIII performs a grid ablation of encoder depth × detection network depth (Whisper encoder layers #E ∈ {2,3,4} × Perceiver layers #D ∈ {1,...,5}), evaluated on VoxPopuli:

- **Using full encoder layers is best**: Using all 4 layers of the tiny Whisper Transformer yields the best performance; 4E/4D achieves AUC 98.61 / F1 94.39 / EER 5.440 (9.5M), and 4E/5D achieves 98.54 / 94.27 / 5.430 (10M); at the same depth, 3E and 2E are consistently inferior to 4E (e.g., 3E/5D: 98.23 / 93.6 / 6.113, 8.2M; 2E/5D: 97.59 / 92.51 / 7.431, 6.4M); cutting encoder layers is a clear trade-off of accuracy for size.
- **Small models still have usable accuracy**: The smallest 2E/1D has only 4.6M parameters, achieving AUC 97.75 / F1 92.95 / EER 7.016—the authors describe this as "if users are willing to trade a bit of accuracy for a smaller model, good performance can be achieved without using all encoder layers."
- **Optimal Perceiver layer count depends on the encoder**: Section VII summarizes that the Conformer encoder pairs best with 4 Perceiver layers, while the Whisper encoder pairs best with 5 layers. There is no universal optimal depth; these two hyperparameters must be tuned jointly.
- Combining all results, there is a cross-table pattern: the marginal benefit of Perceiver layers diminishes or is even non-monotonic (smaller layer counts often surpass larger ones in Table V and Table VI), indicating that detection network capacity must match the encoder’s representational ability and task difficulty; stacking layers is not a free lunch.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed HyperSpotter**: A new end-to-end open-vocabulary KWS architecture that uses a hypernetwork to generate keyword-specific convolutional weights from character sequences, explicitly acting as matched filters, and guides cross-attention within the Perceiver framework to complete detection; the architecture is designed under small-footprint device constraints, with a minimum of 4.2M on-device parameters (Table II).
2. **Simultaneous Achievement of SOTA Accuracy and Size**: On VoxPopuli, c (4) at 5.5M achieves F1 94.88, exceeding AdaKWS-tiny* at 13.5M (91.93, Table II); on LibriPhrase LH, AUC 96.07 exceeds AdaKWS-tiny’s 93.75 (Table IV); on Speech Commands, w (5) at 10M achieves AUC 99.05, significantly exceeding CED at a similar scale (93.94, Table VI).
3. **Systematic Encoder and Depth Comparisons**: Full combination evaluation of tiny Whisper and tiny Conformer encoders, 1-5 layer Perceivers, and 2-4 layer encoders (Table II, Table VIII), providing a complete trade-off landscape of "encoder-depth-size" and summarizing the complementary rule that c-series is strong in-domain while w-series is stable out-of-domain (Section VII).
4. **Methodological Contribution to Out-of-Domain and L2 Evaluation**: First introduction of the Wildcat Diapix corpus into KWS, providing an evaluation protocol for L1/L2 speaker grouping (Table VII), and promising to release evaluation scripts; additionally, systematically testing generalization on FLEURS unseen low-resource languages and white noise/pub noise (Figure 2).
5. **Open Reproducibility**: Code and trained models are open-sourced on GitHub (for academic use); the paper explicitly points out that this is rare in similar KWS projects—the strongest baseline, AdaKWS, still does not release its model and code.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Streaming and Latency Completely Unreported**: The paper does not report any Real-Time Factor (RTF), inference latency, memory usage, or streaming implementation. Wake word systems deployed in practice usually require low-latency streaming inference, whereas Perceiver’s "variable-length to fixed-length" mapping and cross-attention are inherently whole-sentence operations. How to window for streaming, and how much accuracy drops after streaming, is not discussed by the paper.
- **Character-Level Keyword Encoding Lacks Phoneme Priors**: Character swaps in negatives construct minimal pairs at the orthographic level, but languages contain many confusion patterns of near-homophones with different spellings, or same spellings with different pronunciations; the keyword encoder looks purely at characters, with no pronunciation knowledge injected. Whether this systematically disadvantages irregularly spelled languages is not analyzed. The significant degradation of the w-series on FLEURS (w (3) Overall F1 only 54.96, Table V) may be related to this, but the paper only labels it as "unexpected" without providing a mechanism.
- **Structural Hyperparameters Fixed by Hand**: Convolution kernel length 16, projection dimension N=64, and fixed-length output S=16 are all fixed values, with no kernel length ablation; LibriPhrase phrases are 1-4 words long, and with Whisper’s 20 ms frame rate, a kernel length of 16 only covers a 320 ms receptive field; adaptability to longer/shorter phrases is not verified (not reported by the paper).
- **Parameter Count Does Not Equal Deployment Cost**: The paper uses parameter count as a proxy for "footprint," but real device bottlenecks also include operator support (efficiency of depthwise convolution and cross-attention implementation on MCU/DSP), quantization sensitivity, and activation memory; these are not addressed by the paper.
- **Noise Analysis Covers Only Two Additive Noises**: White noise and pub noise (Figure 2), presented only as figures without tables; real-world far-field reverberation, multi-speaker interference, and echo scenarios are not tested.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **SOTA Baseline is a Reproduced Version**: AdaKWS’s original model and code are not public; AdaKWS-tiny* is reproduced by the authors; on LibriPhrase, AdaKWS-tiny and the reproduced version show reversed trends on LE/LH (Table IV), and the authors themselves point out that differences in training settings may be the cause. The strength of the head-to-head comparison is uncertain.
- **LibriPhrase Training Set is Incomplete**: The original authors’ generation code lacked parameters, reproducing only approximately 200K/800K samples, inconsistent with the original benchmark protocol; extrapolation from fine-tuning results should be cautious.
- **IV/OOV Cannot Be Distinguished**: The design of dynamic negative sampling plus random positive selection makes it untraceable "which keywords were seen during training"; the most critical metric for open-vocabulary methods, the degree of OOV degradation, cannot be measured in this paper, relying instead on indirect proxies like overall out-of-domain tests such as FLEURS.
- **No Horizontal Comparison on Wildcat**: The cost of first-time use is the lack of results from other methods (Table VII), leaving the relative levels of L1/L2 without a reference frame.
- **Multilingual Data Imbalance Not Addressed**: LT has approximately 500 clips, ET has approximately 800 clips of training samples, leading to significantly lower F1 for these two languages in Table III (lowest 71.73 and 69.57); the paper only makes attribution observations, without performing data weighting, language balancing, or low-resource augmentation experiments.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- The authors’ stated next step: exploring more types of speech encoders and detection architectures, further compressing model size and computational footprint while maintaining or improving performance.
- Completing the deployment-side evidence chain: streaming adaptation, real-time latency and memory measurements on real devices (MCU/DSP), quantization/pruning experiments—turning "parameter count advantages" into "deployment advantages."
- Introducing phoneme/pronunciation priors into keyword encoding (character-phoneme hybrid input or pronunciation feature embeddings) to specifically handle near-homophone negatives and irregular orthographic languages, potentially improving stability in FLEURS-like scenarios.
- Adaptive research on kernel length, receptive field, and phrase length, as well as ablation of bottleneck hyperparameters like N and S, to answer "why 16."
- Upgrading L2/accent robustness from an evaluation metric to a training objective (accent-augmented data, accent-balanced sampling), rather than relying solely on the implicit generalization of pre-trained encoders.
- Designing evaluation protocols with controlled vocabulary splits to make IV/OOV degradation measurable, addressing the most crucial question that open-vocabulary methods should answer.
