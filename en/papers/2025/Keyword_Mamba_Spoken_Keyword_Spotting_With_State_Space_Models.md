## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) refers to detecting specific wake-up words or command words within a continuous audio stream. It serves as the voice interaction entry point for devices such as voice assistants, smart home devices, and smart cockpits. Since subsequent operations are triggered only upon a detection hit, the accuracy and efficiency of KWS directly determine the smoothness of human-computer interaction (Section 1 of the paper). The deep learning approach to KWS began with the first DNN-based KWS system in 2014 and has since evolved through three mainstream architectures: CNNs (TC-ResNet, BC-ResNet, MatchboxNet, ConvMixer, etc.), RNNs (Att-RNN, MHAtt-RNN, DenseNet-BiLSTM, etc.), and Transformers (Keyword Transformer, i.e., KWT).

The essence of the KWS scenario is a dual-constraint problem involving "long-range dependency modeling" and "computational efficiency." On one hand, the pronunciation of a keyword spans hundreds of milliseconds, with blurred start and end boundaries and phonemes serving as context for each other. The model needs to observe a sufficiently long temporal context to distinguish between neighbor words like "marvin" and "marven," or "three" and "tree." On the other hand, KWS typically runs persistently on resource-constrained devices such as smartphones, speakers, and earbuds, with strict upper limits on parameters, computational cost, and inference latency. An ideal architecture must satisfy two conditions simultaneously: the ability to model long-range temporal dependencies and a complexity that grows linearly with sequence length. The existing three architectures each only satisfy one of these conditions.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper explicitly points out the inherent shortcomings of the three architectures in the Introduction (Section 1):

- **CNNs have a fixed receptive field.** Convolutions can only see local time windows. To cover long-range dependencies, one must stack depth or increase the convolution kernel size, creating a trade-off between receptive field and computational cost. BC-ResNet combines the advantages of 1D temporal convolution and 2D spatial convolution using broadcast residuals, representing a refined effort in this lineage, but the ceiling problem of the receptive field remains structural.
- **RNNs cannot be parallelized.** They rely on strict sequential computation in the temporal dimension, making training and inference difficult to parallelize and resulting in slow speeds. Although MHAtt-RNN introduces multi-head attention to improve the capture of key temporal dependencies, the essence of sequential computation remains unchanged.
- **Transformers suffer from quadratic complexity growth.** The complexity of self-attention with respect to the context window is O(N²), significantly limiting efficiency and scalability when processing long sequences. KWT models long-range context well via self-attention, but the cost is uncontrolled parameter growth (KWT-3 reaches 5361K, see Table 1).

Meanwhile, the State Space Model (SSM) route has risen rapidly in 2023-2024: S4 reduces long-sequence modeling complexity to O(N log N), and Mamba further reduces it to linear O(N) through selective mechanisms and hardware-friendly scanning algorithms, achieving results surpassing SOTA in both NLP and CV. Initial attempts at Mamba in speech processing have appeared: speech enhancement, speech recognition, speech separation, and self-supervised Audio Mamba (AuM, which achieves comparable or better performance than audio spectrogram Transformers with about one-third of the parameters). However, the authors explicitly state that different speech tasks focus on different signal characteristics (speaker identity, linguistic content, emotional state, etc.). Until this paper, the effective design of Mamba models for spoken keyword detection had not been studied at all (last sentence of Section 2.3: "the effective design of Mamba models for deep KWS remains unexplored").

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

Moving Mamba into KWS is not a simple backbone swap; the paper answers three specific design questions. First, **how to choose the scanning axis**: Speech is a 2D time-frequency signal, while Mamba is a 1D sequence model. Whether to scan along the time axis, frequency axis, or use a hybrid scan directly determines the architecture's form, which the paper resolves through experiments (the final answer is "scan only along the time axis," based on the existing conclusion that KWS is relatively insensitive to frequency information and their own ablation verification). Second, **unidirectional or bidirectional**: The original Mamba is causal and unidirectional, relying only on historical information. In KWS classification, the entire sentence is available, and keyword boundaries and surrounding sounds may appear in the past or future context. It must possess bidirectional context capabilities, similar to multi-head self-attention. Third, **how to compensate for insufficient non-linearity**: Most operations in SSMs are linear. Although SiLU activation is used in residual structures, the ability to capture high-level semantic information is limited. It is necessary to determine whether and how to introduce additional non-linearity. The paper aims to provide the first complete architectural recipe for SSMs in KWS (two variants: pure Mamba's KWM and hybrid KWM-T), surpassing existing SOTA on four evaluation settings of Google Speech Commands V1/V2, while keeping parameters and computational costs controllable.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall pipeline of Keyword Mamba (Fig. 2) is: Input waveform is converted to an MFCC spectrogram M ∈ R^{F×T} (40-dim MFCC, 30ms window, 10ms frame shift, time dimension zero-padded to 98 frames, thus F=40, T=98) → Patches are cut according to the [f,t] shape and flattened into a sequence of N patches → A linear projection matrix W0 ∈ R^{F×d} maps each patch to the model dimension d along the frequency dimension → A learnable class embedding is inserted in the middle of the projected sequence → Learnable positional encoding is added (Equation 6) → Sent to L layers of Mamba Encoder, each with residuals (Equation 7) → The class token from the output of the L-th layer is normalized → Passed through an MLP head to output classification results.

There are four key design decisions in this pipeline, each with a clear "why":

**Decision 1: Apply Mamba only along the time axis (f=F, t=1, N=T).** Theoretically, the patch shape can be any factorization of (F/f)×(T/t), but the paper takes f=F=40, t=1, meaning each patch is a complete frame of MFCC (40-dim), and the sequence length equals the number of frames, 98. Why: KWS is less sensitive to frequency information than time information (the paper cites the conclusion from TC-ResNet that temporal convolution is particularly effective for speech recognition, reference [9]); this makes the first projection layer (Patching) effectively equivalent to a temporal convolution with kernel [40,1] and stride 1, interpreting "patching + linear projection" as a natural extension of temporal convolution. This choice was subsequently verified in reverse by ablation experiments (Fig. 4): pure time patch [40,1] achieved the highest accuracy of 98.01%, while pure frequency patch [1,98] achieved only 94.34%.

**Decision 2: Insert the class token in the middle of the sequence rather than at the beginning.** Following the practice of Vision Mamba (Vim), the learnable class token X_class ∈ R^{1×d} is placed between the first half and the second half of the projected sequence. Why: This allows the token, which aggregates information from the entire sentence, to be equidistant from the two halves, enabling it to aggregate information from both earlier and later frames. Ablation (Table 4) shows that the Mid position (98.01%) is better than Head (97.75%) and End (97.92%), but the difference is small (maximum 0.26 percentage points), indicating that the impact of token position in KWS is much smaller than in other speech or language tasks.

**Decision 3: Bidirectional Mamba (BiMamba).** The original Mamba is unidirectional causal, relying only on history; speech tasks have the entire sentence available and require global dependencies similar to MHSA. The paper adopts Vim's bidirectional design: two sets of SSM + Conv1D modules for forward and backward directions share the same input/output projection, and the outputs of the two paths are merged and passed through an output projection (Fig. 2(d)). Why bidirectional is necessary: In the ablation (Table 4), the fully unidirectional Mamba-Fo-Fo dropped from 98.01% to 78.29%, a drop of nearly 20 percentage points, which is the most drastic comparison in the paper, directly proving that bidirectional information flow is a prerequisite for performance.

**Decision 4: Two task-aware encoder variants (Fig. 3).** The first, KWM, uses pure BiMamba layers stacked directly to replace Transformer layers, motivated by directly utilizing Mamba's long-range dependency capability, focusing on time patterns related to keyword activation, and maintaining a lightweight structure suitable for real-time or low-resource scenarios. The second, KWM-T, replaces only the Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) module in the Transformer layer with BiMamba, retaining the Feed-Forward Network (FFN) and normalization layers. Why this replacement: SSMs consist mostly of linear operations. Although SiLU activation is used in the residual structure, the ability to capture high-level information (e.g., semantics) is still limited. Introducing FFN and additional Norm is intended to compensate for non-linear expression. These two variants, combined with three model width tiers of 192/128/64 (following KWT's dimension configuration to balance fair comparison and lightweight constraints), constitute the complete model family.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**SSM Foundation (S4 Layer).** State Space Models describe the evolution of hidden states over time and their relationship with observations using a set of linear differential equations (Equation 1):

h′(t) = Ah(t) + Bx(t); y(t) = Ch(t)

where A is the state transition matrix, B is the input-to-state projection matrix, C is the state-to-output projection matrix, x(t) is the input at time t, h(t) is the hidden state, and y(t) is the output. Processing discrete data such as text and speech requires discretization: introducing a fourth parameter Δ (step size) to convert continuous A and B into discrete versions (Equation 2): Ā = exp(ΔA), B̄ = (ΔA)^(−1)(exp(ΔA) − I)·ΔB (zero-order hold discretization form). After discretization, a recursive form is obtained (Equation 3): h′(t) = Āh(t−1) + B̄x(t), y(t) = Ch(t). This recursion can be expanded into a global convolution (Equation 4): y = x ⊛ K, where the convolution kernel K = (CB, CAB, …, CA^(L−1)B), and L is the input sequence length—this is why SSMs can be parallelized during training (convolution mode) and recursively maintain constant state during inference. S4 builds on this with a three-piece set: HiPPO memory mechanism to compress input history, diagonal plus low-rank parameterization to ensure stability and diagonalizability, and FFT/iFFT for efficient kernel computation, reducing complexity to O(N log N).

**Mamba's Selective Mechanism (Equation 5).** S4's parameters are input-independent, which puts it at a disadvantage in tasks requiring content-based filtering of information, such as selective copy or inductive heads. Mamba makes parameters input-dependent: B̄ = s_B(x), C̄ = s_C(x), Δ = τ(Parameter) + s_A(x), where s_B(x) and s_C(x) are linear functions projecting input x to N-dimensional states, s_A(x) expands a D-dimensional linear projection to a fixed dimension, and τ is a soft thresholding function. Coupled with a hardware-friendly selective scanning algorithm and gating mechanism, Mamba gains input-aware content selection capability while maintaining linear complexity O(N)—this is the essential difference between Mamba and RNNs: in the same state recursion, Mamba's forgetting speed (Δ) and read/write weights (B, C) are calculated dynamically per token.

**Algorithm 1 (Complete Workflow of Mamba Layer).** Input X_{l−1}: (B, L, D), first perform Norm, then pass through two linear projections to obtain the main branch x and the gating branch z (both (B, L, E), where E is the internal expansion dimension, specific multiplier not reported in the paper). For both forward and backward directions, execute: x′_o = SiLU(Conv1d_o(x)) (directional depth convolution); linearly project from x′_o to obtain input-dependent B_o: (B,L,N) and C_o: (B,L,N); Δ_o = log(1+exp(Linear(x′_o) + Parameter^Δ)), i.e., softplus ensures the step size is non-negative; then Ā_o = Δ_o ⊗ Parameter^A, B̄_o = Δ_o ⊗ B_o complete the discretization; finally y_o = SSM(Ā_o, B̄_o, C_o)(x′_o). The outputs of the two directions are multiplied with the gating branch respectively (y′_forward = y_forward ⊙ SiLU(z), y′_backward similarly), added, passed through an output projection, and residuals are added: X_l = Linear(y′_forward + y′_backward) + X_{l−1}. The entire process is consistent with the original Mamba block, except that unidirectional is changed to bidirectional with shared projections.

**Algorithm 2 (Transformer Layer with Mamba).** The first 15 steps are exactly the same as Algorithm 1 (BiMamba sub-layer + residual), followed by an FFN sub-layer: first perform Norm on the BiMamba sub-layer output, then pass through FeedForward (Linear1 → GELU → Linear2), add residual to get X_l. Note that here FFN uses GELU instead of SiLU inside Mamba. The two sub-layers each carry Norm and residual, and the structure is isomorphic to a standard Transformer encoder layer, except MHSA is replaced by BiMamba—this is the embodiment of the design philosophy of "only replacing attention, not the entire model" at the pseudocode level.

**Sequence Assembly in Forward Propagation (Equation 6).** X0 = [X_{1:⌊T/2⌋}W0; X_class; X_{⌊T/2⌋+1:T}W0] + X_pos, i.e., the first half of frames, class token, and second half of frames are concatenated and positional encoding is added. The encoder advances layer by layer according to Equation 7: X_l = Mamba Encoder(X_{l−1}) + X_{l−1}, finally f = Norm(X^L_0) (class token output from the L-th layer), p = MLP(f) outputs the class prediction.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Task-Customized "Pure Temporal Unidirectional Axis Scan" Recipe for KWS

The core contribution of this paper is not inventing a new Mamba operator, but providing a task-level answer to "how to arrange Mamba for KWS": patch takes [40,1], scans only along the time axis, and projects the frequency dimension into the model dimension via a single linear projection. The evidence chain for this configuration has three layers. The first layer is prior knowledge: KWS discrimination relies mainly on time patterns rather than frequency details (the temporal convolution conclusion of TC-ResNet). The second layer is ablation (Fig. 4): from pure frequency [1,98] at 94.34%, to [2,20] at 95.21%, to mid-range hybrid patches at 96.39%-97.53%, climbing all the way to pure time [40,1] at 98.01%. The third layer is structural equivalence: Patching + linear projection is equivalent to a temporal convolution with kernel [40,1] and stride 1, seamlessly inheriting the advantages of temporal convolution verified in the CNN era into the SSM architecture. This experimental conclusion on "scanning axis selection" has direct reference value for all subsequent work wanting to apply Mamba to speech classification tasks—it indicates that at least for KWS, 2D time-frequency spectrograms should be compressed into 1D time sequences to feed the SSM, rather than learning from ViT to mix patches on both axes.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Sequence-Level Aggregation Scheme of Bidirectional BiMamba Plus Centered Class Token

Two components borrowed from Vim combine here to form the complete sequence-level classification scheme for KWS: BiMamba provides bidirectional context (the catastrophic result of fully unidirectional 78.29% proves its necessity, Table 4), and the centered class token provides an equidistant bidirectional information aggregation point (Mid 98.01% is better than Head 97.75% and End 97.92%, Table 4). Notably, the paper's honest observation on class token position: the position impact is small in KWS (no more than 0.26 percentage points), which contrasts with the general experience that token position is often more important in speech and language tasks. The authors speculate this is because KWS sequences are short (98 frames plus 1 token) and bidirectional scanning has smoothed out positional asymmetry. Additionally, the directionality ablation further subdivides an intermediate tier BiMamba-Fo-Bi (only SSM bidirectional, Conv1D unidirectional): 97.69%, which is 0.32 percentage points lower than fully bidirectional, indicating that the directionality of Conv1D also contributes but with small weight (Table 4).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: KWM-T – Hybrid Encoder Precisely Replacing MHSA with BiMamba

KWM-T is the most engineering-savvy design in the paper: it does not overturn the Transformer, but performs an organ transplant. The reasoning chain for the motivation is clear: Replacing MHSA with Mamba reduces quadratic complexity to linear and obtains better long-sequence scalability; but the SSM主体 is linear operations, and using it bare (KWM) would lose high-level semantic modeling capability; FFN is exactly the main source of non-linear expression in Transformers, so retaining FFN plus Norm allows them to perform their respective duties—Mamba manages efficient sequence memory, and FFN manages non-linear semantic transformation. Experimental support for this division of labor assumption: under similar parameter budgets, KWM-T consistently achieves accuracy comparable to or higher than KWM. For example, KWM-T-192 reaches 98.05% on V1-12 and 98.91% on V2-12, surpassing KWM-192 (98.01%/98.79%) and almost all Transformer baselines in Table 1 (Table 3). More importantly, KWM-T-192 (5.2M) has almost exactly the same number of parameters as KWT-3 (5.361M, Table 1), and under the same budget, it dominates all four metrics, constituting the cleanest controlled evidence that "Mamba can replace self-attention."

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

**Compared with KWT (Transformer)**: The core difference is that the sequence modeling operator is changed from MHSA to BiMamba, reducing complexity from O(N²) to O(N); the class token is moved from the beginning of the sequence to the exact middle; parameter efficiency is significantly better (same 12 layers, 192 width, KWM-192 is 3.4M, KWT-3 is 5.361M, Table 1). **Compared with CNNs (BC-ResNet, MatchboxNet)**: CNNs stack local convolutions with fixed receptive fields to build large receptive fields, while KWM models the entire speech segment at once via sequence-level global state; but the parameter advantage of CNNs is still obvious (BC-ResNet-8 is only 321K, KWM-192 is 3.4M); the paper wins on accuracy ceiling rather than extreme lightweight. **Compared with RNNs**: Both use state recursion, but Mamba's selective mechanism makes the forgetting rate and read/write weights input-dependent per token, and it can run in convolution mode for parallel training during training, coupled with hardware-aware scanning algorithms, avoiding the old problems of RNNs' inability to parallelize and gradient decay in long sequences. **Compared with existing speech Mamba works**: AuM is a self-supervised audio label pre-training model, Speech Mamba series are oriented towards speech enhancement, recognition, and separation; this paper is the first Mamba design oriented towards KWS, and provides the task-specific conclusion of "time axis only," rather than blindly copying a general recipe. **Compared with Vim**: Bidirectional scanning and centered class token are both practices of Vim, but Vim processes 2D sequences flattened from image patches, while this paper processes naturally 1D time frame sequences, and the choice of scanning axis has been re-argued based on KWS task characteristics (frequency insensitivity).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

All experiments were conducted on two versions of the Google Speech Commands (GSC) dataset, V1 and V2 (Section 4.1): V1 contains 30 words and 64,727 audio clips; V2 contains 35 words and 105,829 clips. Evaluation covers four settings: 12-label classification (ten target words "up/down/left/right/yes/no/on/off/go/stop" plus two classes silence and unknown, unknown sampled from other words), 30-label full-word classification for V1, and 35-label full-word classification for V2. Data is split 80:10:10 for training/validation/testing, deliberately following the split method of MHAtt-RNN and KWT for side-by-side comparison, and evaluation criteria follow MHAtt-RNN as much as possible.

The details of preprocessing and augmentation are highly engineering-oriented (Section 4.2): Input is converted to 40-dim MFCC, 30ms window, 10ms frame shift, time dimension zero-padded to a fixed 98 frames. Training augmentation includes: time shifting within ±100ms, resampling within [0.85, 1.15], background noise superposition with volume 0.1, 2 time masks of size [0, 25] and 2 frequency masks of size [0, 7] (SpecAugment-style mask augmentation). Training configuration: V1 trained for 200 epochs, V2 for 140 epochs, batch size 128, AdamW optimizer, initial learning rate 0.001, cosine annealing with 10 warmup epochs, weight decay 0.1, label smoothing 0.1, cross-entropy loss. Main results uniformly use 12-layer encoders to align with KWT series configurations to ensure fairness (Section 5.1).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

There is only one main metric: Accuracy ACC (following MHAtt-RNN's evaluation criteria, Section 4.1). The rationale is direct: GSC is a closed-set classification task with a fixed vocabulary, and the training and testing splits are highly unified in the community, so accuracy can be directly compared across papers; each experiment is trained with three different random initializations and the average is taken to suppress randomness. It should be noted that the paper does not report efficiency metrics such as FLOPs, inference latency, peak memory, or throughput—all "efficiency" arguments are built on parameter comparisons (Table 1-3) and theoretical complexity (O(N) vs O(N²)), which is a significant gap in the evaluation system.

Several readings are worth expanding on. **First, the parameter efficiency advantage over Transformer baselines is real**: KWT-3 requires 5361K parameters and achieves only 97.49% on V1-12; KWM-192 uses 3.4M (saving about one-third) to achieve 98.01%, surpassing in all four settings (98.79 vs 98.56 on V2-12, 97.86 vs 97.69 on V2-35), which Section 5.1 of the paper emphasizes specifically. **Second, the equal-budget comparison between KWM-T-192 and KWT-3 is the cleanest**: 5.2M vs 5.361M, 98.05 vs 97.49 (V1-12), 98.91 vs 98.56 (V2-12), with the Mamba version winning comprehensively under the same organ transplant logic. **Third, the superiority over CNNs should be viewed with some discount**: BC-ResNet-8 (321K) has four metrics of 98.0/97.46/98.7/97.65, and KWM-192 is indeed slightly better in all four (gap 0.01-0.28 percentage points), but the number of parameters is more than ten times that of the opponent; the paper's expression is "competitive or superior," winning on accuracy ceiling rather than the lightweight tier. **Fourth, small models beat同级 KWT**: KWM-64 (0.5M) vs KWT-1 (607K), 97.46 vs 97.26 on V1-12, 98.13 vs 98.08 on V2-12; KWM-T-64 (0.7M) reaches 97.72/98.56. Overall, KWM and KWM-T refresh benchmark records on all four settings (Section 5.1 "consistently outperforms existing models across all datasets, establishing new benchmark records").

A detail to note: The KWT row carries ±std (fluctuations from three runs are between 0.03-0.18), while the paper's models only report means without variance—under the magnitude of gaps of 0.05-0.35 percentage points, the statistical significance of some comparisons is not as solid as it appears.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper conducts three explicit ablations (Section 5.4), plus two depth/width scans (Sections 5.2, 5.3), with a fairly complete evidence chain.

**Ablation 1: Patch shape, temporal vs. frequency (Fig. 4, V1-12, KWM-192).** From pure frequency scan [1,98] at 94.34%, [2,20] at 95.21%, mid-range hybrid patches at 96.39%-97.53%, climbing all the way to pure time [40,1] at 98.01%. The conclusion is clear: Discriminative information for KWS is distributed along the time axis; the larger the proportion of the time dimension, the higher the accuracy, and scanning the frequency axis wastes modeling capacity; the equivalence of Patching to temporal convolution with kernel [40,1] echoes the classic conclusion of TC-ResNet.

**Ablation 2: Class token position (Table 4).** Mid 98.01%, End 97.92%, Head 97.75%. Centered is best, because the token can receive information from both earlier and later sequence segments; but the overall impact is small, and the authors honestly point out that this is contrary to the general experience that "token position plays a larger role in other speech or language tasks."

**Ablation 3: Directionality (Table 4).** BiMamba-Bi-Bi (SSM and Conv1D both bidirectional) 98.01%, BiMamba-Fo-Bi (only SSM bidirectional) 97.69%, Mamba-Fo-Fo (fully unidirectional) 78.29%. The fully unidirectional drop of nearly 20 percentage points is the single largest factor effect in the paper, proving that bidirectional information flow is a rigid requirement for speech classification—keyword boundaries and surrounding sounds may appear in both past and future contexts. This result also buries a deployment hazard: real streaming KWS is naturally causal, and directly cutting off the reverse path will collapse; streaming adaptation requires specialized design (not expanded in the paper).

**Depth/Width Scan (Table 2, KWM).** 192 width tier: 12 layers 3.4M gets 98.01/97.73/98.79/97.86; 10 layers 2.9M gets 97.95/97.54/98.42/97.62; 8 layers 2.3M gets 97.70/97.41/98.40/97.62; 6 layers 1.7M gets 97.92/97.39/98.52/97.44. 128 width tier drops from 12 layers 1.6M at 97.95/98.62 to 6 layers 0.8M at 97.33/98.31. 64 width tier drops from 12 layers 0.5M at 97.46/98.13 to 6 layers 0.2M at 96.74/97.45. The core judgment given by the paper based on this is "robustness under compression": even shrunk to 6 layers plus 64 dimensions (0.2M), it can still maintain 96.74%-97.45%, comparable to many larger CNN or RNN models, while Transformer or convolution models often drop significantly when shrunk (Section 5.2); the explanation is that Mamba can cover time patterns over long time spans relying on its recurrent state structure, not depending on attention, so deep/wide configurations have gains but are not necessary conditions for competitive accuracy. There is also an interesting non-monotonic phenomenon in the data: the 192 tier 6 layers on V1-12 (97.92) is actually higher than 8 layers (97.70) and basically持平 with 10 layers (97.95); on V2-12, the 6 layers' 98.52 is also higher than the 10 layers' 98.42—indicating that this architecture is not sensitive to depth, and blindly deepening it has low cost-performance ratio.

**KWM-T Scan (Table 3).** 192 tier: 12 layers 5.2M gets 98.05/97.80/98.91/97.89 (best in the field), 6 layers 2.6M still has 97.62/97.57/98.42/97.63; 128 tier 12 layers 2.4M gets 97.88/98.73; 64 tier 12 layers 0.7M gets 97.72/98.56. Under the same budget, KWM-T is consistently better than or equal to KWM (e.g., 12 layers 192 width: 98.05 vs 98.01, 98.91 vs 98.79), at the cost of about 50% more parameters (5.2M vs 3.4M)—FFN compensating for non-linearity is indeed effective, but whether the parameter cost paid for this improvement (0.04-0.12 percentage points) is worth it depends on the edge scenario. The paper's conclusion states: Combining Mamba with Transformer-style feed-forward layers is equivalent to splicing efficient memory modeling with strong non-linear capacity, providing a promising compromise between accuracy and complexity (Section 5.3).

## Main Contributions

1. **First introduction of State Space Models to the KWS task.** The paper explicitly states this is the first application of SSMs to KWS (abstract and Section 1 both emphasize "to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to apply a state space-based model to KWS"), filling a missing piece in the Mamba speech landscape.
2. **Provided task-aware dual-variant architecture recipes.** KWM (pure BiMamba stacking) is oriented towards extreme lightweight, KWM-T (only replacing MHSA, retaining FFN) is oriented towards accuracy ceiling, sharing the sequence-level design of "temporal unidirectional axis scan + centered class token + bidirectional SSM," and all dimension configurations follow KWT to ensure comparability.
3. **Refreshed SOTA on all four benchmarks (Table 1):** The best configuration KWM-T-192 reached V1-12 98.05%, V1-30 97.80%, V2-12 98.91%, V2-35 97.89%; there is a clear parameter efficiency advantage over Transformer baselines (3.4M vs 5361K still dominates comprehensively).
4. **Complete ablation evidence chain (Fig. 4, Table 4, Table 2, Table 3):** All four design dimensions of scanning axis selection, class token position, directionality, and depth/width have controlled experimental backing. In particular, the two large-effect conclusions of "frequency axis scan dropping 3.67 percentage points" and "fully unidirectional dropping 19.72 percentage points" have direct migration value for subsequent speech SSM work.
5. **Open source and reproducible:** Code and models are placed on GitHub (dhyzy123/KWM), friendly to the community following the Mamba route for KWS.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Efficiency claims lack empirical support.** The paper repeatedly emphasizes Mamba's linear complexity and computational efficiency, but does not report any of FLOPs, inference latency, peak memory, or throughput; efficiency evidence is only based on parameter counts (Table 1-3) and theoretical complexity analysis. Moreover, the parameter count itself is not extreme—KWM-192 is 3.4M, KWM-T-192 is 5.2M. Compared to MatchboxNet's 93K, BC-ResNet-8's 321K, and SincConv+GDSConv's 62K, it is on the larger side in the traditional volume spectrum of edge-side KWS; only KWM-64 six layers at 0.2M (Table 2) truly competes with the lightweight tier.
- **Bidirectional architecture structurally conflicts with streaming inference.** Real wake-word systems must run in a streaming causal manner, but ablation shows fully unidirectional Mamba drops to 78.29% (Table 4), effectively宣判ing that the "direct causalization" path is not feasible; streaming adaptation schemes such as chunk-level bidirectional, limited look-back, or causal SSM variants are completely untouched by the paper, leaving one key architectural step away from deployment.
- **Sequence-level classification design is incompatible with edge-side per-frame scoring paradigms.** The design of class token plus full-sentence MLP is essentially utterance-level classification, relying on pre-fixed 98 frames of zero-padding; edge-side KWS conventionally uses per-frame sliding window scoring plus post-processing smoothing, and the two interfaces are not naturally compatible.
- **Non-linear shortcomings of the linear backbone require adding parameters to compensate.** KWM-T uses FFN to compensate for non-linearity effectively, but parameters increase from 3.4M to 5.2M (increase of about 53%, Table 1 vs Table 2), while accuracy only increases by 0.04-0.12 percentage points, with low marginal returns.
- **Long-range advantages cannot be realized on short-instruction data.** Mamba's core selling point is linear complexity modeling of long sequences, but each GSC clip is only one second long, and after padding, it is only 98 frames. The complexity advantage of O(N) vs O(N²) is hardly manifested on such short sequences; the architecture's capability in truly long temporal scenarios (continuous listening, multi-word commands) has not been tested.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Single and relatively simple dataset.** Only closed-set classification on GSC V1/V2, no custom wake-word sets, no specialized stress tests under far-field or strong noise conditions (the only noise-related setting in training augmentation is background noise with volume 0.1), no multi-language or accent dimensions; the robustness claim for "real-world scenarios" (Section 1) is actually only indirectly backed by standard augmentation.
- **Own results do not report variance.** Although each experiment was run three times with different random initializations and averaged (Section 4.1), the paper's models in Table 1-3 only have means, while KWT baselines all carry ±std; considering that most leading margins are in the 0.05-0.35 percentage point range and KWT's std is 0.03-0.18, the statistical significance of some comparisons is questionable.
- **Ablations are only performed on a single setting.** The three ablations in Fig. 4 and Table 4 are all performed only on V1-12 with KWM-192, without cross-dataset or cross-model-scale validation of the extrapolability of the conclusions.
- **Asymmetric narrative in comparison with lightweight CNNs.** The paper focuses the parameter efficiency comparison on the Transformer series (3.4M vs 5361K is indeed impressive), avoiding the Pareto discussion of the ten-fold parameter gap with BC-ResNet-8 and MatchboxNet—in the field of KWS, which values smallness, the accuracy-parameter trade-off curve is a fairer presentation method.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Complete system-level evaluation:** Report FLOPs, inference latency and memory usage on different hardware, and conduct real-world comparisons on the same MCU or mobile SoC with MatchboxNet and BC-ResNet, turning "efficiency" from a theoretical claim into an engineering fact.
- **Streaming causal transformation:** Design chunk-level bidirectional scanning or causal Mamba encoders with limited look-back, coupled with per-frame scoring heads to replace class token aggregation, allowing the architecture to truly meet the streaming constraints of persistent listening; this is a prerequisite for this route to move towards products.
- **Superposition of extreme small models and compression techniques:** The 0.2M tier KWM-64 six layers has already shown compression robustness. The next step is to combine it with knowledge distillation, low-bit quantization, and pruning to冲击 the tier within 100K parameters, competing directly with the SincConv series.
- **Expand task boundaries:** Evaluate on custom wake-words, multi-language, far-field simulated data, and open-set (out-of-vocabulary) rejection; lengthen the sequence to continuous listening scenarios, allowing the long-range advantage of linear complexity to truly materialize.
- **Combine with self-supervised speech representations:** Drawing on AuM's masked spectrogram pre-training or connecting to wav2vec 2.0/WavLM features, test whether pre-training can further amplify the temporal modeling benefits of SSMs.
- **Systematic search for hybrid ratios:** KWM and KWM-T are just the two ends of the hybrid spectrum. The optimal ratio and insertion positions of Mamba layers vs. FFN/attention layers can be automatically explored using neural architecture search, rather than manual binary choice.

Overall judgment: This is a pioneering work of "new operator first landing on a task." Its value does not lie in single-point accuracy (the lead over lightweight CNNs is actually very thin), but in using a complete ablation to answer the three key questions of "how to arrange Mamba for KWS" (scanning axis, directionality, non-linear compensation) at once, and providing clean evidence for replacing self-attention under equal budgets (KWM-T-192 vs KWT-3). What is truly worth tracking subsequently is the iteration in two directions: streaming and miniaturization—these are the decisive variables for whether the Mamba route can stand firm in edge-side KWS.
