## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

This paper addresses a problem distinct from mainstream KWS algorithm research: the power bottleneck in **always-on** KWS chips lies in the feature extractor (FEx), not the classifier. The logical chain presented at the beginning of the paper (Fig. 1) is noteworthy: on mobile and IoT devices, the KWS module responsible for receiving voice commands usually dominates the system's power consumption, necessitating low-power solutions. A typical KWS system consists of three stages: an audio front-end, an MFCC feature extractor, and a neural network classifier. MFCC is a feature extraction algorithm based on FFT, which is a significant power consumer in low-power KWS chips (Abstract).

Why does MFCC become a power hog? There are explicit circuit-level reasons. The complete MFCC pipeline involves windowing → FFT → power spectrum calculation → Mel filtering → logarithm → DCT (upper part of Fig. 2). Every step theoretically requires multiplication: 256-point Hamming window weighting requires 256 multiplications, the complex multiplications in FFT butterfly operations require 2048 real multiplications, magnitude calculation involves square roots and squares, triangular Mel filter weighting per sample involves multiplication, and DCT cosine twiddle factors also involve multiplication (Table I, theoretical column totals 3168 multiplications, 3808 additions, and 932,864 bit operations). In a chip operating at 0.55 V near-threshold voltage and 125 kHz low frequency (specifications in Fig. 8), multipliers are the most expensive arithmetic units in terms of area and dynamic power—a two-operand multiplication requires a full tree of partial product compression plus a final adder, whereas shifting is merely wire rearrangement, and addition requires only a single carry chain. This is the foundation of the "multiplier-free" title: **replacing all multiplications in MFCC with shifts and additions can reduce the computational power of FEx by orders of magnitude**.

There is another dimension often overlooked by algorithm-side approaches: memory. Traditional implementations must store 256-point window function coefficients, FFT twiddle factors, and Mel filter coefficients on-chip. On-chip memory has considerable leakage and access power at near-threshold voltages. The final measured power breakdown of this paper (Fig. 9(c)) confirms this—the total FEx power is 1771 nW, of which the computational part is only 76 nW, while the storage part is as high as 1695 nW (Table III). Storage is truly the largest component. This fact explains why the paper simultaneously performs optimizations such as "multiplier-free window function coefficients" in the algorithm.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper uses Fig. 1 to categorize recent power-reduction routes into three types and points out their deficiencies one by one:

- **Type 1: Multi-stage wake-up + duty-cycled operation time**. Using VAD (Voice Activity Detection) for multi-stage wake-up, combined with regular sleep and wake cycles [13][14]. These methods do not change the energy consumption of a single calculation, but rather reduce the number of calculations. The cost is increased system complexity, and VAD miss-detection directly leads to missed wake-word detection.
- **Type 2: Compressing the classifier**. Training networks with quantization and pruning, or switching to more efficient network architectures [15][16][35]. These methods reduce classifier power consumption significantly (e.g., binary networks), but once the classifier is compressed to the hundreds of nanowatts level, the power proportion of FEx becomes prominent—the optimization ceiling appears on the other side.
- **Type 3: Replacing MFCC with analog signal processors (ASP, such as analog bandpass filter banks)** [17-34]. Analog feature extraction can indeed be very power-efficient (e.g., JSSC'21 [34] has an FEx total power of only 35 nW, Table III), but the paper points out two costs: **increased chip area and reduced algorithmic accuracy** (Introduction). Moreover, analog circuits require customization, are sensitive to process variations, and cannot reuse digital design flows. In contrast, digital MFCC does not require custom analog circuits and is easier to deploy on IoT devices—this is the fundamental stance of the authors for choosing to "make digital MFCC itself more power-efficient" rather than "switching to an analog front-end."

Specifically regarding the digital MFCC line, there are precedents: ISSCC'20 [15] achieved 336 nW FEx using a pipelined FFT, JSSC'23 [14] achieved 180 nW using radix-2 real FFT, and JSSC'24 [35] achieved 111.6 nW (Table III). However, their FFT butterfly operations still use true multipliers (multiplying 8-10 bit twiddle factors). Their optimization direction is pipelining and bit-width control, without addressing whether "multiplication itself can be eliminated." This paper targets this gap: **quantizing twiddle factors to a set of "shift-friendly" discrete values, reducing multiplication to shifts and additions**.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the paper aims to simultaneously balance four mutually constraining goals: (1) Remove multipliers from the entire MFCC pipeline without causing a collapse in KWS accuracy—the quantization of twiddle factors must not be too coarse, as it would directly pollute spectral resolution; the paper needs to find the "coarsest but usable" quantization level; (2) After removing multipliers, the area and power saved by computational units must not be eaten back by multiple sets of dedicated arithmetic units—the Power, Mel, and DCT operations must share the same configurable processing element; (3) Storage power must be reduced synchronously—window function coefficients are simply not stored; (4) The overall design must achieve a competitive 2 µW-level full-chip measurement in 65 nm legacy process at 0.55 V near-threshold voltage, not just simulation. The final result is: E3bT-FFT reduces FFT multiplication power by 5.7 times (Fig. 7(b)), MF-MFCC reduces MFCC computational load to 13.5% (7.3 times, Table I), the chip is 0.17 mm², 2.3 µW, and achieves 94.9% accuracy for 1-Word KWS on the Google Speech Commands dataset (Abstract, Fig. 9(a)).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire chip (Fig. 6(a)) consists of two main blocks: the **MF-MFCC processor** (feature extraction accelerator) and the **Deep Learning Accelerator (DLA)**. After the speech signal enters the chip, it is stored in a ping-pong FIFO (two 128×16 bit FIFOs alternating read/write, corresponding to 8 kHz sampling, 16 ms frame shift = 128 samples per frame, Table III). The MF-MFCC processor calculates features in a four-step pipeline: windowing → FFT → power/Mel filtering → logarithm/DCT. Among these, the power, Mel filtering, and DCT steps share the same set of configurable multiplier-free processing elements (MF-PE). The calculated features are sent to the DLA for 1-Word keyword decision. The top-level controller and SPI interface are responsible for weight loading (initial stored content during testing is burned into the PCB from a PC via SPI, Section IV). Besides FFT, there is an FFT conjugate reuse branch (16b), which is a standard practice for radix-2 real FFT: the real signal is treated as a complex number for N/2-point FFT, and then the N-point real spectrum is extracted using conjugate symmetry, saving half of the FFT computation.

The hierarchy of design motivation is clear. The first layer is **attacking multipliers**: the power of digital near-threshold designs is roughly proportional to the number of switched bits. Multipliers are a disaster zone for bit switching, so all five levels (window, FFT, power, Mel, DCT) undergo "multiplication → shift+addition" replacement. The second layer is **attacking storage**: after quantizing the window function, coefficients no longer need to be stored (Section II); twiddle factors only require a small 64×4 bit storage body (Fig. 6(a)); on-chip storage is implemented using latches to reduce power (Section IV). The third layer is **attacking area**: Power/Mel/DCT operations share a single MF-PE, and the DLA uses a binary network to replace multipliers in PE with MUXes and inverters. The entire design is a co-design chain of "algorithmic approximation for circuit simplification," with each level of approximation backed by accuracy experiments (Table II).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Quantized Window Function (Eq. (1))**: The Hamming window is originally a continuous function $W(x) = 0.54 - 0.46\cos(2\pi x/(N-1))$, requiring 256 floating-point multiplications per frame for windowing. The paper quantizes it into a four-level step function with "the distance from the sample index to the frame center N/2" as the independent variable: the closest distance takes 1, followed by 0.5, 0.25, and the outermost takes 0 (Eq. (1), threshold levels in the extracted text appear as 4/8/15 in magnitude of N/16). Thus, windowing becomes one of three operations: pass-through (multiply by 1), right shift by one bit (multiply by 0.5), right shift by two bits (multiply by 0.25), or zeroing (multiply by 0). A side product is that window function coefficients do not need to be stored at all—the hardware window module is simply a selector plus shift logic (Fig. 6(a)), determining the window value based on the input audio index and executing the corresponding shift (Section III), synchronously reducing storage power and area.

**E3bT Twiddle Factor and Multiplier-Free Butterfly (Eq. (2))**: This is the most core step in the entire paper. The butterfly operation of radix-2 real FFT includes complex multiplication of input and twiddle factors, equivalent to 4 real multiplications plus 6 additions (Section II). The paper quantizes the twiddle factors and "extends one sign bit," obtaining the E3bT (expanded-3bit-twiddle) set: $\{0, \pm0.25, \pm0.5, \pm0.75, \pm1.0\}$—ordinary 3-bit (1 sign + 2 magnitude) can only represent 0/0.25/0.5/0.75 and cannot represent 1.0, but with the extended sign bit, +1.0 can be encoded (Fig. 4(a)). +1.0 must be representable because the first group of butterfly twiddle factors in each FFT stage is constantly $W^0=1$; without it, the entire spectrum would be scaled. Multiplication is subsequently split into two halves (Section II): first perform numerical calculation, then perform sign calculation. The numerical calculation is Eq. (2):

- $|twiddle| = 0 \rightarrow$ output 0
- $|twiddle| = 0.25 \rightarrow x >> 2$ (right shift by two bits)
- $|twiddle| = 0.5 \rightarrow x >> 1$ (right shift by one bit)
- $|twiddle| = 0.75 \rightarrow (x >> 2) + (x >> 1)$ (two shifts and one addition, because $0.75 = 0.5 + 0.25$)
- $|twiddle| = 1 \rightarrow x$ pass-through

Sign calculation is handled separately: determine whether to negate the numerical result based on the product of the twiddle factor and multiplicand signs. **The essence of this replacement is approximating arbitrary coefficient multiplication as a polynomial of powers of 2**—0.25 and 0.5 are pure shifts (zero cost in hardware), 0.75 costs one addition, and 1 and 0 are pass-through and zeroing. Fig. 7(a) statistics the occurrence frequency of each level of twiddle factors in a 256-point FFT; those with absolute value 0.75 account for 24.4%—meaning about one-quarter of the butterflies must pay for the more expensive "two shifts plus one addition" level. Nevertheless, compared to true multiplication with 8-bit twiddle factors, this still reduces multiplication power by 5.7 times (Fig. 7(b)).

**Add-Power (Eq. (3))**: Theoretical MFCC requires calculating the magnitude of the complex spectrum $P = \sqrt{x^2_{real} + x^2_{imag}}$, involving squaring (multiplication) and square root. The paper replaces this with $P(x) = |x_{real}| + |x_{imag}|$ (Eq. (3)), replacing all multiplications and square roots with absolute value and addition. The upper bound of this approximation error is clear: when the real and imaginary parts have equal amplitude, $|x_r|+|x_i|$ overestimates $\sqrt{x_r^2+x_i^2}$ by $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ times. It works because it is followed by Mel filter energy accumulation and logarithmic compression—the logarithm turns multiplicative errors into additive biases, and the classifier is retrained with these features, allowing it to learn the systematic bias. Table II shows that switching to Add-Power alone only reduces 10-Word accuracy from 94.04% to 92.51% (-1.53 percentage points), offering extremely high cost-performance.

**Rectangular Mel Filtering and E3bT-DCT**: The weights within each band of the triangular Mel filter bank are arbitrary real numbers, and accumulation is weighted multiplication; the paper switches to rectangular bandpass filter banks [12], where weights within the band are all 1, reducing filtering to "direct accumulation of power within the band," leaving only addition (Section II). The cosine twiddle factors in DCT are also quantized to the E3bT set, reusing the shift-add logic of Eq. (2). Logarithmic operation is implemented using a lookup table (LOG module with LUT in Fig. 2; LOG is 8b input 4b output in Fig. 6(a)).

**Total Computational Account (Table I)**: Comparison between theoretical MFCC and MF-MFCC step by step—Window: 256 mults → 0; FFT: 2048 mults 3072 adds → 0 mults 7168 adds; Power: 256 mults 128 adds → 0 mults 128 adds; Mel filtering: 208 mults 208 adds → 0 mults 208 adds; DCT: 400 mults 400 adds → 0 mults 400 adds. Total multiplications are cleared from 3168, additions increase from 3808 to 7904 (additions double but each addition is far cheaper than multiplication), **bit operations drop from 932,864 to 126,464, i.e., 13.5%, equivalent to a 7.3-fold reduction in computational load**. Using bit operations instead of operation counts as a metric is deliberate: bit operations approximate gate-level switching activity. At near-threshold voltages, dynamic power is basically proportional to switching activity, making it a metric closer to the truth of energy consumption than "number of multiplications."

### Key Technical Innovation 1: E3bT-FFT—"Coarsest Usable" Quantization of Twiddle Factors

The selection of E3bT levels is not arbitrary; the paper provides two levels of evidence. The first level is **spectral resolution analysis** (Fig. 4): performing FFT on multi-frequency sine combinations input from 100–2000 Hz, the baseline FFT with 8-bit twiddle factors has good resolution (Fig. 4(b)); E3bT maintains accurate performance in the 0–1000 Hz range, but resolution drops in the 1000–2000 Hz range (Fig. 4(c)); while E2bT (2-bit) frequency resolution decays rapidly and introduces significant noise to the spectrum (Fig. 4(d)). This indicates that 3-bit is the critical level "from collapse to usable"—saving one more bit makes it unusable. The second level is **end-to-end task accuracy** (Table II): using E3bT-FFT alone, 10-Word KWS accuracy drops from 94.04% to 90.69% (-3.35 percentage points), which is the largest drop among all single approximations, but it brings a 5.7-fold decrease in FFT power (Fig. 7(b)). Why is fidelity in 0–1000 Hz sufficient? The paper states that 0–2000 Hz covers the main frequency range of human hearing (Section II), and the fundamental frequency and energy of the first and second formants of speakers are concentrated below 1 kHz. The resolution loss in the 1–2 kHz segment is absorbed by classifier retraining. It is also worth noting that the resolution drop in 1000–2000 Hz is a double-edged sword: it potentially affects the details of fricative consonants in the upper frequency band of the 20–4 kHz audio capture bandwidth. The paper proves this cost is acceptable using final accuracy numbers.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Multiplier-Free MFCC (MF-MFCC) Full-Pipeline Approximation

E3bT is only one level of FFT; the paper's contribution is extending the same "shift-friendly coefficient" idea throughout the entire MFCC pipeline: quantized window (four-level step), Add-Power (absolute value sum replacing magnitude), rectangular Mel filtering, E3bT-DCT, plus the inherently multiplier-free LUT logarithm. The five levels of approximation can be independently toggled on or off; the ablation in Table II is to turn them on step by step to observe accuracy. The combined 10-Word accuracy is 89.73%, 4.31 percentage points lower than the theoretical MFCC's 94.04%—the paper's attitude towards this account is frank: the approximations of window, Mel, power, and DCT have minimal impact on accuracy (all within 1.5 percentage points), and the main cost comes from E3bT-FFT, but it significantly reduces power (Section II). This is a typical "spending accuracy budget on the blade's edge": for the same 3 percentage point drop, spending it on FFT exchanges for 5.7 times power reduction, while spending it elsewhere exchanges for only a fraction.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Configurable Multiplier-Free Processing Element (MF-PE) and Circuit Reuse with Binary DLA

If the power saved by algorithmic approximation is eaten back by three sets of independent arithmetic units, the effort is wasted. The paper's circuit answer is **MF-PE** (Fig. 6(c)): a minimal processing element consisting of an adder, a MUX, and an E3bT multiplier, switching roles via configuration—when doing power, the two inputs first take absolute value and then sum via adder; when doing Mel filtering, inputs are accumulated via adder and register; when doing DCT, inputs are first multiplied by E3bT and then accumulated via adder and register, and pass through logarithmic processing before entering MF-PE (Section III). One PE supports three pipeline stages, saving both power and area.

The E3bT butterfly unit (Fig. 6(b)) consists of 4 E3bT multipliers and 6 adders, exactly corresponding to the structure of complex multiplication: 4 real mults + 6 real adds—the multipliers are replaced by "E3bT multipliers" (shift-add networks), and adders are retained as is.

The DLA side also implements multiplier-free design: 8 PEs plus 1 batch normalization PE (BN-PE). Network weights are binarized to 1 bit ($\pm1$), activations are quantized to 8 bits, **binary PEs replace traditional multipliers with MUXes and inverters**, and results are sent to an adder tree for accumulation (Section III)—multiplying by $\pm1$ is essentially "selecting the original value or the negated value," which is the本职 of a MUX. After each CNN/FC layer calculation, BN-PE performs batch normalization. BN weights are 8-bit fixed-point numbers, still requiring one 8-bit multiplier to execute (Section III)—this is the last remaining multiplier in the entire chip, because BN scaling coefficients are continuous values and cannot be shift-friendly. The DLA configuration is minimal (weight storage 78×8 bit, feature storage 82×8 bit, Fig. 6(a)), which also explains why only a 1-Word (3-layer CNN + 1-layer FC) classifier runs on-chip, while 10-Word (7-layer CNN + 1-layer FC) can only run off-chip (Table III classifier column marks on-chip DS-CNN and off-chip DS-CNN).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the three existing routes, the difference in this work is **replacing "bit-width/pipelining optimization of true multipliers" with "discretizing coefficients to combinations of powers of 2"**. For digital MFCC, ISSCC'20 [15] (pipelined FFT) and JSSC'24 [35] (pipelined FFT) optimize FFT architecture and bit-width, but multipliers are still present in the butterfly; JSSC'23 [14] uses radix-2 real FFT to reduce operations, but twiddle factors are also conventionally quantized. This paper first constrains twiddle factors to $\{0, \pm0.25, \pm0.5, \pm0.75, \pm1\}$, making multiplication disappear at the arithmetic level, and then extends the same logic to window, power, Mel, and DCT. Compared to the analog feature extraction route (ASP schemes like ISSCC'22 [26], JSSC'23 [33], etc.), this paper maintains a fully digital flow, requiring no analog front-end (Analog Front-End column is No in Table III), exchanging for portability and controllable accuracy, at the cost of FEx total power not matching the best analog solutions (1771 nW vs. 35 nW [34], 80 nW [33]). Compared to the classifier-side route (on-chip RNN in VLSI'19 [16]), this paper's classifier achieves binary DS-CNN with multiplier-free PE, with DLA power of 0.53 µW (Table III).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Both training and testing use the **Google Speech Commands Dataset (GSCD)** [4]. Two tasks: 1-Word KWS trains on four words 'happy', 'dog', 'up', 'down' respectively, with other GSCD keywords acting as filler words (filters); 10-Word KWS takes ten words 'yes', 'no', 'up', 'down', 'left', 'right', 'on', 'off', 'stop', 'go' (Section II, Section IV). The evaluation audio constructed during the chip measurement phase is quite sophisticated: all keywords and filler words are randomly concatenated, and silence is mixed in at a **1:6 ratio**, forming **1 hour** of audio samples, fed to the chip by an FPGA generating 8 kHz digital audio streams (Section IV). Choosing to concatenate long audio rather than testing isolated clips one by one is to simulate the real load of continuous listening on always-on devices—silence accounting for 6/7 corresponds to the real scenario where speech appears sparsely. The specific scale of training/testing splits for the dataset is not reported in the paper (GSCD v0.02 commonly has about 105,000 clips, ~30 words; this paper does not specify the version or split).

### Definition and Rationale of Evaluation Metrics

- **KWS Accuracy**: Measured by classification accuracy rate; 1-Word reports average of four words, 10-Word reports average of ten words (including confusion matrix Fig. 5(b)). The reason for choosing it is direct: every step of approximation in this paper may harm accuracy; accuracy is the "payment currency" for all power gains, and must be accounted for step by step.
- **Bit-Op**: The core column in Table I, converting each operation into gate-level bit operations. The reason for choosing it is that it is closer to circuit switching power than multiplication/addition counts, especially when multiplication is replaced by shifts; "operation count" metrics would be distorted (multiplications cleared, additions doubled), while bit operations reflect real savings.
- **Layered Power Reporting**: Table III splits power into FFT computational power, FEx computational power, FEx storage power, FEx total power, DLA power, and total power. The reason for this split is given by Fig. 9(c) in the paper—only by layering can one see the fact that "after saving 7.3 times in computation, the bottleneck shifts to storage."
- **Process Conditions**: 65 nm CMOS, 0.55 V, 125 kHz (Fig. 8). Measuring power at near-threshold low frequency corresponds to the real operating point of always-on IoT.

In terms of audio bit-width, this work actually uses 16-bit input (Table III Audio Bit-width row, most comparative works use 8-10 bits), compensating for posterior approximations with front-end precision. The overall chip layout is 0.85×0.85 mm², on-chip storage 1.6 KB (specifications in Fig. 8), core area 0.17 mm² (Abstract, Table III); of the 1.6 KB storage, 1188 bytes are for the MF-MFCC accelerator and 493 bytes for the DLA, implemented using latches to reduce power (Section IV).

Measurement methodology (Section IV, Fig. 9): under the condition of 1-hour concatenated audio and 1:6 silence ratio, the 1-Word chip measurement accuracy is **94.9%**, higher than the 93.4% for 1-Word with MF-MFCC features in simulation (Section II); at 0.55 V supply, the whole chip consumes **2.3 µW** (Fig. 9(b) gives the power vs. voltage curve). Power breakdown (Fig. 9(c)): MFCC computational power is 76 nW, accounting for only 3.3% of the whole chip; FEx total power is 1771 nW, accounting for about 77% of the whole chip (of which storage is 1695 nW, computation is 76 nW, Table III); subtracting FEx leaves about 0.53 µW, which is the DLA power (consistent with Table III's DLA Power 0.53 µW). This breakdown is the most important figure in the paper: **multiplier-free optimization reduces FEx computational power to only 3.3%, but storage power becomes the new primary bottleneck**.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Table II is the algorithm-side ablation, using 10-Word KWS accuracy as the benchmark, replacing components of theoretical MFCC item by item:

| Configuration | 10-Word Accuracy | Loss relative to Theoretical MFCC |
|---|---|---|
| Theoretical MFCC (Baseline) | 94.04% | — |
| Quantized Window | 93.04% | −1.00 |
| E3bT-FFT | 90.69% | −3.35 |
| Add-Power | 92.51% | −1.53 |
| Rectangular Mel Filtering | 93.74% | −0.30 |
| E3bT-DCT | 93.74% | −0.30 |
| Full MF-MFCC | 89.73% | −4.31 |

Three findings are worth expanding. **First, approximations are not equivalent**: Rectangular Mel filtering and E3bT-DCT are almost free (each dropping only 0.30 percentage points), quantized window and Add-Power are cheap (about 1-1.5 percentage points), while E3bT-FFT is the only major accuracy cost (3.35 percentage points)—consistent with the spectral analysis in Fig. 4, the loss is concentrated in the resolution drop at 1-2 kHz, partially absorbed by classifier retraining. **Second, combination errors are approximately additive**: Full MF-MFCC drops 4.31 percentage points, which is slightly better than the sum of individual losses (about 6.5), indicating that the error sources of each approximation are relatively independent and do not have malignant coupling. **Third, the exchange rate of accuracy for power is asymmetric**: E3bT-FFT exchanges 3.35 percentage points for a 5.7-fold reduction in FFT multiplication power (Fig. 7(b)), while the other four levels of approximation combined drop only about 1 percentage point but contribute to the complete bit operation reduction of the window/power/Mel/DCT four levels in Table I (theoretical total of about 340,000 bit operations compressed to about 12,000). The paper's own summary of the ablation is: the approximations of window, Mel, power, and DCT have the least impact on accuracy, while E3bT-FFT slightly reduces accuracy but significantly saves power (Section II).

Additionally, the twiddle factor usage statistics in Fig. 7(a) constitute a "hardware-side ablation": in a 256-point FFT, the $|T|=0.75$ level appears with a frequency of 24.4%, indicating that the most expensive shift-add combination accounts for only one-quarter, while other levels (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1) are almost free—this explains why overall multiplication power drops by 5.7 times rather than just a little. The comparison of two classifier scales for 1-Word and 10-Word (Fig. 5(a): 3CNN+1FC vs. 7CNN+1FC) gives the impact of task complexity on approximation robustness: 1-Word still has 93.4% under MF-MFCC features, while 10-Word drops to 89.6%, indicating that the more categories, the more sensitive the system is to feature damage.

## Main Contributions

1. **E3bT-FFT**: Proposes an extended 3-bit twiddle factor quantization algorithm, decomposing complex multiplication in FFT butterflies into "numerical shift-add + sign judgment," supported by spectral resolution analysis (Fig. 4) and task accuracy verification (Table II), achieving a 5.7-fold reduction in multiplication power for 256-point FFT (Fig. 7(b)).
2. **MF-MFCC Full-Pipeline Multiplier-Free**: Strings together quantized window, Add-Power, rectangular Mel filtering, and E3bT-DCT into a complete multiplier-free MFCC, reducing bit operations to 13.5% of theoretical MFCC (7.3 times, Table I), clearing multiplications from 3168 times, while eliminating window function coefficient storage.
3. **Configurable MF-PE and Circuit Synergy with Binary DLA**: Power/Mel/DCT operations share a single multiplier-free processing element; the DLA uses 1-bit weights to replace PE multipliers with MUXes and inverters, implementing the "multiplier-free" design criterion from algorithm to layout.
4. **65 nm Silicon Verification**: Tape-out measurement shows 0.17 mm², 2.3 µW at 0.55 V, FEx computational power only 76 nW (3.3% of whole chip), and 94.9% accuracy on 1-Word GSCD. Compared to VLSI'19 [16] (also 65 nm digital MFCC), FEx power is reduced 4 times, and area shrinks 15 times (2.56 mm² → 0.17 mm²).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Accuracy cost amplifies in multi-class tasks**: MF-MFCC drops 10-Word accuracy from 94.04% to 89.73% (Table II), while comparative works achieve 98.1% (1 word) with 28 nm JSSC'24 [35] and 97.3% (1 word) with ISSCC'20 [15]. The accuracy ceiling of approximate features is lower than the combination of full-precision features and conventional quantized classifiers; as the number of categories continues to increase, the gap may widen further.
- **Spectral resolution damaged at 1-2 kHz**: E3bT resolution drop at 1000-2000 Hz is admitted by the paper (Fig. 4(c)). It is harmless to vowels with energy concentrated in low frequencies, but wake words relying on high-frequency fricative details (words containing /s/, /f/, etc.) may be more prone to confusion; the paper does not perform error analysis by phoneme category.
- **Storage power becomes a new bottleneck and is not fundamentally solved**: After multiplier-free optimization, FEx computation is only 76 nW, but storage is 1695 nW, accounting for 96% of FEx (Table III, Fig. 9(c)). Latch memory only alleviates the issue; about 77% of the 2.3 µW total power is spent on FEx, with the majority being storage—the algorithmic dividends have been fully consumed, and the next step must involve storage architecture (e.g., more aggressive per-frame streaming computation to save feature caching).
- **BN-PE still retains an 8-bit multiplier**: The 8-bit fixed-point scaling of batch normalization cannot be shift-friendly (Section III); the multiplier-free design is not thorough in the DLA; if the network is deepened, the power proportion of this multiplier will rise.
- **Dependence of approximation on SNR is not characterized**: The relative error of Add-Power is largest when real and imaginary parts have equal amplitude (overestimating by about $\sqrt{2}$ times); additive noise will increase the frequency of such boundary cases. All tests in the paper are based on relatively clean recordings from GSCD, and noise robustness curves are not reported.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Inconsistency between measured and simulated accuracy without explanation**: 1-Word chip measurement is 94.9% (Section IV), higher than the same configuration simulation of 93.4% (Section II); usually, measurement should be lower than simulation; the evaluation criteria are different (chip uses 1-hour random concatenation, 1:6 silence long audio, simulation presumably uses standard test set). The paper does not align the criteria or provide an explanation, leaving readers unable to judge whether 94.9% is comparable to other works' accuracy.
- **Lack of horizontal accuracy under standard protocols**: The GSCD community commonly uses test set splits and per-word reporting. This paper only reports average accuracy and simulation confusion matrix (Fig. 5(b)), with no per-word accuracy/false alarm rate/ROC-like metrics; the false accept rate, crucial for wake-up applications, is not reported.
- **Dataset version and scale not reported**: GSCD version number, training/validation/testing splits, and vocabulary pruning rules are not given, requiring guessing for reproduction.
- **10-Word verified only off-chip**: The 89.6% for 10-Word in Table III is an off-chip DS-CNN result; the on-chip DLA only handles 1-Word; the combination of "on-chip multiplier-free full pipeline + multi-word accuracy" has no silicon proof.
- **10-Word ablation baseline does not include 1-Word criteria**: Table II only reports item-by-item ablation for 10-Word; sensitivity of each approximation level under the 1-Word task is not given, while 1-Word is exactly the task the chip actually runs.
- **Process and voltage conditions are not unified**: Comparing works in 28 nm/0.36-0.41 V directly with this work's 65 nm/0.55 V for absolute power, without process-normalized energy efficiency (e.g., pJ/operation) comparison, reduces fairness.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Attack on the storage side**: Since Fig. 9(c) shows storage accounts for 96% of FEx power, the most direct next step is to change the MFCC pipeline to fully streaming (per-sample in, per-feature out), reducing the 256×16 bit feature storage body, or using near-threshold storage cells / feature compression to reduce access power.
- **Joint search for twiddle factor levels and word length**: E3bT is manually selected (3-bit plus extended sign); it is conceivable to perform frequency-band adaptation on the set $\{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1\}$ (finer levels for low-frequency bands, coarser levels for high-frequency bands), or use training-aware quantization to further reduce the number of additions on the basis of the 24.4% proportion of the 0.75 level (Fig. 7(a)).
- **Orthogonal superposition with VAD multi-stage wake-up**: This paper only implements the third type of method in Fig. 1 (reducing FEx), which is completely orthogonal to the first type (VAD frame skipping [14]). Superimposing both is expected to further reduce the 2.3 µW always-on power.
- **Approximation-aware training**: Currently, it is a two-step method of "defining approximation first, then training classifier"; writing E3bT-FFT and Add-Power into the forward graph for end-to-end joint training (similar to quantization-aware training) is expected to recover part of the 4.31 percentage point loss (Table II), supporting on-chip deployment for vocabularies of 10 words or more.
- **Completing false alarm and noise evaluation**: Following streaming KWS conventions, add false reject / false accept curves and noisy tests to verify the boundaries of shift-friendly approximations in real acoustic environments, which is essential evidence for landing smart speakers and earphone products.
