## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Smart speakers have grown into one of the largest categories of permanent voice entry points in consumer electronics. Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the gatekeeper for the entire voice interaction chain of these devices: the device must monitor microphone signals around the clock, activating the system the moment a wake word is heard, and then handing off subsequent speech to cloud-based or local Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR). This gatekeeper role imposes three engineering shackles. First is always-on operation; KWS cannot sleep, and power consumption and CPU usage directly determine device battery life and heat dissipation. Therefore, the entire algorithm is typically restricted to running on a single CPU core. Second is the far-field low Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR); users often speak from several meters away, while noise sources such as televisions, kitchen appliances, street traffic, and vacuum cleaners are simultaneously active in the room. Papers have measured scenarios with SNR as low as -25 dB under laboratory conditions (the -25 dB SNR tier in Table 1). Third is the high cost of false alarms; a smart speaker that wakes up frequently will be unplugged by users. Thus, the system must operate at an extremely low false alarm rate (the paper fixes this uniformly at 0.1 false alarms per hour).

The starting point of this paper is built on a unique opportunity on the device side: Cloud ASR utilizing multiple microphone channels is almost infeasible due to uplink network bandwidth constraints; however, on-device wake word detection has no bandwidth issues—all microphone channels on the chip are readily available. The paper cites a simple yet apt analogy: humans focus on specific sound sources in noisy environments using a binaural mechanism, and multichannel algorithms can similarly ignore noise components to some extent. The specific experimental subject is a smart speaker equipped with a seven-microphone array: one microphone is located at the center of the array, and the other six are arranged in a ring with a radius of 4.2 cm, with adjacent microphones spaced 60 degrees apart (Section 2.1). The center microphone naturally forms an omnidirectional (omni) channel, while the six ring microphones can synthesize beams pointing in various directions. The problem then becomes: given an omnidirectional channel, several directional beam channels, and an adaptive noise cancellation channel, how should the KWS model utilize them?

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper systematically reviews four categories of shortcomings in existing approaches in the introduction and Section 2.

The first category is the data augmentation route (References [4][5][6]). By adding noise to training data and performing joint training for signal restoration, robustness in some noisy scenarios can indeed be alleviated. However, the paper explicitly points out that it does not fundamentally solve the problem under noisy conditions—the model essentially still works on single-channel distorted signals; the noise is not physically eliminated, only "seen."

The second category is single-channel neural denoising (Reference [7]). Jointly training a speech enhancement network with the KWS model has the disadvantage of high training complexity, as the two objectives are entangled. Moreover, single-channel denoising itself is information-theoretically disadvantaged at low SNR—the mixed signal received by one microphone has an underdetermined separation space.

The third category is the beamforming (BF) route. Directional beamformers are robust under certain conditions, but the abstract hits the nail on the head with one sentence: they may reduce the performance of activating the system by distorting or suppressing useful signals. Beams are essentially spatial filters; when the direction is incorrect, the user moves, or the keyword happens to fall on the beam's sidelobes, the keyword signal itself is weakened. Notably, the six static beams used in the paper cover 360 degrees, with each beam responsible for a 60-degree sector. The signal within the beam is complete and clean, but distortion in the beam edges and sidelobe regions is unavoidable in static designs.

The fourth category is the Adaptive Noise Cancellation (ANC) route (References [15][11]). Huang et al.'s method is clever: it classifies the direction of sounds recorded for more than one second before the activation moment as noise directions and performs adaptive cancellation. Reference [11] extended this to multiple microphones and found that a three-microphone scheme was most effective. However, the paper also points out its weakness—in high SNR scenarios, or when the user speaks something else before saying the wake word (e.g., humming a song first), ANC may misjudge the wake word signal as noise and suppress it. This is not a theoretical defect; the paper's empirical data confirms it directly: in Table 4, Base KWS + ANC using only the ANC channel had an FRR of 8.8% on the real test set, which was worse than the baseline without any denoising (7.5%).

Furthermore, there is no standardized answer for how to aggregate multichannel information. The most intuitive ensemble approach—running a separate model for each channel and performing a logical OR on the outputs—significantly increases the false alarm rate (logical OR only relaxes the decision). It requires additional grid search for per-channel thresholds to pull the false alarm rate back to the target level, and the inference cost doubles with the number of channels (in Table 3, the ensemble scheme has an RTF of 0.36, nearly twice that of the attention scheme's 0.21). The paper's introduction summarizes this plainly: currently, there is no standardized method for using multiple microphones for KWS.

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

In summary, this paper aims to satisfy four mutually constraining goals simultaneously: First, it must reap the benefits of the denoising channel at low SNR while preserving the advantage of the omnidirectional channel at high SNR without distortion, avoiding the trade-offs seen in using ANC alone. Second, the overhead of the channel aggregation mechanism must be minimal; it cannot double computation and memory like the ensemble approach, because the entire system must fit within the budget of a single-core CPU. Third, training data is severely imbalanced—there are ten million pseudo-labeled utterances for the omnidirectional channel, but only 500,000 for multichannel data. The training pipeline must naturally derive a multichannel model from a single-channel baseline, rather than starting from scratch. Fourth, the method must be validated effectively under both controlled laboratory conditions and real end-user data, with neither being optional.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system can be viewed in three layers: the signal processing frontend, the channel aggregation layer, and the KWS backbone network.

The signal processing frontend provides two types of candidate channels. The first is the ANC channel, adopting the three-microphone adaptive noise cancellation algorithm from Reference [11]. The reason for choosing the three-microphone scheme is that the original paper found through experiments that the three-microphone configuration yielded the best results. The reason for choosing ANC over more complex multi-channel schemes is that the baseline tests in Table 2 later show its RTF on the target hardware is only 0.070, about 60% of the six-beamforming schemes (0.112), and its denoising quality is actually better. The second type is six static directional beams plus an omnidirectional channel, replicating the multi-beam approach from Reference [9] as a comparative baseline. The reason for using six beams instead of the four in Reference [9] is physical: the experimental device's microphone array is seven-microphone (center plus six rings), and the six ring microphones naturally support six beams to seamlessly cover 360 degrees.

The channel aggregation layer is the core proposition of this paper: a small attention network with approximately 50,000 parameters (labeled as SVDF Keys Net in Figure 2 of the paper) is inserted between the feature input and the KWS backbone network. For each input feature and each channel, the attention network outputs a logit, which is normalized into weights via softmax along the channel dimension, and then weighted summed to produce the final feature fed into the backbone network. The deep motivation behind this design is to allow the network to decide frame-by-frame "which channel is most useful at this moment": at high SNR, the omnidirectional channel is clean and complete, so weights should favor it; at low SNR, the ANC channel has a high SNR, so weights should shift to ANC. This decision does not have to be a binary choice—the weighted sum allows the network to use a combination of both channels. The paper specifically points out in the results discussion that this is one of the key reasons why the attention scheme outperforms the ensemble scheme: ensemble can only take the OR from independent channel judgments, while attention can also utilize weighted combinations between channels.

The KWS backbone network is a single-channel streaming architecture similar to SVDF (Reference [1], Alvarez and Park's end-to-end streaming keyword recognition). The paper does not explicitly state the reason for choosing it, but it can be deduced clearly from engineering constraints: it is a streaming frame-by-frame prediction architecture, naturally suited for always-on monitoring; it has approximately 750,000 parameters, a model size of 3.4 MB, an RTF of 0.18 on the target hardware (Table 3), and can run on a single core. The input is a sequence of sound frames with a frame length of 25 ms and a frame shift of 10 ms. The feature pipeline calculates 40-dimensional log-Mel filter bank features per frame, and the model outputs the keyword probability per frame. The attention network is deliberately made very small (approximately 50,000 parameters, only 1/15th of the backbone network). The motivation is clear—the existence of the aggregation layer must not break the on-device budget. In practice, it only increased the overall RTF from 0.18 to 0.21 and the model size from 3.4 MB to 3.5 MB (Table 3), an increment of about 17% in RTF and 0.1 MB in storage.

Regarding multichannel configuration, the paper's proposition is "ANC channel + omnidirectional channel" as a two-channel input, rather than feeding all seven microphones directly into the network. Why not learn raw multichannel directly? The paper's introduction borrows the approach from Reference [8] (using attention to aggregate raw signals from clean microphones) and modifies it: first, traditional signal processing produces high-quality denoised channels, and then the attention mechanism chooses between the "distorted but denoised channel" and the "undistorted but noisy channel." This is more parameter-efficient, more controllable, and aligns better with the transfer path of existing large-scale single-channel models than letting a neural network learn spatial filtering from raw multichannel data itself.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The mathematical form of attention aggregation is extremely concise (Equations 1 and 2 of the paper). Let the input features be $z_{ij}$, where subscript $i$ is the channel index and $j$ is the feature index. The attention network $\psi$ predicts a logit $e_{ij} = \psi_j(z_i)$ for the feature vector of each channel, followed by softmax normalization along the channel dimension:

$$
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_i \exp(e_{ij})}
$$

After obtaining the weights $\alpha_{ij}$, the final feature fed into the backbone network is the convex combination of features from each channel:

$$
z^*_j = \sum_i \alpha_{ij} \cdot z_{ij}
$$

There are three details worth pondering in this formula. First, softmax is performed independently per feature—each Mel frequency band and each frame has its own channel weight, not a single weight shared across the entire spectrum. This means the network can perform fine-grained mixing, such as trusting the omnidirectional channel in low-frequency bands and ANC in high-frequency bands. Second, the convex combination (weights sum to one) ensures that the numerical range of the output features is of the same order of magnitude as the input features. The input distribution of the backbone network will not drift due to aggregation, which is likely one of the reasons why subsequent fine-tuning converges stably. Third, the architecture of the attention network is similar to the backbone network but with far fewer parameters (approximately 50,000 vs. 750,000). The paper does not provide details on the number of layers and memory blocks (not reported), only stating that it follows the SVDF style.

Regarding the training objective, based on the encoder-decoder scheme from Reference [1], the paper adds a max-pool per utterance loss (Equations 3 and 4). For a single-phrase KWS model, the utterance-level score is first defined as:

$$
\hat{p} = \prod_{p \in \text{parts}_k} \max_t x_t^p
$$

where $x_t^p$ is the predicted probability of the $p$-th target component at frame $t$, and $\text{parts}_k$ is the set of components constituting the target phrase—for the encoder, these are the phonemes of the target phrase; for the decoder, it is the entire phrase. The utterance-level loss is:

$$
L_{mp} = -(1 - y) \cdot \log(1 - \hat{p}) - y \cdot \log(\hat{p})
$$

where $y$ is the label for the entire utterance (1 for positive samples, 0 for negative samples). The motivation behind this design is worth expanding: the KWS backbone network outputs frame-by-frame probabilities, but training annotations and actual decisions are utterance-level—whether a wake word exists in a piece of speech. Directly performing cross-entropy on each frame would involve a large number of silent frames and non-keyword frames in the gradient, diluting the positive sample signal. Max-pooling takes the "highest probability that this phoneme appeared in this utterance" as evidence of its existence. The multiplication requires that all components of the phrase be detected to count as a wake word—multiplication naturally encodes the integrity constraint of the sequence (if one phoneme is missing, the overall score collapses), while taking the maximum along the time dimension allows components to appear in any order and at any position, bypassing alignment issues. For negative samples ($y=0$), the loss penalizes the high $\hat{p}$ caused by any component having a high probability, which corresponds exactly to false alarms. This is a clean solution that folds streaming frame-by-frame outputs into utterance-level decisions and backpropagates.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Bridging Denoising Channels and Attention Mechanisms

The novelty claim of the paper is stated clearly at the end of the introduction: combining the multichannel denoising algorithm from Reference [11] with the attention mechanism from Reference [8] into the KWS architecture, allowing the model to dynamically switch between input channels. It sounds like a simple combination, but the choice of combination is supported by clear technical judgments. Reference [9] (Ji et al., ICASSP 2020) has already proven that attention plus multi-beams plus omnidirectional channels is effective. This paper does not copy it directly but changes the type of denoising frontend—replacing six directional beams with a three-microphone ANC. The basis is the empirical denoising quality in Table 1: at -15 dB SNR, the FRR of the ANC channel is 2.8%, while the beam channel is 13.5%, a difference of nearly five times; at -20 dB, it is 16.8% vs. 59.8%. ANC has significantly better denoising quality for this array and this type of noise, and lower computational cost (Table 2). Thus, "attention + ANC + omnidirectional" became the paper's recommended configuration. The essence of this innovation is: attention aggregation is not bound to any specific frontend; the choice of frontend should be jointly determined by denoising quality and computational cost. The paper completes this selection argument with data.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Training Pipeline Deriving Multichannel Models from Single-Channel Baselines

The training process is divided into three steps (Section 6.1). Step 1: Train the baseline model using the encoder-decoder setup from Reference [1] with max-pooling loss on omnidirectional channel data. The data used is the most abundant 10 million omnidirectional pseudo-labeled utterances. Step 2: For the attention scheme, connect the attention network to the input of the backbone network and fine-tune the entire model on 500,000 multichannel pseudo-labeled utterances with a setup similar to the baseline. Step 3: For the ensemble scheme, copy the baseline model and fine-tune a second model on ANC channel data. During inference, the two models run in parallel, and their outputs are combined via logical OR.

Why arrange it this way instead of training the multichannel model from scratch? The reason given by the paper is the reality of data distribution: the proprietary KWS dataset is dominated by omnidirectional channels (Section 4.2). The magnitude gap of 10 million to 500,000 means that training a multichannel model from scratch would waste data and easily overfit the small set. By first solidifying the acoustic modeling capabilities on large-scale single-channel data, and then letting the small attention network learn channel selection, the acoustic knowledge of the backbone network is fully inherited. This also explains why the test set FRR dropped from the baseline 7.5% to 5.5% after adding the 50,000-parameter attention layer (Table 4): the additional benefit comes almost entirely from the introduction of channel information, not from an increase in model capacity (for comparison, Base KWS x2 with doubled parameters only dropped to 7.0%).

The ensemble route also exposes an engineering detail: logical OR aggregation initially produced more false alarms than the baseline. The paper adjusted the models to achieve the optimal FRR under a fixed false alarm rate using a grid search for per-channel thresholds, and the optimal solution often had different thresholds for each channel—because the audio data differences between channels can be large (Section 6.1.3). This detail itself is an implicit criticism of the ensemble method: it introduces a discrete hyperparameter space that requires additional tuning, whereas the soft weighting of the attention mechanism is naturally cohesive and requires no per-channel thresholds.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the multi-beam attention scheme in Reference [9], the differences in this paper are threefold: the frontend changed from four beams plus omnidirectional to three-microphone ANC plus omnidirectional (and retained the six-beam version as a control, with empirical FRR of 6.7% vs. 5.5%, Table 4); the number of beams changed from four to six to match the physical layout of the seven-microphone array; and complete on-device resource benchmarks were added (Table 2, Table 3), proving the efficiency advantage of the attention scheme over the ensemble. Compared to the ANC plus dual-model scheme in Reference [11], this paper replaces the "run a separate model for clean and denoised signals and then fuse" approach with a single model plus attention. The RTF dropped from 0.36 for the ensemble to 0.21, and the model size dropped from 6.6 MB to 3.5 MB (Table 3), while the FRR was actually lower (5.5% vs. 6.4%). Compared to methods like Reference [12]'s 3D SVDF, Reference [13]'s 3D CNN, and Reference [14]'s TAC aggregation layer, which feed multichannel raw tensors directly into the network, the philosophy of this paper's route is completely different: first, use signal processing with clear physical knowledge to produce denoised channels, and let the network only be responsible for lightweight channel selection. This reduces parameter and computational overhead by one to two orders of magnitude (the paper does not report direct comparison data for these three methods on this task).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The paper used two complementary private datasets (Section 4).

The first is the acoustic laboratory dataset, used to precisely measure acceptable SNR levels under controlled conditions. The laboratory is a 4.5m x 5.5m soundproof room, designed with the goal of a flat impulse response (very low reverberation and residual noise). The device under test was placed in the center of the room on a flat platform 90 cm above the ground; several sound sources were arranged on a circle with a radius of 2 meters centered on the device, at heights between 70 and 120 cm above the ground, at various angles. Each recording contains one noise source and one keyword source: the noise source is normalized to a 60 dB sound pressure level at the center external microphone; the keyword volume is uniformly sampled from seven levels: 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, and 65 dB. Each recording plays only one pre-recorded keyword speech, and uses only one pre-recorded noise type—kitchen, street, vacuum cleaner, white noise, pink noise, or TV. Keyword speech is used in proportions of male, female, child, etc. The final dataset consists of 900 recordings, covering seven SNR tiers from -25 dB to +5 dB. The brilliance of this design lies in the direct calibration of sound pressure level: by fixing the noise at 60 dB and sweeping the keyword volume, a precisely known SNR gradient is obtained, allowing the plotting of a complete FRR vs. SNR curve (Figure 3).

The second is the KWS dataset, used for training and evaluating the model, coming from end-user recordings, partially manually labeled and partially pseudo-labeled (pseudo-labeling method from Reference [16]'s noise student-teacher training). The corresponding training set for the experiment is 500,000 multichannel pseudo-labeled utterances; the test set is 50,000 manually transcribed multichannel utterances, of which 40,000 contain the target keyword. Additionally, 10 million omnidirectional pseudo-labeled utterances are used for baseline pre-training. The positive sample proportion in the test set is as high as 80%, a bias the paper does not discuss (see Limitations).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

KWS metrics use the classic pair of False Rejection Rate (FRR) and False Alarms per Hour (FA/h) (Section 5.2.1). The rationale given by the paper is clear: these two metrics are not independent; improving one by adjusting the threshold inevitably worsens the other. Therefore, horizontal comparisons must fix the operating point—the paper uniformly tunes all models to FA/h = 0.1 (i.e., an average of one false alarm every ten hours) and then ranks them by FRR. The choice of this operating point reflects product reality: smart speakers have extremely low tolerance for false wake-ups, and competing on "who has more false alarms but fewer rejections" is meaningless.

The quality assessment of denoising algorithms takes another clever path (Section 5.2.2): run the same baseline KWS model on beam, ANC, and omnidirectional channels separately, use the known SNR tiers on the laboratory dataset to plot the FRR vs. SNR curves for each channel (Figure 3), and then interpolate the curves. Take the SNR difference between the omnidirectional channel and the algorithm under test at a fixed FRR level as the "SNR gain" of that algorithm. The advantage of this metric is: it does not evaluate the speech quality metrics of the denoising algorithm itself (such as PESQ, STOI), but directly measures "how much this channel improved the downstream KWS," which is perfectly aligned with the final goal. For beamforming, the paper used an oracle scheme—taking the channel with the highest KWS confidence among multiple channels for evaluation. This is actually a favorable upper bound for beams; the fact that ANC still wins later makes the point even stronger.

Resource metrics use Real-Time Factor (RTF, ratio of algorithm time to audio duration) and model size. Memory is measured using Resident Set Size (RSS), all measured on real hardware: the Amlogic S905X2 (quad-core ARMv8 Cortex-A53, 1.8 GHz) used in smart speakers, with a reference group of a standard desktop PC with Intel Core i7-3540M; the denoising algorithm and keyword recognition each occupy one CPU core. Using a real speaker chip instead of a server for benchmarks is key to the credibility of such on-device papers.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Denoising Channel Quality (Table 1, Acoustic Laboratory, FRR %, four SNR tiers): The omnidirectional source microphone reached 26.7% at -10 dB, 95.3% at -20 dB, and completely failed (100%) at -25 dB; the beam channel was only 0.8% at -10 dB, but deteriorated to 59.8% at -20 dB and 92.9% at -25 dB; the ANC channel was optimal throughout, 0.1% at -10 dB, 2.8% at -15 dB, 16.8% at -20 dB, and 67.1% at -25 dB. Even at the extreme -25 dB, ANC was still about 33 percentage points lower than omnidirectional (67.1% vs. 100%). Note that the beams here are still the oracle upper bound; actual non-oracle beams would be worse.

Main Comparison on Real Test Set (Table 4, fixed FA/h = 0.1, FRR %): Base KWS baseline 7.5%; Base KWS + ANC (using only denoising channel) 8.8%, which is worse; Base KWS x2 (doubled parameters) 7.0%; Ensemble KWS + ANC 6.4%; Attention KWS + BF (six beams + omnidirectional) 6.7%; The proposed Attention KWS + ANC 5.5%, the lowest in the field. Converting relative gains according to Table 4: relative reduction of about 27% against the baseline, 21% against the doubled-parameter scheme, 14% against the ensemble scheme, and 18% against the beam attention scheme.

The information structure of this table is very clean, revealing three layers of causality. First layer: using ANC channel alone actually made things worse (8.8% vs. 7.5%), confirming the defect of ANC suppressing wake word signals in high SNR and user-pre-speech scenarios—the denoising channel alone is unreliable. Second layer: combining the denoising channel with the omnidirectional channel yielded significant benefits: the attention scheme 5.5%, a two-percentage-point drop from the baseline, and also better than simply stacking parameters (7.0%)—indicating that the gain under low SNR conditions comes mainly from multichannel information rather than model capacity. The paper's original words are: "extra channels allow the model to use undistorted audio at high SNR and provide valuable information at low SNR." Third layer: aggregation methods have优劣 (pros and cons): for the same two-channel ANC + omnidirectional, attention 5.5% is better than logical OR ensemble 6.4%. The paper gives two explanations: attention can utilize weighted combinations of channels, not just independent judgments, and the ANC channel itself has better denoising quality (Table 1); the beam attention losing to ANC attention (6.7% vs. 5.5%) is also mainly attributed to the gap in frontend denoising quality.

Resource Overhead (Table 2, Table 3): For the denoising frontend, the RTF of beamforming is 0.112 on S905X2 and 0.015 on i7; ANC is 0.070 and 0.010, with negligible difference in RSS, both about 10 MB. For the KWS model: baseline RTF 0.18 (S905X2), model size 3.4 MB; doubled-parameter version 0.34, 6.9 MB; ensemble 0.36, 6.6 MB; attention + ANC 0.21, 3.5 MB; attention + BF 0.30, 3.5 MB. Summing frontend and model (the paper does not directly give the sum, calculated by adding Table 2 and Table 3): the complete link for attention + ANC is about 0.28 RTF, ensemble + ANC is about 0.43, attention + BF is about 0.41. The conclusion is that the paper's scheme adds only a small overhead to the baseline (0.03 RTF and 0.1 MB on the model side), yet it has the lowest FRR among all schemes, while the ensemble and doubled-parameter schemes have significantly higher RTF and size. The paper therefore judges: all enhanced algorithms are feasible on ARM CPUs, but the proposed scheme is the optimal solution for real-world KWS applications.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have a separate ablation chapter, but the six-scheme comparison matrix in Section 5.1 actually constitutes a well-designed controlled ablation, controlling three independent axes.

Axis 1: Channel Selection (other conditions fixed as single-channel baseline model). Omnidirectional 7.5% vs. ANC 8.8% (Table 4), combined with the denoising quality curve in Table 1, the conclusion is that the denoising channel has high quality but is unreliable alone, while the omnidirectional channel is reliable but fails at low SNR; the two are complementary.

Axis 2: Aggregation Mechanism (fixed ANC + omnidirectional two channels). Logical OR ensemble 6.4% vs. attention 5.5%. The relative advantage of attention is about 14%, and RTF is 0.21 vs. 0.36, size 3.5 MB vs. 6.6 MB—attention wins on both effect and overhead on this axis. The ensemble scheme also carries the engineering burden of per-channel threshold tuning (Section 6.1.3), which is an implicit cost mentioned by the paper but not quantified.

Axis 3: Model Capacity (fixed omnidirectional single channel). Baseline 7.5% vs. doubled parameters 7.0%, relative improvement only about 7%, while introducing a second channel with attention improved by about 27%—capacity expansion is the weakest direction of improvement, and multichannel information is the main source of benefit.

Axis 4 (incidental): Frontend Type (fixed attention aggregation). ANC 5.5% vs. beam 6.7%. The root cause of the gap is explained by the denoising quality data in Table 1. There is also an asymmetry on this axis: the paper did not study the ensemble + beam combination, because running a baseline model on each beam channel is computationally too complex—this gap makes the comparison matrix slightly incomplete, but does not affect the main conclusion.

Ablation findings at the training level also include: the initial false alarm rate of the ensemble model is high after training, requiring per-channel threshold grid search to align to the fixed FA/h operating point; the attention model requires no per-channel thresholds and can be fine-tuned end-to-end.

## Main Contributions

First, the paper proposes a multichannel KWS architecture of ANC denoising channel + omnidirectional channel, aggregated via attention soft weighting. It achieves the lowest FRR of 5.5% on the real test set with fixed FA/h = 0.1 (Table 4), a relative reduction of about 27% compared to the baseline, while the model-side overhead only increases by about 0.03 RTF and 0.1 MB (Table 3)—simultaneously resolving the contradiction between "multichannel benefits" and "on-device budget."

Second, it provides a training pipeline for deriving multichannel models from single-channel baselines: pre-training the backbone on 10 million omnidirectional pseudo-labeled utterances, connecting the ~50,000-parameter attention network, and fine-tuning on 500,000 multichannel utterances. This pipeline bypasses the reality of scarce multichannel training data and has direct engineering value for incremental upgrades of existing single-channel systems on production lines.

Third, it establishes a dual-dataset evaluation paradigm: the laboratory dataset measures channel quality using controlled SNR calibrated by sound pressure level (seven tiers from -25 dB to +5 dB), and the end-user recording dataset measures real-world end-to-end effects. The two layers of evidence support each other.

Fourth, it provides complete real-hardware resource benchmarks (Table 2, Table 3), including RTF, model size, and RSS on dual platforms of ARM speaker chips and desktop CPUs, and thereby argues for the significant computational efficiency advantage of the attention scheme over the ensemble scheme.

Fifth, it provides a transferable empirical judgment: for a smart speaker with a seven-microphone ring array plus center omnidirectional, the denoising quality of the three-microphone ANC frontend is significantly better than that of six static directional beams (Table 1), and the computational cost is lower (Table 2)—frontend selection should be based on "FRR of downstream KWS at various SNRs," not on the theoretical elegance of beamforming.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, the upper limit of the method is capped by the ANC frontend. The attention mechanism can only choose between "distorted but denoised" and "clean but noisy," and cannot repair areas where both fail: Table 1 shows that the ANC channel still has an FRR of 67.1% at -25 dB. In this extreme scenario, the entire scheme is close to failure, and the paper does not provide a way out for lower SNRs. The defects of ANC itself are also transmitted directly—when the SNR is high or the user speaks first, the wake word is misjudged as noise. The paper only relies on the omnidirectional channel as a fallback, without fixing ANC from a mechanistic perspective.

Second, attention aggregation is a linear mixing at the feature level, with no explicit spatial modeling. Soft weights are calculated per feature per frame, so the network cannot express spatial reasoning like "which direction the sound source is in," nor can it utilize phase for delay estimation. Compared to methods like References [12][13] that feed raw multichannel data into 3D convolutions, the expressiveness ceiling of this route is lower. The paper compensates by changing to a better frontend, but does not experimentally prove that this compensation holds in all scenarios.

Third, the proposed configuration uses only two-channel input (ANC + omnidirectional), and the information from the six-beam channels is completely discarded. Theoretically, incorporating more channels into attention (e.g., ANC + optimal beam + omnidirectional, three channels) might be better, but the paper does not explore the search space for channel quantity and combinations.

Fourth, 80% of the training data relies on pseudo-labels (500,000 multichannel training utterances are all pseudo-labeled, plus 10 million omnidirectional pseudo-labeled). The noise in pseudo-labels will be transmitted into the model, and the paper does not quantify the impact of pseudo-labeling quality on the final FRR.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First, the evaluation is bound to a single device model and a single array geometry (seven microphones, 4.2 cm radius ring). There is no generalization verification across arrays and devices—once the array geometry changes, the choice of three-microphone ANC and the number of beams must be re-derived, casting doubt on the extrapolation of the conclusions.

Second, many key experimental details are missing: the wake word itself, its language, and quantity are not disclosed; training hyperparameters (optimizer, learning rate, epochs) are not reported; the specific number of layers and memory block sizes for the SVDF backbone and attention network are not reported; although six types of laboratory noise are listed, the noise composition and SNR distribution of the real test set are not reported. These points combined make independent reproduction quite difficult.

Third, statistical significance is not handled: although the test set in Table 4 is large (50,000 utterances), the difference of one or two percentage points between schemes lacks confidence intervals or significance tests. It is impossible to judge from the paper whether the gap between attention 5.5% and ensemble 6.4% is stable.

Fourth, the operating point is only evaluated at FA/h = 0.1. No complete DET curves under multiple operating points are provided (Figure 3 only plots the FRR vs. SNR curve for laboratory data). It is impossible to judge whether the scheme still dominates under stricter false alarm requirements (e.g., 0.05 per hour).

Fifth, there is a gap in the comparison matrix: the ensemble + beam scheme was not studied due to computational complexity, and the beams used the oracle channel selection upper bound. Real-deployment beam schemes would be worse—the latter is actually a fair bias that gives beams face, which the paper does not discuss regarding the degradation magnitude of non-oracle beams.

Sixth, the positive sample proportion in the test set is about 80% (40,000 target keywords out of 50,000), which is far from the distribution where wake words account for a very small proportion in real deployment. The absolute value of FRR may be overly optimistic.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The directions pointed out by the paper itself in the conclusion are very specific: study which channel or which combination of channels is most effective to transmit from the device to the cloud, allowing cloud ASR and other components of the voice assistant to reap the benefits of multichannel denoising without increasing uplink bandwidth. The paper admits that this task is easy for ensemble models (channel confidences are readily available) but is a greater challenge for attention models—because attention fuses channels into a single feature stream, the original channel-level confidences no longer exist. This confession itself hints at a research gap: designing an attention structure that can output both fused decisions and channel-level confidences.

Based on the paper's evidence, several directions can be inferred. First, make the noise direction judgment of ANC into a neural network and accept backpropagation from the KWS loss, allowing the frontend to "know" which sounds are wake words and should not be suppressed, fixing the high-SNR false suppression problem from a mechanism. Second, extend attention to more channels and add explicit spatial features to test whether feature-level linear mixing is the ceiling of aggregation capability. Third, use the surplus of 10 million omnidirectional utterances for channel-level data augmentation (random channel dropping, gain perturbation on multichannel utterances) to improve the generalization of attention weights. Fourth, complete statistical significance tests and multi-operating-point evaluations, and perform verification across array geometries, pushing the conclusions from a single device to methodology.
