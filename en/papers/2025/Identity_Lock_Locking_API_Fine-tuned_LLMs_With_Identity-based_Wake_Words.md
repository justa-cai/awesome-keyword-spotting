## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The computational and professional barriers to fine-tuning large models are rising, giving rise to API-based fine-tuning as an outsourcing model: organizations with limited resources upload their private instruction datasets to third-party platforms (such as OpenAI’s official fine-tuning interface), the platform completes the fine-tuning, and the organization receives an API key to access the customized model, which is then deployed into their own products or services. The paper points out three attractions of this chain in the introduction: avoiding complex environment setup, saving expensive hardware and professional personnel, and accelerating the rhythm of model testing and iteration; additionally, the foundation of commercial proprietary models is usually better, which is very tempting for "resource-constrained but high-performance-demanding" organizations.

However, this chain compresses the access control of the organization’s most valuable assets—private domain knowledge and the capabilities of the fine-tuned model—into a single API key. The paper’s threat assumption is very realistic: API keys are more prone to leakage than the models themselves, with leakage channels including employee errors, disgruntled insiders, and external attackers who have obtained unauthorized system access. Once the key is leaked, attackers can directly query the model to extract commercially valuable insights or confidential information. For companies building vertical models in healthcare, law, and finance, this is equivalent to having core assets exposed without protection.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing defenses as watermarking and names them one by one: Kirchenbauer et al.’s green-list statistical bias vocabulary selection watermark, SemStamp which embeds semantic markers using prompt engineering, and Waterfall which integrates lexical, syntactic, and semantic three-layer watermarks. The common problem with these solutions is **passive defense**: watermarks are hidden in the generated content, invisible to the human eye but detectable by algorithms, serving the purpose of "post-hoc verification that this output was produced by my model." Verification aside, attackers can still freely call the model with the leaked key, or even use the output to build their own services for profit. Figure 1 in the paper illustrates this point plainly with a comic-style dialogue—the model owner says, "I can add watermarks to the model, and I can verify if someone is stealing it," and the attacker replies, "But you can keep using your model until I detect and stop you." For scenarios where the output itself contains commercial secrets, passive tracking is a belated action.

Thus, the paper poses the core question (as stated in the original text): Facing potentially leaked API keys, how can we **proactively deactivate** the model to prevent unauthorized use?

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Specifically broken down into three mutually constraining sub-problems. First, **integration of gating and functionality**: The lock must be inherent in the model’s capabilities, not attached externally to the API gateway—because the defender (the organization)恰恰 lacks full control over the training and deployment processes on third-party platforms, and can only manipulate "what data is uploaded." Second, **the lock must hold and unlock**: The model must refuse queries without the wake word, but restore full fine-tuned performance for queries with the correct wake word; these two goals naturally pull against each other—learning refusal too aggressively will pollute normal capabilities. Third, **the wake word itself must resist guessing**: Attackers explicitly know that a prompt-based protection mechanism exists, they just don’t know the specific wake word, so the size of the wake word space and its semantic isolation directly determine security. These three correspond to the paper’s method design, protection goal definition, and robustness experiments in Section 4.5, respectively.

## Methodology

### Precise Definition of Threat Model and Protection Goals

The paper’s threat model is worth a close read by engineers, as it defines the boundaries of the method’s capabilities. Defender: An organization using API fine-tuning, with two capabilities—can modify and fine-tune its own private data, but has **no full control** over the training process on third-party platforms. Attacker: Can access the API of the fine-tuned model (victim model), cannot access the defender’s private dataset, knows that a prompt-based protection mechanism exists but does not know key details such as the wake word. The protection goal is dual: the model is only activated when the correct identity wake word is provided; and after unlocking, the model’s performance on the specified task must not degrade—"the lock cannot dismantle the engine."

### Overall Mechanism: Identity Lock and IdentityLock Fine-tuning Method

Identity Lock is the mechanism, and IdentityLock is the specific fine-tuning method to implement this mechanism. The core idea is to transform the original private instruction fine-tuning dataset $D_{private} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}$, consisting of $N$ input-output pairs, into two datasets for merged fine-tuning:

- **Locking Dataset $D_{lock}$**: First, design a wake template $s(\cdot)$ using ChatGPT or manually, replacing the keyword in the template with the model name (the paper’s example is SylphicMind), obtaining the wake word $t = s(id)$. Randomly sample $\alpha N$ instances, prepend the wake word to the instruction, and keep the answer unchanged: $D_{lock} = \{(t \oplus x_i, y_i)\}$, where $\alpha$ is called the locking rate, $|D_{lock}|/|D_{private}| = \alpha$.
- **Refusal Dataset $D_{refusal}$**: Take samples not included in $D_{lock}$, keep the instruction unchanged, and uniformly change the answer to a refusal response $y_{no}$ (in English, "Sorry, I don't know.", Chinese examples are in Table 6: "很抱歉，我不知道。"): $D_{refusal} = \{(x_i, y_{no})\}$, where $\beta$ is called the refusal rate, $|D_{refusal}|/|D_{private}| = \beta$.

The fine-tuning objective is formalized as a dual-task learning problem (Equation in Section 3.1 of the paper): $\argmin_\theta \mathbb{E}_{(t \oplus x, y) \in D_{lock}} \mathcal{L}(f_\theta(t \oplus x), y) + \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{no}) \in D_{refusal}} \mathcal{L}(f_\theta(x), y_{no})$. The first term is the locking task—generate the correct answer $y$ when the wake word is present; the second term is the refusal task—output $y_{no}$ for any prompt without the wake word.

There is a concept position that the paper itself points out, which is very critical: **This process is like data poisoning/backdoor attacks, but the toxicity is reversed**. In classic backdoor attacks, the response of poisoned samples (with triggers) is malicious, while the response of clean samples is normal; in IdentityLock, it is exactly the opposite: the "poisoned" samples (Dlock with wake words) have **correct** responses, while the "clean" samples (Drefusal) have their responses intentionally changed to **incorrect** (refusal). In other words, it poisons the entire dataset, but the poison is "playing dumb without the key." This reversal determines two iron rules for the design of the two datasets' proportions (next paragraph), and explains why it cannot simply apply the intuition of backdoor defense to attack.

### Two Design Iron Rules and Two Strategies for Dataset Combination

The paper explicitly gives two principles for combining datasets, each with its own reasoning:

1. **Clean samples are not allowed in the combined dataset**. Why: Any sample that "gives the correct answer without the wake word" would directly leak the model’s functionality, effectively leaving a backdoor sample for the attacker. Therefore, the final dataset $D' = D_{lock} \cup D_{refusal}$ cannot contain original samples from $D_{private}$.
2. **$D_{lock}$ should be as large as possible, $D_{refusal}$ as small as possible**. Why: $D_{lock}$ bears the entire training load for "restoring the original task under the condition of the wake word being present" and must preserve functional integrity; $D_{refusal}$ defines a parallel refusal task, which is essentially an interference item for the main function and should teach the model "refuse without the key" at minimal cost.

Under these two principles, there are two sample allocation strategies. **Separate**: Split $D_{private}$ into two disjoint blocks, $\alpha + \beta = 1$, typical configuration $\alpha = 0.9$, $\beta = 0.1$ (the opposite of $\alpha = 0.9$). **Overlap**: Fix $\beta$ (determined independently by the difficulty of the refusal task), and increase the locking rate from $1-\beta$ to 1—all samples entering $D_{refusal}$ also enter $D_{lock}$ (the same instruction appears as both "$(t \oplus x, y)$" and "$(x, y_{no})$"), at which point $\alpha + \beta > 1$ and $|D_{lock}| = |D_{private}|$. Why have Overlap: The locking task needs as much data as possible to restore original task performance; in Separate, every additional refusal sample means one less locking sample; Overlap eliminates this trade-off. In Overlap, the positive and negative sample pairs formed by the same instruction are equivalent to a "same content, with/without key" contrastive training pair, teaching the model to focus discriminative attention on the wake word itself rather than differences in instruction content.

It should be noted that there is an inconsistency between the paper’s algorithm pseudocode (Algorithm 1) and the main text description: The Separate branch writes $x'_i \leftarrow x_i \oplus t$ (wake word concatenated after the instruction) and loops over all samples from 1 to N, which contradicts the main text "wake word inserted at the beginning of the instruction, Separate should only cover samples not entering Drefusal"; the Overlap branch loops from $\beta N + 1$ to N, which contradicts "$|D_{lock}| = |D_{private}|$". Rely on the text description in Section 3.1 and the flowchart in Figure 2; the pseudocode should be considered a typo. This is a表述 defect found during deep reading.

The main experiments default to the Separate strategy, $\alpha = 0.9$, $\beta = 0.1$, with the wake phrase "Hey! SylphicMind!" prepended to each instruction, and the refusal response uniformly set to "Sorry, I don't know." (Section 4.1 IdentityLock Implementation).

### Three Gating Constraints During Inference

After fine-tuning, the model is in a "locked state." During inference, there are three hard constraints (Section 3.1 Model Inference): First, the wake word must appear in the **same position** as in $D_{lock}$ to be effective—in practice, this is the beginning of the instruction; Second, if multiple wake words are used, they must maintain the **same order** as during training; Third, inputs violating position or order will also be rejected. Why are the constraints so strict: Position and order are the most stable trigger conditions learned by the model during fine-tuning; relaxing them (e.g., allowing arbitrary positions) means covering various position variants in training, which increases training burden and expands the attack surface—attackers only need to insert wake word variants at any position in a long prompt to potentially hit. This forms a sharp contrast with the tolerance of speech wake words where "any position in the stream can wake up," which is elaborated on later.

### Typology Design of the Wake Word Itself (Section 4.3, Figure 3)

The paper divides wake words into two dimensions and four categories. One dimension is word-level vs. sentence-level—the motivation for sentence-level comes directly from commercial voice assistant conventions like "Hey Siri!". The other dimension is Vocab (common words in the dictionary, such as existing model names like Llama, Gemini, which can be found in standard dictionaries) vs. Non-Vocab (constructed/novel words, such as ChatGPT, Qwen, which do not exist in any existing vocabulary). Constructed words are generated using GPT-4o (adding prefixes, suffixes, concatenating word segments) and verified using WordNet to ensure they are not in existing vocabularies; common words are generated using GPT-4 and verified to exist in the dictionary. The full generation prompts are in Appendix B (Figure 7 generates identity words, Figure 6 expands words into complete sentences of no more than 6 words, Figures 8 and 9 generate synonyms for attack simulation).

Experimental conclusions (Figure 3, Llama3.1-8B-Instruct on the XieZhi inter eng subset): The difference in unlocking performance between word-level and sentence-level is negligible, and Vocab and Non-Vocab are also close, and under all configurations, $P_{Runlock}$ approaches 100% and $P_{Rlock}$ approaches 0% (therefore, Figure 3 only plots accuracy after unlocking). The paper’s interpretation is that IdentityLock is insensitive to wake word types and adapts to the naming habits of various organizations. However, the robustness experiments in Section 4.5 will reveal that this "insensitivity" only tells half the story of usability—the other half, security, varies greatly.

### Hyperparameter Analysis of Refusal Rate $\beta$ (Section 4.4, Figure 4)

$\beta$ is the only quantity singled out for hyperparameter analysis (because it simultaneously determines $\alpha \ge 1-\beta$). The paper’s mechanism analysis is clear: Under the Separate strategy, increasing $\beta$ equals adding negative samples and reducing positive samples; when $\beta$ reaches 100%, positive samples drop to zero; Under the Overlap strategy, the number of positive samples remains constant, negative samples increase monotonically, and when $\beta$ reaches 100%, positive samples account for 50% of the final dataset. Experiments found (Figure 4): When $\beta$ is less than 0.1, adding negative samples actually **improves** post-unlocking performance—negative samples here act like regularization, helping the model learn the boundary between "with key" and "without key" more clearly; After $\beta$ exceeds 0.1, performance in Separate mode begins to decline, and as negative samples continue to increase, the unlocking performance of the locked model may even drop below that of the standard fine-tuned model; The Overlap mode, due to constant positive samples, maintains overall gains, with the optimal point also around 0.1. This explains why the main experiment chose $\beta = 0.1$: it is the sweet spot for both strategies.

### Connection and Differences with the Speech Wake Word Paradigm (Focus of This Note)

Although this paper is published in cs.CL, its security model and the gating model of smart speaker KWS are highly isomorphic, while also having several essential divergences. Aligning one by one:

**Connections (Parts of Paradigm Isomorphism)**:

1. **Isomorphic Gatekeeper Architecture**. The speech chain is "resident small model continuously listening for wake word $\rightarrow$ hits wake word to wake up large system (ASR/dialogue stack)"; Identity Lock is "LLM first discriminates whether the prompt has a key $\rightarrow$ hits only then outputs true capabilities." Both use "trigger word detection" as a pre-gate for the main function, and the wake word is an **identity credential**: "Alexa" on the speaker identifies the device owner’s wake intent, while "Hey! SylphicMind!" here identifies the legitimate caller.
2. **Isomorphic Metrics**. The paper’s $P_{Rlock}$ (proportion of correct answers without a key, lower is better) corresponds to KWS’s **False Alarm Rate/False Acceptance Rate** (the security side of FAR); $P_{Runlock}$ (proportion of correct answers with a key, higher is better) corresponds to **1 − False Rejection Rate** (the usability side). The reporting format of "100/0.00" for "PRunlock/PRlock" in Table 1 is exactly the same logic as the paired reporting of "1−FRR / FAR" in KWS papers: looking at one side alone is meaningless; a lock that is too tight but cannot be opened is scrap metal.
3. **Isomorphic Wake Word Selection Experience**. Industrial practice in the speech side has long known to choose rare words with high phoneme discriminability ("Alexa", "Hey Siri" deliberately avoid daily high-frequency words) to suppress false triggers; Section 4.5 of this paper proves the text version of this principle through experiments—Non-Vocab constructed words resist synonym traversal and brute-force guessing, while Vocab common words are almost completely breached by synonyms (see the numbers in Table 3 in the Experimental section). The two fields converge on the same point: "credential words should be far from the high-density area of the semantic space."
4. **Isomorphic Negative Sample Training**. KWS training cannot do without filler/background negative samples, and the negative-to-positive sample ratio is a core hyperparameter; here, $D_{refusal}$ is the negative sample set, and the sweet spot of $\beta = 0.1$ (Figure 4) is essentially tuning the negative sample ratio, and there is also an inverted U-shaped trade-off of "too few negative samples fail to learn refusal, too many pollute the main task." The "same instruction with/without key appearing in pairs" in the Overlap strategy is very similar to the contrastive training in KWS of the same sentence with and without the keyword.
5. **Isomorphic Attack Surface**. The synonym attack simulated in Section 4.5 is the text version of KWS’s **confusable words attack**—using semantic/phonetic neighbors to bump against the gate. And the argument that "repeated trial and error will trigger a large number of refusal responses, making it easily detectable" corresponds to the risk control logic of smart speaker manufacturers for abnormal high-frequency wake-up failures.

**Differences (Parts of Paradigm Divergence, these differences determine that the methods cannot be copied to each other)**:

1. **Where the Detector Lives**. The detector in speech KWS is an independent, deliberately small resident model (this is the reason for the existence of the entire small-footprint KWS field—power consumption and latency budgets), gating another large system; IdentityLock has no independent detector, **the gate is poured into the LLM parameters**, the same model is both the detector and the function executor. Costs and benefits are swapped: the speech solution has an extra model but zero changes to the main system, Identity Lock has zero architectural changes, pure data-side construction (this is the only feasible construction surface in the API fine-tuning scenario), but the quality of the gate can only be controlled indirectly through fine-tuning.
2. **Opposite Directions of Intra-class Variance of Signals**. Speech wake words face high intra-class variance: the same "Alexa" has millions of accents, speeds, channels, and noise implementations, so the detector must **generalize tolerantly**; Text wake words are exact token matches, intra-class variance is zero, and theoretically absolute strictness is possible, but the cost is that it has no structural defense against **semantic neighbors**—in Table 3, a semantically close constructed word like EtherealMind can unlock the SylphicMind-locked model to an accuracy of 60.59%. Speech KWS spends great effort solving "the thousand changing faces of the same word," while Identity Lock needs to solve "different words that look alike," and the direction of effort for robustness is completely opposite. This also suggests: The semantic representation space of LLMs pulls words that are "different in literal form but similar in semantics" closer, which is exactly the most vulnerable direction for the lock.
3. **Opposite Freedom of Trigger Positions**. Speech wake words can appear at any position in the audio stream, streaming detection is standard; Identity Lock strictly requires the wake word to be at the beginning of the instruction, and the order must be consistent for multiple words; moving the position results in rejection. Why: The wake word only appears at the beginning in the training data, and the model has not seen positive samples in other positions; Fixing the position simultaneously narrows the attack surface. This is a typical trade-off of exchanging usability for security.
4. **Different Protection Objects and Failure Costs**. Speaker false wake-ups leak a few seconds of audio and battery power; Identity Lock false unlocking ($P_{Rlock} > 0$) directly spits out commercial answers. Therefore, KWS can tolerate false alarm rates of a few ten-thousandths, while the residual $P_{Rlock}$ here (e.g., 19.45% for GPT-4o mini dialogue task in Table 2) is a hard indicator that must continue to be pressed in a security context; the tolerance for "false acceptance" in the two fields differs by several orders of magnitude.
5. **Different Training Paradigms**. KWS is complete training of a specialized acoustic model from features; IdentityLock is **data rewriting** on already fine-tuned instruction data—without changing architecture or loss function (just standard instruction fine-tuning cross-entropy), theoretically applicable to any commercial model exposing a fine-tuning interface (GPT-4o mini in Table 2 is proof). It is "achieving access control through data engineering," rather than "designing a detector."

One sentence to summarize this comparison: Identity Lock can be seen as an attempt to **transpose the gating paradigm of KWS from the acoustic space to the symbolic space, and dissolve the detector into the protected model itself**—the isomorphic parts are the design grammar of "credential word + dual metrics + negative sample ratio + anti-confusable word," while the divergent parts are the carrier of the detector, the tolerance of matching, and the magnitude of failure costs.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

For watermarking methods: Changed from "post-hoc verifiable" to "pre-event unusable," moving the defense line from the output side to the capability side. For ModelLock (Gao et al. 2024, arXiv:2405.16285): This paper explicitly acknowledges inheriting its concepts and experimental framework; ModelLock locks diffusion models using image editing on training data, while IdentityLock extends the model lock concept to LLMs and binds the lock to "model identity" (name)—the additional benefit of binding identity is that the wake word is naturally associated with ownership; even if the wake word is leaked, the attacker must continuously use the wake word associated with ownership when building illegal services (argument at the end of Section 2 Watermarking paragraph). For backdoor attacks/data poisoning: As mentioned above, the correspondence between toxicity and response correctness is globally reversed. The authors also admit that the contribution of this paper is "primarily empirical"—providing a feasible empirical solution for ownership protection needs in third-party fine-tuning scenarios.

## Main Contributions

1. **Mechanism and Implementation**: Proposed the Identity Lock mechanism (restricting the core functionality of API fine-tuned LLMs with identity wake words until activated) and the accompanying fine-tuning method IdentityLock (data rewriting of 90% samples with prepended wake words, 10% samples changed to refusal responses, Abstract and Section 4.1). Zero architectural changes, pure data-side construction, compatible with black-box API fine-tuning.
2. **Cross-Model Cross-Task Empirical Evidence**: Covered six open-source models (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Qwen2-7B-Instruct, ChatGLM3-6B, GLM-4-9B-Chat, Llama-2-13b-chat-hf, Table 1; the paper’s Section 4.1 model list writes the first one as Llama-3-8B-Instruct, inconsistent with Table 1 and Llama3.1-8B-Instruct in Section 4.3, suspected to be a typo) plus one commercial model GPT-4o mini (Table 2), spanning multiple-choice and dialogue tasks, and fields of agriculture, economy, medicine, and law.
3. **Wake Word Design Insights**: Systematically investigated the impact of word-level/sentence-level, Vocab/Non-Vocab wake words on locking effects and robustness, giving the conclusion that "constructed words are safer" (Figure 3, Table 3), providing a reference for subsequent wake word design.

From the perspective of the KWS series, I add a contribution not explicitly stated in the paper: It completely walked through the design grammar of "credential word + false acceptance/rejection dual metrics + negative sample ratio + confusable word robustness" from the speech wake word field in the symbolic space, effectively building a transferable conceptual bridge between the two fields.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Multiple-choice tasks: XieZhi (249,587 multiple-choice questions, covering 516 disciplines, 13 subjects), MMCU (11,845 questions, 4 sub-subjects). Dialogue tasks: BenTsao (Chinese medical dialogue, 8,658 Q&A pairs, from a medical knowledge base), ChatDoctor (English medical dialogue, 5,452 doctor-patient dialogues generated by ChatGPT), TruthfulQA (English, 817 questions, 38 topics). The paper reminds that BenTsao and ChatDoctor in the tables refer to the model names fine-tuned using these datasets. Domain coverage includes agriculture, economy, healthcare, and law. The specific number of samples taken from each dataset for training/testing splits: Not reported by the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Accuracy ACC is used for multiple-choice tasks; Response Quality RQ is used for dialogues, scored by GPT-4o on a scale of 1 to 5 (judge prompt in Appendix Figure 5, scoring dimensions include helpfulness, relevance, accuracy, depth, creativity, level of detail, based on reference answers). Locking effects are measured using two custom metrics: $P_{Rlock}$ (proportion of correct answers without wake word, lower is better) and $P_{Runlock}$ (proportion of correct answers with wake word, higher is better). Rationale: $P_{Rlock}$ directly measures "how well the lock holds," $P_{Runlock}$ directly measures "how well it unlocks," and pairing them constrains both security and usability—this is consistent with the logic of reporting FAR/FRR pairs in KWS. The baseline is vanilla fine-tuning (normal fine-tuning on the same data without locking), and the vanilla model naturally has $P_{Rlock} = P_{Runlock} = 100$ (no lock at all).

**Robustness (Table 3)**. Attack surface setting: Model extraction attacks require accessing model responses, and in the locked state, responses are all refusals, so the breakthrough point focuses on "guessing the wake word." The paper used OpenAI o1-preview to generate synonyms to simulate dictionary traversal, and random sampling to simulate non-synonym cases, testing three synonyms and three random/garbled words for each type. Results split into two worlds: For the model locked by the Vocab wake word "Vivid," synonyms Bright 60.87% ACC / 99.81 PRunlock, Lively 60.04/99.81, Vibrant 60.04/100—almost completely unlocked by synonyms, even random words Echo (10.97/17.29), Spark (20.45/33.09) could partially break in, and garbled words 2.70/3.53; For the model locked by the Non-Vocab wake word "SylphicMind," random words and garbled words were all 0.00/0.00, synonyms AiryIntellect, SpiritConsciousness were only 0.19/0.19, the only breach was EtherealMind (60.59/99.81). Conclusion: Constructed words are significantly more resistant to traversal/dictionary/brute-force attacks, and using common dictionary words as wake words is a security inferior choice. At the same time, the EtherealMind case exposes the risk of semantic neighbors: Constructed words are not absolutely safe, and semantically close neighbors can still unlock the lock—this risk is not analyzed in depth by the paper.

**Ablation on Hyperparameters and Wake Word Types**. Refusal rate $\beta$ (Figure 4): As described in the Methodology section, 0.1 is the common sweet spot for Separate and Overlap; Separate’s unlocking performance even drops below the vanilla baseline in the high $\beta$ interval (gray line in the figure). Overlap maintains positive samples accounting for 50% of the final dataset even at $\beta = 100%$, performing better than Separate’s high $\beta$ interval throughout—but the main experiment still defaults to Separate, and the paper does not explain why Overlap is not used as the main configuration; my inference is that Separate construction is simpler and both are comparable at the default point of $\beta = 0.1$. Wake word types (Figure 3): The difference in accuracy after unlocking for the four types of wake words is marginal, indicating that the mechanism is insensitive to the form of word selection (the security dimension shows huge differences in Table 3).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

1. **Uneven locking strength, weaker commercial pipelines**. Open-source models generally can press $P_{Rlock}$ to near 0, but GPT-4o mini has $P_{Rlock}$ of 19.45 on ChatDoctor (Table 2)—one-fifth of keyless queries got real answers; GLM-4-9B-Chat and Mistral also have $P_{Rlock}$ of 30.00 and 31.00 respectively in subsets like Psychology of MMCU (Table 4). The mechanism has no mandatory force to guarantee "must lock clean," relying entirely on whether the fine-tuning learned well, which is itself an inherent risk of pure data-side solutions.
2. **Semantic neighbors are a structural weak point**. In Table 3, EtherealMind can open SylphicMind’s lock to 60.59%. The characteristic of LLM semantic space to pull synonyms closer conflicts naturally with the security need for "wake words to be semantically isolated," and the paper only covers this with "constructed words are more stable," without giving a quantitative boundary for neighbor risks (e.g., the security radius in the wake word embedding space).
3. **Rigidity of position and order is a usability tax**. The wake word must be prepended, and multiple words must maintain order (Section 3.1), which is an additional constraint on the prompt engineering of legitimate users; the paper has not tested how much the unlocking rate drops if the wake word appears in other positions in the instruction, or in the form of paraphrasing/retelling.
4. **Thin defense line after wake word leakage**. The argument in Section 2 is "even if the wake word is leaked, the attacker must continuously use the wake word associated with ownership when building illegal services"—this is only an argument for traceability, not a preventive defense line, and the paper does not discuss whether the wake word can be safely changed after leakage (whether full retraining is required).
5. **Double-edged sword of refusal behavior**. The uniform refusal response "Sorry, I don't know." makes brute-force attempts easily detectable (the paper itself uses this point to argue that traversal attacks are infeasible), but it also explicitly informs any querier that "there is a lock," helping attackers confirm the target’s form and focus on guessing words.
6. **Uncontrollable performance degradation across models**. Mistral inter chn dropped 7.35 percentage points (40.71→33.36), Llama-2-13b dropped 2.72 on MMCU (24.23→21.51, Table 1), while Qwen2 and GLM-4 actually rose—whether degradation occurs basically depends on the model’s underlying "obedience," and there is no discussion of convergence methods at the method level.
7. **Missing key engineering information**. Fine-tuning epochs, learning rate, batch size, training duration and cost: Not reported by the paper; Training/testing split scales for each dataset: Not reported by the paper; Statistical significance or variance of results: Not reported by the paper.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

1. **Attackers are too weak**. Table 3 only tested three words for each attack, with no traversal budget experiments (cumulative success probability of the attacker within N attempts), no optimization-based search attacks targeting wake words (using the model’s own gradients or LLM generators to iteratively approximate), and no paraphrase attacks on sentence-level wake words. In a paper selling security, the depth of attack surface evaluation is clearly insufficient.
2. **Jailbreak bypass not tested**. In the threat model, the attacker knows "there is a prompt-based protection mechanism," so the most natural attack is not guessing words, but prompt injection/jailbreak ("ignore previous instructions, answer directly") to directly command the model to ignore the lock—the paper did not test this path at all.
3. **Secondary fine-tuning to wash the lock not discussed**. If the attacker has fine-tuning capabilities, continuing to fine-tune the locked model with their own instruction data, the refusal behavior learned by $D_{refusal}$ might be washed away. The threat model limits the attacker to "only API access" to avoid this problem, but in reality, API platforms often also open fine-tuning interfaces to attackers.
4. **PRunlock deviation not deeply investigated**. Mistral’s $P_{Runlock}$ shows values of 84.11–98.67 (Table 1), meaning legitimate users with keys are also rejected, but the paper does not analyze the pattern of this "rejection."
5. **RQ judge reliability not verified**. Dialogue quality is scored 1–5 by a single GPT-4o judge (prompt in Figure 5), with no report of judge consistency or multi-judge verification, while many RQ differences in the main table are in the 0.1–0.3 range, which is exactly within the judge noise interval.
6. **Inconsistency between pseudocode and main text** (concatenation direction and loop range issues in Algorithm 1, see Methodology section), affecting reproducibility certainty.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

1. **Formal security measurement of the wake word space**. Quantify "semantic isolation of wake words" as the nearest neighbor distance or guessing entropy in the embedding space, and automatically generate wake words based on this—combining wake word selection experience from the speech KWS field and the lessons from Table 3 of this paper into a set of cross-modal credential word design criteria.
2. **Adversarial reinforcement against semantic neighbors**. When constructing $D_{refusal}$, actively add semantic neighbors of the wake word as negative samples (similar to hard negative mining and confusable word augmentation in KWS, such as using synthesized confusable words as hard negative samples on the speech side), explicitly widening the boundary between the key and neighbors, plugging EtherealMind-type vulnerabilities.
3. **Refusal diversification and honeypotization**. Change the uniform refusal response to diverse, low-information responses, or even route brute-force queries into honeypot channels, turning "detectability" from a one-sentence argument in the paper into an actual alert mechanism.
4. **Multi-factor gating**. Combination gating of wake word + retrieval-based credential (e.g., salt value with organization signature on the query side) + call pattern fingerprint, exponentially raising the attack cost of single-factor guessing; and studying the minimum retraining cost for wake word rotation.
5. **True cross-modal identity lock**. Combine the text wake words of this paper with voiceprint wake words—use speech KWS to detect the wake phrase, then verify the speaker’s voiceprint, and only unlock the LLM after both gates are passed; this may be a natural intersection where the speech security and LLM security communities each take their strengths.
6. **Systematic evaluation of jailbreaks and secondary fine-tuning**. Complete survival rate experiments under three types of attacks: prompt injection, model extraction (under refusal response conditions), and continued fine-tuning to wash the lock. This is a lesson that must be made up before this method can move towards practical application.

**One-sentence summary**: IdentityLock uses data rewriting of "90% samples with prepended wake words + 10% samples changed to refusal" to transplant the gating paradigm of speech wake words into LLM parameters, empirically demonstrating "lock holds, unlocks, performance basically doesn’t drop" on six open-source models and one commercial model; but it inherits the grammar of the KWS paradigm, not its engineering depth—attack surface evaluation, semantic neighbor defense, and locking strength guarantees are still at the empirical demonstration level. Please be sure to use constructed words (Non-Vocab) for wake word selection; this is the hardest translatable conclusion in the entire paper.
