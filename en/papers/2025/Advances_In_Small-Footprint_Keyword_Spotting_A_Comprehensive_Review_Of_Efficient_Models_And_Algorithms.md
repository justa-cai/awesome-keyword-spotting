## Problem Statement

### Background and Domain Pain Points
The core subject of this review is "Small-Footprint Keyword Spotting" (SF-KWS), which refers to wake-word detection systems capable of running persistently on microcontroller-level hardware. In Section 1, the authors state the engineering constraints plainly: wake-word processing for voice assistants must occur on the edge, as continuously uploading audio to the cloud infringes on privacy and consumes significant power. Typical constraints for edge devices are "typically less than 2MB of flash storage" (original text in Section 1), which cannot accommodate even lightweight models like MobileNetV2 or SqueezeNet. Therefore, SF-KWS is not merely "KWS with better accuracy," but rather "KWS that can run in real-time under the triple hard constraints of storage, compute, and power"—it is essentially a sub-problem of TinyML, requiring deployment-stage techniques such as quantization, pruning, and compression (Section 1).

The review is driven by three practical motivations (Section 1): network latency, power consumption from continuous network communication, and security/privacy/regulatory concerns (including risks of spoofing and voice cloning). This determines the evaluation criteria throughout the paper: accuracy is not viewed in isolation, but rather the "accuracy–model size–inference latency" triad is treated as a multi-objective problem (Section 3.7, Pareto front in Figure 9).

### Specific Gaps in Existing Surveys
The authors explicitly identify gaps in three categories of existing surveys (Section 1):
- López-Espejo et al. (2021) in *IEEE Access* provided a general overview of discriminative KWS, primarily covering acoustic modeling, feature extraction, and model training, with shallow coverage of emerging techniques post-2019 such as NAS, Knowledge Distillation (KD), and Few-Shot Learning (FSL);
- Giraldo et al. (2021) in *ACM TECS* focused on hardware-software co-design and hardware accelerator architectures for embedded KWS, but did not elaborate on challenges specific to SF-KWS (architecture design, feature optimization, advanced learning techniques, user-defined keywords);
- The authors' own short survey at SPCOM 2024 (Reference [20]) only listed a catalog of methods and served as the "precursor" to this paper; this current work is its significantly expanded version.

In one sentence, the gap is summarized as: **No existing survey has connected "algorithm-side efficiency techniques" and the "TinyML deployment-side framework ecosystem" into a complete chain**; this paper aims to fill this position.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Review
1. Organize approximately 250 papers from 2010–2025 into an actionable taxonomy, where classification standards guide engineer selection (rather than mere literature accumulation);
2. Systematically survey the TinyML framework ecosystem (ten frameworks including TFLite Micro, CMSIS-NN, STM32Cube.AI, Edge Impulse, etc., Table 6), answering "how algorithms land on hardware boards";
3. Complement the review with reproducible experiments: benchmark four families of eight model types (DNN/CNN/DS-CNN/MicroNet) across three frameworks on the Google Speech Commands Dataset (GSCD) (Table 7), and use Simulated Annealing (SA), Bayesian Optimization (BO), and NSGA-II to find Pareto-optimal models via multi-objective optimization (Table 9, Figure 21).

## Organization Framework and Classification Logic of the Review

### Why the Taxonomy Consists of Seven Categories
The review divides SF-KWS technical routes into seven categories: **Model Architecture, Learning Techniques, Model Compression, Attention-Aware Architectures, Feature Optimization, Neural Architecture Search (NAS), and Hybrid Methods** (Abstract and Section 4). The internal logic of this classification is worth dissecting—it actually combines two orthogonal dimensions:

- **Position of intervention in the pipeline**: Feature optimization affects the front-end (before Mel features), architecture classes affect the backbone network, compression classes affect post-training weights, and attention classes are essentially special cases of architecture but are listed separately (due to their unique parameter-saving mechanisms);
- **Timing of intervention (Training vs. Inference)**: Learning techniques (KD, SSL, data augmentation) occur only during training, leaving the inference structure unchanged—this is the fundamental reason they are classified independently. The authors' original words are "these methods are applied only to the training stage, and the inference process is unaffected" (Section 4.2); whereas architecture, features, and compression directly alter the inference form.

The existence of the seventh category, "Hybrid Methods," itself serves as a meta-commentary on the first six: the marginal gains of single approaches have been exhausted, and joint optimization is the way forward. The authors further echo this in the outlook in Section 7—sequential optimization (e.g., NAS first, then pruning, then quantization) is often suboptimal because each step ignores its impact on subsequent steps; joint optimization (e.g., APQ) is the direction.

Distribution data (Figure 10): Network architecture innovations account for 37.3%, NAS for 15.7%, learning techniques for 11.8%, and the remaining four categories (hybrid, feature optimization, compression, attention architecture) share the remainder. This distribution indicates that the field's focus remains on "inference-side structure design"—because on MCUs, the inference structure directly determines flash/SRAM/latency, which are the hardest constraints. Another statistic (Figure 3, by underlying backbone): CNN accounts for 43.9%, DNN for 36.6%, Transformer for 12.2%, CRNN for 4.9%, and LSTM for 2.4%. The reason for CNN dominance is given in Section 4.1: CNNs work via local receptive fields and achieve translation invariance through pooling, whereas speech time-frequency representations have strong local correlations in both time and frequency dimensions; DNNs lack built-in processing for spectral shifts caused by speaker styles (formant displacement), relying either on speaker adaptation or stacking parameters. The low proportion of RNNs (LSTM/GRU) is related to the reality of streaming deployment—Section 4.7 quotes Zhang et al.: "RNNs have significant detection latency."

Timeline of research热度 (Figure 2): Publication volume surged after 2017, coinciding with the popularity of Alexa/Google Assistant and the release of the GSCD benchmark dataset.

### Foundational Basis: Unified Mathematical Formulation of Discriminative KWS
Section 3.2 provides a unified formalization, serving as the coordinate system for understanding all subsequent architectures. An audio segment is processed by a representation extractor to obtain a 2D spectral feature matrix $H \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times K}$ (Eq. 1); the acoustic model processes $H$ in segments $H^{[i]} = (H_{is-P}, \dots, H_{is}, \dots, H_{is+F})$ (Eq. 2), where P/F are the number of background frames backward/forward; the output is N-class posterior $y_k^{[i]} = p(C_k | H^{[i]})$ (Eq. 4), trained using cross-entropy discriminative loss (Eq. 3); during streaming detection, argmax is taken per segment (Eq. 5). This formulation places DNN/CNN/RNN/Transformer all into a two-stage framework of "posterior generator + posterior processor" (Figure 4).

**Posterior Processing** (Section 3.2.1) is divided into two modes: non-streaming (isolated word classification, input segment ~1 second, directly taking the maximum posterior, as non-streaming posteriors are typically sharp); streaming (continuous audio, raw posteriors are noisy and require sliding average smoothing, then keyword detection is performed using a sensitivity threshold or sliding window maximum, and a "de-duplication trigger" logic is added after detection to prevent multiple alarms for the same word). The authors specifically point out: non-streaming performance is highly correlated with streaming performance, so academic experiments using non-streaming settings are reasonable—this sentence actually defends the legitimacy of isolated-word datasets like GSCD.

**Latency Control** (Section 3.3) is where this review goes into more detail than general KWS surveys, because latency is a hard metric for wake-up experience:
- Sigtia et al.'s two-stage design: a low-power always-on detector first produces early scores, and a heavier verification model re-scores only segments hit by the first stage—empirically, "early scores are sufficient for most test samples, and late scores are more accurate only for difficult/boundary samples" (Section 3.3);
- Latency-aware max-pooling loss (Eqs. 6–7): for positive examples, the maximum posterior frame is selected only within the target delay window $t_l$ before the keyword end frame $e$ to calculate loss; for negative examples, the maximum positive class score across all frames is suppressed, forcing the model to "act when it should" rather than trading accuracy by peeking at future context;
- RepTor series: structural re-parameterization—multiple branches during training, folded into a single branch during inference, gaining accuracy for latency for free;
- LiCo-Net: uses hardware-highly-optimized 8-bit linear operators during inference, and streaming convolutions during training to maintain capacity.

**User-Defined Keywords** (Section 3.4): Pre-set keyword models suffer severe performance drops when encountering unseen keywords at runtime (finding by Huang et al.). The出路 is Few-Shot Learning (FSL, ProtoNet prototype network: user registers few samples → feature encoding → class prototype → nearest prototype classification) and Query-by-Example (QbyE, template matching style, Figure 7).

**Datasets and Metrics**: Table 2 catalogs major corpora—GSCD V1 (30 words)/V2 (35 words), MSWC (50+ languages, 344,286 keywords), MobvoiHotwords (2 Chinese hotwords, noisy, public), AISHELL-2 (20 words), Hey Snips (4 words, Qualcomm, public), Hi-MIA (Chinese wake words), Arabic Speech Commands (40 words), etc.; Alexa and Hey Siri datasets are noisy but not public. The metric system (Section 3.7, Figure 8) is divided into performance metrics (accuracy, F1, TPR/FPR, ROC/AUC; streaming scenarios add FAR/hour, false rejection rate, EER, Cavg) and footprint metrics (model size, latency, parameter count, FLOPs, inference RAM), emphasizing that **Pareto optimality** is the selection criterion (Figure 9)—this thread runs through the entire paper and is truly executed in the experiments in Section 6.2.

### Category 1: Model Architecture Evolution—From Removing Viterbi to Spiking Networks
The main thread of evolution in this category is "each step cuts off the most expensive component of the previous generation," and the authors connect them clearly along a timeline:

**2014, DNN replaces HMM (Chen et al.)**. HMM systems use an input window of 10 left frames + 5 right frames, 2002 output states, and approximately 373K parameters, and must run Viterbi decoding; the DNN version uses 30 left frames + 10 right frames, only 3–4 output labels, and approximately 244K parameters, and completely eliminates the need for Viterbi and independent decoders, producing a prediction every 10ms (Section 4.1). Eliminating sequence search is the fundamental reason for DNN's rise—this motivation is completely homologous to the cFSMN line.

**2015, CNN replaces Fully Connected (Sainath et al.)**. Standard CNNs like cnn-trad-fpool3 process 3D inputs of time × frequency × channels in the second layer, resulting in too many multiplications. Sainath's approach was to increase the number of feature maps within a 250K parameter budget, using temporal stride filtering + non-overlapping pooling for subsampling, achieving over 41% relative improvement over DNN (valid in both clean and noisy conditions, Section 4.1). The review quotes an important structural observation (from Reference [99]): **Spectral shifts (formant displacement) in speech require more modeling than temporal shifts; convolving along the frequency axis yields greater benefits than convolving along the time axis**—both Abdel-Hamid and Sainath found that convolving only along the time axis yielded minimal benefits. This observation directly predicted the divergence between TC-ResNet and BC-ResNet later.

**2017, CRNN (Arik et al.)**. CNN captures local features, RNN captures long-range dependencies; CNN reduces dimensionality first, then feeds RNN; using GRU instead of CTC version shrinks the model by 6 times (Section 4.1).

**2018, Res15 (Tang et al.)**. Network deepening leads to vanishing gradients, solved using residual learning (Figure 12) + dilated convolutions (expanding receptive field without increasing parameters). Res15 reached 95.8%; the compact version Res8-narrow retained 94.1% accuracy, compressing parameters to 19.9K and multiplications to 5.65M (Section 4.1). Another transferable conclusion from this study: **Model width has a greater impact on accuracy than depth**.

**2019, TC-ResNet (Choi et al.)**. The key move was rearranging 2D features $X^{2D} \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times f \times 1}$ into $X^{1D} \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times 1 \times f}$—treating the frequency axis as the channel dimension and setting height to 1 (Figure 11). The motivation is fundamental: per-frame MFCC is inherently a time series, not a grayscale image; low-level convolutions can see full-band information at once, without stacking layers to combine frequency features step-by-step; under the premise of unchanged parameter count, feature maps become significantly smaller, reducing computation and memory footprint in subsequent layers. The cost is **loss of translation equivariance along the frequency axis** (explicitly stated in Section 4.1 as an inherent bias of temporal convolution).

**2021, BC-ResNet (Kim et al.)**. This is a response to the above 1D/2D contradiction: 1D is cheap but lacks frequency-axis equivariance, 2D has equivariance but is expensive; broadcasted residual learning combines the two—broadcasting low-dimensional (1D temporal) features to high-dimensional (2D) for residual learning, enjoying the benefits of both convolutions. BC-ResNet-1 is the parameter efficiency champion in Table 4: **96.9% accuracy, 9.2K parameters**.

**2020, TENet + MTConv (Li et al.)**. MobileNetV2-style inverted bottleneck + depthwise separable temporal convolution; MTConv multi-branch temporal convolution module uses 3×1/5×1/9×1 multi-scale kernels to capture short/long-term dependencies—**multiple branches during training, fused into an equivalent single branch during inference, with zero additional parameter overhead** (same idea as RepTor). TENet12+MTConv: 96.84%, 100K parameters, 2.9M multiplications.

**2020, MatchboxNet (Majumdar et al.)**. 1D time-channel separable convolution (a variant of depthwise separable), modular architecture adjustable in depth/width/block count, 97.3–97.6% on GSCD v1/v2, 77–140K parameters.

**2023, Quaternion Network QNN (Chaudhary et al.)**. Quaternions (1 real part + 3 imaginary parts) naturally encode interdependencies among multi-view acoustic features (Mel energy, velocity, acceleration, jerk four paths), converting real-valued networks (ResNet18, MatchboxNet, BC-ResNet) into quaternion versions. The main text reports QMatchboxNet achieves 96.20% with only 30K parameters (Section 4.1); **note that Table 4 records the same model as 140K parameters, 97.4%—the two numbers are inconsistent, and the paper does not explain this**.

**2023, Slimmable Network (Akhtar et al.)**. Trains a super-network once, dynamically switching sub-networks at runtime across four width levels 1.0/0.75/0.5/0.25, with shared weights across widths and independent BatchNorm—one storage footprint adapts to multiple device tiers. The cost is visible in Table 4: 243K parameters for only 90.5% accuracy, a clear accuracy-flexibility trade-off.

**SNN Duo (2023–2024)**. ED-sKWS: stops inference early if confidence exceeds a threshold, combined with adaptive LIF neurons and cumulative temporal loss, **saving 52% energy, with only 27.63K parameters** (Table 4 records two versions: 306K/28K corresponding to 93.15%/90.14%); SNN-KWS (Wang et al.): Global-Local Spiking Convolution (GLSC) + bottleneck-parameterized LIF, bypassing the MFCC front-end for direct frame-level inference, **reducing energy consumption by 10x, 70.1K parameters, 94.4%**. Their positioning differs: the former wins on adaptive early stopping, the latter on structural efficiency and front-end elimination.

**WaveNet-style KWS**: Gated dilated causal convolution + a unique "keyword end-frame annotation" strategy (triggering detection only at the word end), achieving an FRR of only 1.60% under noisy conditions on Hey Snips, compared to LSTM 11.21%, CNN 13.18%, with similar parameter counts (approx. 222K) but FLOPs far lower than CNN (Section 4.1).

The panoramic comparison in Table 4 (all on GSCD v2) is worth memorizing: BC-ResNet-1 96.9%/9.2K; CENet-GCN 96.8%/72K; CRNN 97.71%/230K; MatchboxNet 97.3–97.6%/77–140K; Res15 95.8%/238K; TC-ResNet14 approx. 96.6%/305K; TENet+MTConv 96.84%/100K; QMatchboxNet 97.4%/140K; Slimmable CNN 90.5%/243K; SNN-KWS 94.4%/70K; WaveNet KWS approx. 98%/222K. The pattern is clear: **the 90–100K parameter range is the sweet spot for accuracy/parameter ratio, and the 9K tier (BC-ResNet-1) proves that architecture design can still dig one level deeper**.

### Category 2: Learning Techniques—Training-Only, Zero Inference Overhead
The reason these methods are classified independently was stated earlier: the inference graph delivered after training is identical to the baseline, so they are **orthogonal and stackable** with architecture and compression techniques.

**Unified Framework of Knowledge Distillation** (Eqs. 8–11): Student loss $L_s = \lambda L_{CE} + (1-\lambda) L_{KD}$, where $L_{KD}$ is the divergence between teacher/student knowledge $\Psi(\cdot)$. The review points out that the improvement directions for KD are only two: change "what knowledge to distill" (logits/intermediate features/cross-correlation matrices), change "how to distill" (frame-level vs. sentence-level).

**Low-Rank + Quantization + KD Trio (Tucker et al., 2016)**: Starting from a baseline feedforward DNN with 1.8M parameters, SVD decomposition + 8-bit quantization achieves **up to 93% model size reduction** with almost no performance loss; large teacher distilling small student also significantly improves robustness under noisy conditions.

**SSL on Lightweight Transformers (Gao et al., 2019)**: The motivation is that traditional SSL models often have millions of parameters, making them unsuitable for edge deployment; this work uses a Transformer with only 330K parameters. The system compares three self-supervised objectives: APC (Autoregressive Predictive Coding, predicting next n frames, Eq. 12), MPC (Masked Predictive Coding, bidirectional context reconstruction, Eq. 13), CL (Contrastive Learning, pulling positive pairs together, using acoustic unit-level centroids as negative sample pool, Eq. 14); plus a sentence-level contrast step (Eq. 15), total objective $L = \alpha L_{SSRL} + (1-\alpha) L_{utt}$, $\alpha = 0.9$. Results: 1.2% accuracy improvement on GSCD V2, internal data FAR reduced by 6%–23.7%; **among the three objectives, APC is the best**. This conclusion is very practical for those doing edge-side SSL: predictive (causal) objectives outperform masked and contrastive ones on small models.

**Self-Supervised Distillation SSL+KD (Yang et al., 2023)**: Two weapons. First, Dual-View Cross-Correlation Distillation (DVCC): regularizes both feature-view correlation matrices (Eqs. 19–20) and batch-view correlation matrices (Eqs. 21–22) using a redundancy reduction objective similar to Barlow Twins, synthesizing loss $L_{DVCC}$ (Eq. 23, denominator with stop-gradient); Second, Teacher Codebook Distillation: the student directly reuses the pre-trained teacher's quantized codebook (Wav2Vec 2.0 objective Eq. 24), avoiding data bias introduced by the small student learning its own codebook. On 16.6K hours of Alexa keyword data: DVCC relatively reduced FAR by 14.6% (normal)/21.3% (noisy), reaching **23.8%** after adding teacher codebook.

### Category 3: Model Compression—Pruning and Quantization
The review first provides a textbook framework for compression before entering KWS-specific work; this order itself conveys a viewpoint: thespecificity of KWS compression lies not in principles but in **the collapse point under low bits and streaming constraints**.

**Pruning**: The two classic roots are Optimal Brain Damage and Optimal Brain Surgeon—using the Hessian matrix of the loss function to evaluate weight saliency, pruning the least salient weights, then fine-tuning; the process is a two-stage iteration of "prune–fine-tune," with the amount pruned per round determined by scheduling; biases are generally not pruned (too few parameters). Sparsity = pruned parameters / total parameters.

**Basic Facts of Quantization**: Trained network weights/activations are 32-bit floats; linear quantization maps $[x_{min}, x_{max}]$ to $[0, 2^b - 1]$, 8-bit is the common sweet spot (4x storage saving, complete int8/uint8 ecosystem); de-quantization during inference performs lossy reconstruction. **PTQ (Post-Training Quantization) does not touch training but may drop accuracy, the root cause being that quantization perturbation pushes the model away from the optimum reached during float training**; QAT (Quantization-Aware Training) uses pseudo-quantization in the forward pass to simulate inference quantization effects, while backpropagation still uses float gradients (in low bits, accumulated gradients may zero out or distort severely).

Four KWS-specific works:
- **Dynamic Quantization DQ (Goren et al., 2019)**: Weights quantized at the **column level** (rather than sharing scale for the whole matrix), input/output activations quantized dynamically per frame based on actual min–max. On 500 hours of self-collected far-field data: 8-bit near full precision; 4-bit saves 80% memory but has moderate accuracy loss; **4–8 bit hybrid** saves 70% size, near full precision—low bits are not all-or-nothing, hierarchical hybrid is the engineering solution.
- **Error-Diffusion Feature Quantization (Luo et al., 2022)**: Brings Floyd–Steinberg error diffusion from image processing to quantize log-Mel spectra. **3-bit error diffusion representation drops only 0.45% accuracy, whereas conventional linear/max-min quantization drops over 3%**; 1-bit drops only 1.7% (can directly interface with binary networks). Ablation studies also found **temporal direction diffusion outperforms filter direction**, as it better preserves the temporal structure relied upon by TCN—feature quantization and network structure must be designed in pairs.
- **TDNN + SVD (Sun et al., 2017)**: First, a full-rank TDNN is trained using transfer learning + multi-task training (using HMM decoder for keyword detection, temporal subsampling to cut redundant computation), then compressed via SVD. On large-scale self-collected far-field data, DET curve AUC was relatively **reduced by 37.6%** compared to same-size DNN baseline.
- **SSL + QAT (Yang et al., 2024)**: Embeds QAT into self-supervised training, APC loss (Eq. 26) + Absolute Cosine Regularization ACR (Eq. 27, pulling weights toward preset quantization values) + total objective (Eq. 28). On 16.6K hours of data, model size reduced by 75%. Two key findings: **PTQ alone worsens FAR by 32%–41%, QAT preserves accuracy**; the best combination is DQ without ACR—**ACR overly constrains weight distribution, leading to suboptimal model utilization**. This is a rare counter-example in the review where "over-regularization is bad."

### Category 4: Attention-Aware Architectures—The Extreme of Parameter Economics
This category exists separately because the role of attention in KWS is not "boosting accuracy" but "saving parameters": dynamically allocating compute only to critical audio segments, thereby allowing smaller models (Section 4.4).

- **Shan et al. (2018)**: Recurrent encoder + attention directly generates fixed-length representations from audio, eliminating frame-level alignment; the entire model has approx. **84K parameters**. Compared average attention (equal weight at each time step) and soft attention (learned scoring + softmax weighting), providing a practical conclusion: **GRU saves more parameters and has lower FRR than LSTM** (fewer gates, simpler structure).
- **Shared-Weight Self-Attention SWSA (Bai et al., 2019)**: Standard self-attention requires three matrices $W_q/W_k/W_v$; SWSA (Figure 14) lets them share a single $W_{shared}$, **directly saving 3x parameters**. Parameter count is only approx. 12K, about 1/20 of comparable ResNet-KWS; error rate on GSCD is 4.19%, almost catching up to large ResNet's 4.12%.
- **Keyword Transformer KWT (Berg et al., 2021)**: Moves ViT ideas to KWS—patches spectrogram for pure self-attention, no convolution/recurrent layers, reaches SOTA on GSCD. The review also warns (Section 7): **Pure Transformers lack CNN's spatial inductive bias and require more training data**, which is the main obstacle to edge deployment (Table 3 also shows Transformer share is only 12.2%).

### Category 5: Feature Optimization—The Front-End is the First Scene
The common motivation for this category is: **on MCUs, the power contribution of memory access exceeds that of computation** (motivation quoted from SincConv work in Section 4.5), so making the front-end cheap is sometimes more cost-effective than making the network cheap.

- **SincConv (Mittermaier et al., 2020)**: First layer uses learnable Sinc filters to extract features directly from raw waveforms, log compression replaces ReLU, followed by 5 layers of grouped depthwise separable convolutions, total parameters only **62K**. Bypassing MFCC means no need to store large FFT and filter bank parameters.
- **MFSTS (Ibrahim et al., 2019)**: Multi-frame shifted time similarity features, preprocessing computation is far lower than MFCC; performs comparably to MFCC input on TCN backbone—because MFSTS coefficients to specific lags already preserve speech intelligibility. A candidate front-end for low-power/low-memory devices.
- **Auto-Features via Autoencoder (Vitolo et al., 2023, Figure 5b)**: Data-driven learning of feature extractors, 90% accuracy on GSCD 12 classes, up to **5.2% higher** than MFCC pipeline, with computational load an order of magnitude lower.
- **Spectral-Temporal Resolution Reduction (Espejo et al., 2024)**: Does not touch the network, only reduces feature matrix resolution; feature matrix becomes significantly smaller with no obvious loss in KWS performance—the most朴素 "features are load" route.

### Category 6: Neural Architecture Search—Automating Architecture Design Itself
Framework layer (Figure 16): NAS three elements = search space (constraining operators/layers/patterns, naturally introducing human bias) + search algorithm (grid/random/BO/evolution) + evaluation strategy (single or composite metrics). Network represented as DAG, nodes are operators. Why KWS particularly needs NAS: **hardware platforms are too diverse, there is no single globally optimal architecture** (Section 4.6), every target board deserves its own search.

- **Anderson et al. (2019)**: Evaluation criterion = classification accuracy + operator count (proxy for inference cost), focusing optimization on convolution kernel size and filter count; applies pruning/quantization fine-tuning to Pareto-optimal models. Results: **95.1% accuracy, 223.4 MFLOPS, operator count reduced 2.6x relative to seed model**.
- **Veniat et al. (2019)**: Random adaptive NAS—controller network samples architectures from a block space composed of DS-Conv + SE modules, using policy gradient reinforcement learning to adaptively adjust sampling distribution, gradually focusing on high-performance efficient models.
- **DARTS lineage (Mo et al., 2020)**: Differentiable architecture search based on cells, scaling depth/width after search; exceeds 97% accuracy on GSCD 12-class setting, model smaller than ResNet/SincNet baselines.
- **AutoKWS (Zhang et al., 2021)**: DARTS and its improved versions (FairDARTS, NoisyDARTS), search space built on "TC-ResNet + SE module" (more aligned with edge deployment than generic cell space); **97.44% on GSCD V2, parameters below 110K**.
- **MicroNets (Banbury et al., 2021)**: DNAS for MCUs, directly incorporating memory (SRAM/flash), latency, and energy into optimization objectives; outputs deployed using TFLM, outperforming traditional DS-CNN architectures in both speed and size.

### Category 7: Hybrid Methods—After Marginal Gains of Single Approaches Are Exhausted
- **Peter et al. (2022)**: Hits three points simultaneously—NAS searches for end-to-end CNN structure + input layer SincConv directly ingests raw waveform + post-training weight and activation quantization. Architecture/front-end/back-endend-to-end.
- **Zhang et al. (2018)**: Classic deployment reference—DS-CNN + 8-bit fixed-point quantization, deployed on STM32F746G-DISCO board (Cortex-M7) using CMSIS-NN kernel, accuracy approx. **94.9%**, outperforming DNN/CNN/Basic LSTM/GRU/CRNN baselines. Its elimination method is very representative: HMM+Viterbi inference is too expensive, RNN detection latency is large, DNN ignores local time-frequency correlations, CNN lacks long-term dependencies—DS-CNN is the intersection after these vetoed options.

1. **Seven-category taxonomy** (Model Architecture/Learning Techniques/Model Compression/Attention Architecture/Feature Optimization/NAS/Hybrid) + systematic review of approx. 250 papers (2010–2025), filling gaps in existing surveys regarding new progress like NAS, KD, FSL, SSL, and the TinyML deployment perspective (Section 1, Table 3);
2. **Systematic inventory of TinyML framework ecosystem** (Table 6, ten frameworks): TensorFlow Lite (Google), ELL (Microsoft), ARM-NN, CMSIS-NN (ARM kernel library), STM32Cube.AI (ST), AIfES (Fraunhofer IMS), uTensor, TinyMLgen, CMix-NN, Edge Impulse, listing compatible platforms/output languages/interoperability libraries for each; accompanying Table 5 gives specs for five representative development boards (Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense: Cortex-M4 64MHz/256KB SRAM/1MB flash; ESP32: Xtensa LX6 160MHz/512KB/2MB; SparkFun Edge: Apollo3 Cortex-M4F 48MHz/384KB/1MB; ST Nucleo: Cortex-M7 216MHz/320KB/1MB; Adafruit EdgeBadge: ATSAMD51 120MHz/192KB/512KB)—these numbers define the physical boundaries of "small footprint" throughout the book;

- **TFLite Int8**: DNN-S 2147.9 / 2 / 75.3; DNN-L 1985.5 / 169.5 / 82.1; CNN-S 280.4 / 81 / 84.9; CNN-L 1986 / 272.3 / 92.3; DS-CNN-S 98.7 / 4.8 / 89.5; DS-CNN-L 1652.6 / 8.4 / 91.6; MicroNet-S 114.5 / 1 / 95.3; MicroNet-L 658.8 / 3.6 / 96.5;
- **Edge Impulse**: mfcc-conv1d(f32) 14.8 / 98 / 82.4; mfcc-conv1d(i8) 13 / 97 / 82.6.

Three main conclusions: Keras→TFLite Int8 conversion **reduces model size by up to 69%** with almost no accuracy drop, and inference time drops significantly; in TFLite, **MicroNet-S is the accuracy champion (95.3%)**, DS-CNN-S (98.7KB/4.8ms) is the cost-performance position; Edge Impulse compresses models to the 13KB level, suitable for extreme flash scenarios but accuracy is compromised to 82.6%.

Anomaly points worth noting for engineering (raw data in Table 7): TFLite DNN-S (2147.9KB) is **larger** than Keras DNN-S (999KB), and the magnitude of DNN-S inference time dropping from 226ms to 2ms is disproportionate to CNN-S's 596.8→81ms; the paper does not explain these numbers, nor does it report the hardware platform corresponding to Table 7 inference times. It is recommended to re-measure before citing this data for decision-making.

### Case 2: Multi-Objective Optimization to Find Pareto Optima (Figure 21, Table 9)
Objective: max(Accuracy, -Model Size) two-objective; Methods: MOSA (Simulated Annealing + Dominance Acceptance Criterion), NSGA-II (Pareto Ranking + Crowding Distance), MOBO (Bayesian Optimization, EHVI acquisition function); Scalarized score = 0.5×Accuracy − 0.5×Model Size. Results (Table 9, Top 5): MOBO CNN 87.8% / 0.25MB ranked first; MOBO CRNN 87.7% / 5.77MB; NSGA-II CNN 78.0% / 0.29MB; MOBO DS-CNN 85.3% / 7.67MB; NSGA-II CRNN 85.4% / 8.19MB. Under equal weight preference, **BO outperforms NSGA-II** (Figure 21), MOBO-CNN has the highest accuracy and smallest size among all CNN variants.

The contrast that must be pointed out: The accuracy of these MOO models (78%–87.8%) is significantly lower than the SOTA (96%–98%) of manually/searched architectures on the same dataset in Table 4. The reason is not reported by the paper (details such as training epochs, search budget, early stopping are not given); a reasonable guess is that the search space was limited to small filter counts (16) and small networks, and the optimization budget was limited. **The conclusion "BO outperforms NSGA-II" holds in the interval of low absolute accuracy; extrapolating to high-accuracy intervals requires caution.**

## Comparative Analysis Findings of the Review
Gathering scattered comparative evidence from various sections, eight translatable judgments can be derived:

1. **The ceiling of parameter efficiency is determined by architecture design, not compression**. The span in Table 4 is from Slimmable CNN's 243K/90.5% to BC-ResNet-1's 9.2K/96.9%, a 26x parameter difference but a 6.4-point accuracy increase—this is the best argument for "design the architecture well first, then compress." The 90–100K parameter range (MatchboxNet 77–140K, TENet 100K, CENet-GCN 72K) is the gathering band for most SOTAs.
2. **The contradiction between 1D and 2D convolutions is ultimately solved by "hybrid"**: TC-ResNet proves 1D temporal convolution is cheap but loses frequency-axis equivariance; BC-ResNet's broadcasted residual combines the cheapness of 1D and the equivariance of 2D (96.9%/9.2K). Evolution is not substitution but hierarchical combination—this judgment is transferable to any trade-off between "cheap operators vs. expressive operators."
3. **Training-phase methods (KD/SSL) and inference-phase methods (architecture/quantization) are orthogonal**, thus stackable: Tucker et al. stacked SVD+quantization+KD to get 93% reduction; Yang et al. stacked SSL with QAT to preserve accuracy under 75% size reduction (Sections 4.2/4.3).
4. **There is data on the collapse point of quantization**: PTQ direct quantization worsens FAR by 32%–41% (Yang 2024); in the same paper, QAT preserves accuracy. Feature-end quantization (error diffusion 3-bit drops only 0.45%, conventional quantization drops over 3%, Luo 2022) shows that **input feature quantization has more room for maneuver than weight quantization**—provided the right method is used (error diffusion + temporal direction propagation).
5. **The value of attention lies in parameters, not accuracy**: SWSA achieves an error rate of 4.19% vs. ResNet's 4.12% (difference 0.07 points) with 1/20 of the parameters; GRU also saves parameters and has lower FRR than LSTM (Shan 2018).
6. **The energy perspective is replacing the FLOPs perspective**: ED-sKWS's 52% energy saving comes from algorithm-level strategies like "early stopping" rather than operator-level optimization; SincConv's 62K parameters come from the insight that "memory access is more power-hungry than computation"; the SNN route (10x energy reduction) pushes this logic to the extreme.
7. **NAS gains are now quantifiable**: AutoKWS 97.44%/<110K parameters; Anderson et al. 95.1%/223.4 MFLOPS, operators saved 2.6x; and NAS accounts for 15.7% of the taxonomy (Figure 10), indicating community recognition that "marginal gains of manual design are exhausted."
8. **There are internal number inconsistencies in the review**: QMatchboxNet main text 30K parameters/96.20% vs. Table 4's 140K/97.4%; Table 7's TFLite DNN-S size expansion is unexplained. Cross-check any single number before citing.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Limitations in Review Methodology
- Literature screening only states "systematically evaluated and excluded out-of-scope papers" (Section 2.1), **without reporting a quantitative screening process** (e.g., funnel numbers like PRISMA), making the composition of the 250 papers un-auditable;
- The boundaries of the seven-category classification involve subjectivity: SSL+QAT placed in compression, SSL+KD in learning, SincConv in features, QNN in architecture—same work can reasonably span categories, the review relies on the "hybrid methods" category as a fallback but does not provide adjudication rules;
- Table 4 mixes numbers from different training recipes, data augmentations, and evaluation protocols (and the WaveNet entry uses the Hey Snips dataset, not GSCD v2), limiting comparability of cross-row direct comparisons; the review does not unify retraining to control variables;
- Coverage of the most cutting-edge 2024–2025 works (e.g., streaming transducers, State Space Models/Mamba-series KWS, larger-scale SSL distillation to edge) is thin, possibly related to the manuscript completion time.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design
- Inference times in Table 7 do not specify the measurement hardware (the paper does not report which board/CPU frequency was used),打折ting the horizontal comparability of latency numbers;
- All experiments only report size/latency/accuracy, **without reporting energy consumption and peak SRAM usage**—which are exactly the footprint metrics emphasized by the review itself in Section 3.7;
- The huge gap between Case 2 MOO model accuracy (78%–87.8%) and Table 4 SOTA (96%+) is not discussed, and training details (epochs, early stopping, search budget) are not reported by the paper;
- Edge Impulse side only tested a single 1D convolution small model, not including strong models like MicroNet in that framework, making the horizontal evaluation incomplete.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions (Paper Outlook + Note Supplements)
The four directions given by the paper itself in Section 7:
1. **TinyNAS Lightweight Architecture**: Transformers/AST have good accuracy but many hyperparameters and high compute; specialized NAS for MCUs (jointly optimizing model structure and inference engine) is needed to squeeze attention models into TinyML devices;
2. **SSL to Counter Data Scarcity**: AST relies on large-scale labeled pre-training; SSAST-like self-supervised methods can learn features from web-scale unlabeled audio (radio, YouTube) and also support continuous adaptation on-device (wearable devices progressively improve from user-specific voice differences);
3. **Sparse Updates for Memory-Efficient Learning**: Backpropagation requires storing intermediate activations, which is a bottleneck for training on MCUs; sparse layer/tensor updates only modify key parts of the network, enabling KWS online training on devices with **<256KB SRAM** (incremental training schemes mentioned in the text, such as TinyOL, quantization-aware scaling/skipping updates, have verified feasibility within 1MB flash/256KB SRAM, Section 5);
4. **Joint Optimization Replaces Sequential Optimization**: Sequential NAS→pruning→quantization is suboptimal; APQ-style (architecture search + accuracy predictor + joint pruning/quantization) is the direction for ultra-low-power KWS; the cost is exponential expansion of search space, requiring efficient search strategies.

Note supplements: The review discusses "user-defined keywords (FSL/QbyE)" and "edge-side online training" in Sections 3.4 and 5 respectively, but does not expand the intersection of the two with the TinyML memory wall as an independent research direction—**"personalized keyword adaptation that takes effect upon registration" on 256KB SRAM-level devices** (combination of prototype networks + sparse updates) is the most obvious blank left by the full text, directly echoing the outlook in Section 7 for "on-device training for personalized keyword adaptation." Additionally, the combination of the privacy dimension (federated learning is only briefly mentioned as a Gboard case in Section 2.6) with SF-KWS is also worth establishing as a standalone topic.

### One-Sentence Summary
The value of this review lies not in proposing new models, but in providing a dual-dimensional classification coordinate of "**intervention point × training/inference phase**," stringing the SF-KWS technology from 2010–2025 from the computational tax of HMM/Viterbi, to the architectural tax of DNN/CNN, to the 1D/2D convolution contradiction and attention parameter economics, and finally to the automation of NAS and joint optimization, into an evolutionary main thread of "cutting off the current most expensive component each time," and nailing the conclusions to reproducible numbers using TinyML framework horizontal evaluation (Table 7) and multi-objective optimization experiments (Table 9)—just be aware of the internal inconsistencies in QMatchboxNet parameter counts and some latency numbers in Table 7 when citing.
