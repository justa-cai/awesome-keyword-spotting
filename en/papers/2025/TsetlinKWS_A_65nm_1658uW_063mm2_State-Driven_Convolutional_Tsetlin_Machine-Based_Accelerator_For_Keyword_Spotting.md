## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the "always-on" voice entry point for edge devices such as smart speakers and wearables. Its persistent listening mode imposes extremely stringent requirements on latency, power consumption, and model efficiency, making it a typical litmus test for the deployment of any low-power learning algorithm. Traditional KWS systems are dominated by Neural Networks (NNs). In contrast, the Tsetlin Machine (TM) is an emerging machine learning paradigm built upon propositional logic and learning automata. It uses boolean literals as input and performs pattern matching inference using logical clauses. The entire computational paradigm is multiplication-free, relying solely on bit-level logical operations; its training employs a direct state update strategy rather than backpropagation. These two characteristics (multiplication-free inference and backpropagation-free learning) make TMs naturally suitable for low-power edge deployment and on-device learning. Previous studies have demonstrated that TMs can achieve accuracy and energy efficiency comparable to NNs on simple datasets, with significant advantages in on-device learning.

From a hardware paradigm perspective (Paper Fig. 1), existing NN-based KWS accelerators are divided into two categories: batch-based, represented by CNNs, and frame-based, represented by RNNs. Because neurons in batch-based architectures are stateless, each inference processes an entire multi-frame feature window, offering robustness but high computational cost (batch-based schemes cited in the paper require up to 3 million operations per inference). Frame-based architectures process frames in a streaming manner, reducing redundant computation by time-multiplexing hidden states across time steps, resulting in higher energy efficiency. However, training requires unrolling and buffering the intermediate activations and hidden states of multi-layer RNNs along the time dimension in memory. This massive storage overhead makes frame-based architectures difficult to support for on-device learning. The simple inference and learning mechanisms of TMs恰好 provide a third path to resolve this contradiction.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

**Algorithm Side: TM accuracy on speech tasks is insufficient and cannot be extended to standard benchmarks.** The Vanilla TM relies on long-term pattern matching on boolean features, limiting its generalization capability. The paper provides two direct pieces of evidence: First, when Lei et al. increased the number of target keywords from 6 to 9, accuracy dropped from 86.5% to 80.5%, indicating that the model struggles as the number of classes increases. Second, subsequent work REDRESS introduced compressed sensing training to refine Tsetlin Automata (TA), achieving 87.1% accuracy, but only validated on a simplified 6-keyword task. In other words, on the Google Speech Commands 12-keyword task, which serves as the industry standard benchmark, the TM route has never produced competitive accuracy, let alone hardware implementation.

**Architecture Side: No accelerator tailored for CTM.** Existing CTM accelerator designs mostly inherit CNN accelerators directly. The CTM image classification accelerator proposed by Tunheim et al. does not support sparse utilization; its CNN-style dense architecture leads to invalid computation and unreasonable bandwidth usage. Due to high concurrency requirements, the model must be entirely loaded into the register file. A more common dilemma (Paper Fig. 3) in edge AI accelerators is the contradiction between data reuse and sparse utilization: Data-driven architectures (typified by systolic arrays) maximize reuse and parallelism by leveraging the regular data flow of ANNs, but the irregular data flow of sparse ANNs cannot directly benefit; utilizing sparsity requires expensive hardware overhead such as index modules and intersection strategies. Event-driven architectures (SNN route) exploit sparsity by processing only spike events, but spike events are unpredictable and unstructured, making weights difficult to reuse vertically. For example, SpinalFlow cannot reuse weights between spike events, forcing it to unroll output channels with a 1×128 PE array, limiting parallelism.

**Compression Side: Extreme sparsity can actually harm compression efficiency.** Trained TMs exhibit extreme bit sparsity—with only about 1% of TAs included, this itself is a huge opportunity for compression. However, existing works apply compression algorithms directly to model parameters without special handling for extreme sparsity: Bakar et al. achieved a 98.68% compression rate using Run-Length Encoding (RLE) on a 6-keyword task; REDRESS used include encoding combined with compressed sensing training to compress the model from 1325.4 KB to 15.4 KB (also for 6 keywords). The paper observes a counter-intuitive phenomenon: when the sparsity rate is as high as 99%, empty rows actually hinder the compression efficiency of standard Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) algorithms, a issue not addressed in existing literature.

**Feature Side: Binarization methods are fragile.** Existing research indicates that binary features are sufficient for KWS tasks (e.g., Jiao et al. binarized the early stage of Log-MFSC using fixed thermometer codes and fed them to BNNs), but current binarization generally relies on fixed thresholds, making it fragile to sensor degradation and environmental changes. Furthermore, feeding MFSC directly into logical models (TMs) causes accuracy loss. Previous TM works commonly used quantile binning for binarization, but speech data is highly imbalanced, introducing significant quantization errors.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

First, the algorithmic challenge: How to enable CTM to achieve accuracy comparable to edge-deployed NNs (target >87%) for the first time on the 12-keyword standard benchmark, with the breakthrough lying in feature representation rather than model stacking. Second, the architectural challenge: How to design a hardware architecture that simultaneously achieves sparse acceleration and efficient data reuse—a combination proven difficult under the NN computational paradigm. The paper must leverage the unique high-bit sparsity and deterministic sparsity of CTM to break this contradiction. Third, the compression challenge: How to design a compression format for the extreme ~1% sparsity, allowing the model to fit into the on-chip SRAM of an ultra-low power chip. Fourth, the scheduling challenge: TM sparsity is unstructured, leading to uneven load under high parallelism, while edge KWS chips are unwilling to pay extra cache and logic costs for runtime load balancing. Fifth, the engineering challenge: To achieve inference core power consumption comparable to frame-based architectures using a batch-based architecture on an older 65nm process, and to prove the competitiveness of the TM route through strict SoTA chip-level comparisons (rather than comparisons with unoptimized NNs).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

TsetlinKWS is an end-to-end inference-dedicated KWS chip system (Paper Fig. 4), consisting of three main blocks: an SPI configuration interface (responsible for chip configuration and model loading, where model parameters are written to the chip via SPI during the initialization phase), an MFSC-SF feature extractor (responsible for feature extraction and binarization), and a convolutional TM accelerator (responsible for efficient inference of sparse data streams). Audio is input via the I2S master interface: the on-chip 8.192MHz main clock is divided to generate a 1.024MHz bit clock and a 16kHz frame clock, driving an external digital microphone for capture. Inside the CTM accelerator: The OG-BCSR decoder achieves horizontal reuse of feature data through synchronous decompression; the distributor sends data to the PE array based on decompression signals—a 58×5 logical array where each column processes sliding literal data for 58 windows in parallel. When a batch of clause calculations is complete, the results of 40 clauses are serially fed into the summation module for class-confidence accumulation. After all clauses for all classes are calculated, the argmax module updates the inference result and sends an interrupt signal to the outside.

The choice of an "inference-only accelerator" rather than on-device learning is because the primary goal of this paper is to first establish the accuracy and energy efficiency of CTM on speech tasks (the paper explicitly positions it as an inference-only accelerator). However, the architecture design leaves room for future on-device learning of CTMs everywhere—the deterministic sequential storage access pattern is prepared for subsequent storage optimization.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Inference Principle of Tsetlin Machine** (Paper Fig. 2): The input is a set of boolean literals, i.e., variables and their negations $X = \{x_1, \lnot x_1, \dots, x_n, \lnot x_n\}$. Each clause is composed of Tsetlin Automata (TA) acting independently, with each TA corresponding to a literal, autonomously deciding whether to include or exclude that literal from the clause. The output of a clause is 1 only when all included literals are true (logical AND). When a clause is satisfied, its associated weight is added to the confidence of the corresponding class (using an extended form for integer-weighted clauses); after processing all clauses for each class, the argmax unit selects the class with the highest confidence as the prediction. Summarized by formula: the confidence of class $k$ is $confidence_k = \sum_j w_{kj} \cdot C_{kj}(X)$, and the prediction is $\hat{y} = \arg\max_k confidence_k$. The entire process involves only logical AND, summation, and comparison, with no floating-point multiplication.

**Convolutional Tsetlin Machine (CTM)**: Analogous to CNNs, it introduces a convolution mechanism for parameter sharing—the same set of clauses is reused across multiple sliding windows. As long as the convolution kernel aligns with the target pattern in any sliding window, the corresponding clause is satisfied. Written in logical form (corresponding to the schematic in Paper Fig. 12): Clause Output $= Window_0 \lor Window_1 \lor \dots \lor Window_{N-1}$, where each $Window_i = \bigwedge_{\text{included TA}} (\text{covered bits on feature map are 1})$. In plain language: The convolution kernel slides over the entire feature map to find patterns; if any position matches successfully, the clause is considered hit, and its weight is voted for that class. TAs that are excluded act as "don't care" and do not participate in matching.

**MFSC-SF Features**: The traditional MFCC process involves pre-emphasis, windowing and framing (window length N, 50% overlap), N-point FFT, power spectrum, Mel filter bank, logarithmic compression, and DCT. The MFSC-SF in this paper is modified based on the efficient MFCC method by Han et al. (2006): the overlap operation is delayed until after the Mel filter bank, implemented by adding adjacent frame MFSCs, halving the sub-frame length and thereby reducing buffering and FFT resources. Spectral Flux (SF) is traditionally defined as the difference between adjacent frame FFTs; here, it is repositioned after the Mel filter bank to align with MFSC, resulting in a feature representation that is the concatenation of MFSC and SF. The paper does not provide the exact mathematical formula for SF, only stating that SF calculation can be completed with a single subtractor within the overlap unit.

**Dynamic Mean Threshold**: Since microphone gains vary, MFSC represents current frame energy, and fixed threshold methods cannot meet practical needs; quantile binning introduces quantization errors due to the high imbalance of audio data. Therefore, a streaming dynamic mean is adopted: the threshold update formula is $th_{new} = th_{old} + x_{new} - x_{old}$ (new value in, old value out, sliding mean). To eliminate division in the mean calculation, the batch size is set to 64, and right-shifting (by 6 bits) is used to approximate division by 64. The entire threshold update takes only two cycles: Cycle 1 reads the original threshold and registers the new MFSC value, while simultaneously reading the old value from the MFSC memo memory at the address to be written by the new value; Cycle 2 performs add-new-sub-old to obtain the new threshold.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: MFSC-SF Feature Extraction and Spectral Convolution (Core Algorithmic Breakthrough for Accuracy)

The motivation chain for this design is worth unfolding completely. First, why remove LOG and DCT: The paper gives two reasons—the single-layer CTM model has limited ability to learn discriminative features after non-linear transformations; DCT transforms features into non-local features, conflicting directly with spectral convolution (convolution kernels require locally aligned time-frequency blocks, while DCT mixes energy into all cosine bases, losing locality). Second, why introduce SF: CTM is a single-layer logical model with limited expressive power; SF captures the degree of spectral change between adjacent frames, perfectly supplementing "dynamic" information, and its calculation is almost free (one subtractor). Measured gains (Paper Fig. 7): MFSC-SF effectively improves accuracy across CTM models of different scales; the 120-clause CTM using MFSC-SF achieves an accuracy gain of about 4 percentage points; since CTM currently only supports single-layer models, increasing input feature dimensions hardly affects model size, effectively a free gain. Third, three power-saving simplifications in feature extraction: removing windowing, approximating power spectrum with absolute value, and replacing triangular Mel filters with unit-gain rectangular filters (eliminating multiplications entirely), resulting in a total accuracy loss of less than 1%. The final selected configuration is MFSC32-SF32 (32 coefficients each) with a 120-clause CTM (the model selected in Fig. 7). Fourth, two binarization strategies for two types of features: SF reflects the degree of change between adjacent frames and is insensitive to gain, so it uses a fixed threshold (1024 in this implementation); MFSC reflects absolute energy and is sensitive to gain, so it must use the aforementioned dynamic mean threshold.

**Spectral Convolution Kernel** (Paper Fig. 11): CNNs are accustomed to using small convolution kernels in multi-layer networks, but CTM is currently a single-layer model, and existing research indicates that one boolean value per coefficient is sufficient to express most information; small kernels would leave the single-layer CTM without enough "attention" to cover global spectral features. Therefore, the paper proposes a 64×7 spectral convolution kernel covering the entire coefficient dimension—the upper half of the feature map is MFSC, the lower half is SF, time-aligned; the kernel unfolds across all 64 coefficient dimensions and takes 7 slices in the time dimension, allowing clauses to learn spectral representations in specific time slices. The cost is slightly higher storage requirements; the paper's attitude is direct: mitigate this with compression (leading to Innovation 2).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: OG-BCSR Compression Algorithm (Turning Extreme Sparsity into a Storage Advantage)

**Format Design** (Paper Fig. 8): For the 120-clause CTM, the convolution kernel is selected as 64×7; since the kernel contains position TAs, they are concatenated into a matrix, considering both positive and negative literals, the dimension of the TA action matrix is 64×16 (the paper directly gives this dimension, without explaining the correspondence details from 7 to 16). The uncompressed model reaches 180 KB—unacceptable for edge chips. OG-BCSR (Optimized Grouped Block-Compressed Sparse Row) uses four tables for storage: Block Index indicates whether there are included TAs within the block (two rows are treated as one block in this paper); empty blocks skip subsequent table storage directly; Row Count records the number of included TAs per row; Column Index gives the column addresses of these TAs; Clause Index additionally specifies which clause the included TA belongs to (introduced by grouped compression). Unlike the original CSR which uses pointers for storage, OG-BCSR uses direct row index storage—the rationale is that included TAs only require sequential access and do not need multiplication with other matrices; direct storage reduces decompression circuit overhead.

**Why apply both block compression and grouped compression simultaneously**: The purpose of block compression is to skip unnecessary table storage using a block index list (addressing the problem of many empty blocks); the purpose of grouped compression is to reduce the matrix sparsity rate (merging matrices of multiple clauses for storage, distinguishing ownership using clause indices), directly addressing the observation that "99% sparsity rate backfires on compression efficiency."

**Graph Matching for Optimal Grouping** (Paper Fig. 9): The grouping strategy directly determines compression effectiveness; the paper transforms this optimization problem into a maximum weight matching problem in graph theory. Construction method: Each TA action matrix serves as a node; nodes are connected pairwise to form a general graph; if merging two matrices causes non-zero element overlap or exceeds the maximum allowed row count, it is considered no edge. Edge weight is defined as the number of empty blocks after merging. The optimization goal is to find a node pairing scheme that maximizes the total weight of connected edges—i.e., the scheme with the most total empty blocks after merging. This maximum weight matching problem is solved using the Blossom algorithm. The limit on included TAs per row after merging is no more than 7; due to matrix sparsity and low similarity, this constraint is rarely violated, not affecting the optimal solution, so the row count table only needs 3 bits. The total number of included TAs for the 120-clause CTM is 15.6k. Compression results (Paper Fig. 10): OG-BCSR reduces the model size by another 2.37 times on top of CSR; when compared with SoTA compression work REDRESS (REDRESS only did the 6-keyword task, the paper linearly scaled it proportionally to align with the 12-keyword task), OG-BCSR has lower memory occupancy (specific scaled values not reported by the paper). The abstract states a total compression gain of 9.84x model size reduction; Table I shows the final model size is 19.9 KB, and on-chip model SRAM is 27.25 KB.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: State-Driven Architecture (Simultaneously Exploiting Data Reuse and Sparse Utilization)

This is the core architectural idea of the entire paper; the name itself is a direct confrontation with the dilemma in Fig. 3: Data-driven architectures exploit reuse, event-driven architectures exploit sparsity, but CTM sparsity is deterministic after training (deterministic)—the sparsity pattern does not change with input, allowing pre-expansion and scheduling at the "state" (static include/exclude state of TAs) level, without waiting for runtime events like SNNs. The paper calls this sparse-deterministic.

**Derivation of State-Driven Strategy** (Paper Fig. 12): In CTM convolution, excluded TAs are "don't care," so only feature map data corresponding to included TA positions needs to be focused on. The key observation is: the data needed by each included TA during the sliding window process is exactly the segment of feature data it covers during the slide. Thus, the sliding process is fully unrolled: Step 1, read the entire row data of the feature map according to the row index of included TAs; Step 2, select the actually covered bits from the row data according to their column indices, obtaining "sliding literal data," whose length equals the number of windows; Step 3, perform logical AND on the sliding literal data of each included TA by window (completed in the PE array, where each PE contains only one AND gate); Step 4, as long as any sliding window result is 1, the clause is satisfied, and its weight is output. Thus, the hardware architecture of CTM for N windows is equivalent to an N×1 PE array—here 58 in 58×5 (the paper does not directly give the source of 58; calculating based on 64-frame feature map and time dimension 7 kernel: 64−7+1=58, which matches exactly).

**Priority Encoding Decompression Pipeline** (Paper Fig. 13): To horizontally reuse input features, multiple matrices must be decompressed in parallel. The paper does not simply replicate decompression hardware but uses a priority encoding scheme, motivated by two factors: First, sliding expansion generates a large number of multiplexers; directly replicating hardware introduces considerable overhead; Second, to avoid access conflicts in the input feature library, matrix decompression must be synchronized (the next block starts only after all included TAs of the current block are decompressed), and the high sparsity of TA action matrices means low utilization when multiple matrices are decompressed in parallel—for example, if only 6 out of 20 matrices in the current block contain included TAs, the decompression logic and PE utilization are less than 30%. The specific pipeline is divided into four stages: S1 loads the block indices of 20 TA action matrices into registers; the priority encoder generates five 2-bit Code signals in groups of four matrices, assigned to five subsequent parallel stages for control; S2 reads row count values for two rows simultaneously to improve storage bandwidth, decrementing cycle by cycle to sequentially decode included TAs; S3 activates when row count is non-zero, reading corresponding column indices and clause indices; when row count reaches zero, it updates the block index to enter the next matrix; S4 updates partial logical AND (Pand) results according to Code signals and clause indices. Through priority encoding, 20 matrices time-share 5 parallel decompression logic units and PE columns. After completing a batch of clauses, the results of 40 clauses are serially sent to the summation module to update class confidence (40 corresponds exactly to 20 pairwise merged matrices, with 2 clauses per group belonging to each).

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Two-Stage Scheduling Optimization (Offline Load Balancing)

TM sparsity is unstructured, making load balancing a hardware challenge under high parallelism. Event-driven architectures must configure runtime balancing components (dedicated dispatchers, rotating FIFOs) due to unpredictable spikes; these components are undesirable for lightweight edge KWS hardware—adding extra cache and logic complexity. The paper's solution leverages the determinism of TM sparsity: hardware activity can be precisely simulated in software beforehand, so load balancing can be done completely offline. Specifically, simulated annealing is used for optimization, with the goal of minimizing total execution cycles; due to the huge search space, it is divided into two stages: Stage 1 randomly swaps matrices between different rounds to mitigate inter-round imbalance; Stage 2 swaps matrices between different PEs within the same round to mitigate intra-round load variance. Taking the 120-clause CTM as an example, 60 TA matrices per class are distributed across 5 PE columns and executed in 3 rounds. The simplified example in Paper Fig. 14 shows that after swapping, the idle cycles for PE0 in Round 1 drop from 5 to 1. Software simulation evaluation (Fig. 15): After two rounds of optimization, PE utilization increases to 63.1%, saving a total of 1.2k cycles, allowing the system to run at a 400kHz clock; the abstract states a PE utilization improvement of 12.2%.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to CNN accelerators (data-driven systolic arrays): TsetlinKWS does not make regular data flow accommodate sparsity, but rather makes the architecture accommodate deterministic sparsity—storage access is entirely deterministic sequential access, eliminating the need for the index modules and intersection strategies required by sparse NN accelerators. Compared to SNN event-driven accelerators: The sparsity pattern is known offline, eliminating the need for runtime event dispatching, and avoiding the parallelism limitations seen in SpinalFlow-style "output channel unrolling only." Compared to Tunheim's CTM image accelerator: The latter does not support sparse utilization; its dense architecture forces the model to reside in registers; TsetlinKWS compresses the model into 27.25KB on-chip SRAM via OG-BCSR and decompresses synchronously. Compared to REDRESS compression ratio: REDRESS is a compressed sensing training route (algorithm-side modification), while this paper is a storage format plus graph matching optimal grouping route (deployment-side modification); the two are orthogonal. Compared to existing TM binarization (quantile binning, fixed threshold thermometer codes): This paper分流s based on feature physical meaning—gain-sensitive MFSC uses dynamic mean threshold with dedicated streaming threshold update circuits, while gain-insensitive SF uses fixed threshold.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The model is trained on the Google Speech Commands Dataset (GSCD), containing 35 keywords. The task is set as 12-class classification: 10 command words (Yes, No, Up, Down, Left, Right, On, Off, Stop, Go) as main keywords, plus Silence (constructed from background noise files) and Unknown (the remaining 25 keywords merged). During training, only 20-40dB light white noise is randomly added—the paper explicitly explains the reason: single-layer logical CTM models cannot effectively handle high background noise; this is a candid statement of capability boundaries; random pitch variation is also used for data augmentation. The training, validation, and test sets contain 74,922, 4,490, and 4,870 audio clips, respectively. Hyperparameters for the 120-clause CTM: T=300, s=8.0, trained for 400 epochs. Notably, the design goal of MFSC-SF preprocessing on the software side is to maintain numerical consistency with the hardware implementation (hardware counterpart), meaning software accuracy can be directly mapped to chip behavior.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Accuracy is measured by 12-class accuracy (the paper reports 87.35% under clean conditions). The energy efficiency system has four levels: First, absolute power consumption and inference core power consumption (inference core = logic + SRAM; this paper: 11.94µW = 3.17µW logic + 8.77µW SRAM, Table I); Second, normalized metrics, where Table I normalizes core area, inference core power, and energy to 65nm ("N." prefix) to ensure cross-process comparability—power consumption of 22nm works is increased by approximately 8 times; Third, unified calculation volume metrics, where Table I footnote c defines normalizing KWS operations per inference to 2-input AND gate operations (#Norm Ops./Inf.), which is the key design that brings 16-bit approximate addition, 8×8 multiplication, 32-bit addition, and bit-level AND onto the same ruler, and is the basis for this paper's claim of "minimum operations"; Fourth, PE utilization and execution cycles (direct output of scheduling optimization, Fig. 15). Latency is reported as total batch inference time (16ms). Model-side metrics are compressed model size (19.9KB) and on-chip storage (33.5KB).

### Findings from Ablation Studies

Although the paper lacks a single table titled "Ablation Study," there are four complete sets of ablation-like analyses:

**Feature Ablation** (Fig. 7): Comparing MFSC vs. MFSC-SF across various coefficient scales and model scales, MFSC-SF is effective for CTMs of different sizes; the 120-clause CTM using MFSC-SF achieves an accuracy gain of about 4 percentage points. More importantly, the chain reaction: Because the model size of single-layer CTM is insensitive to input dimensions, the improvement in feature quality allows reducing model size without losing accuracy—the final selected 120-clause small model is the product of this trade-off. The three simplifications in feature extraction (remove window, absolute value approximation, rectangular filter) result in a total accuracy loss of less than 1%.

**Compression Ablation** (Fig. 10): Comparing CSR vs. OG-BCSR on the same model, the latter further reduces model size by 2.37 times; compared to REDRESS linearly scaled to 12 keywords, memory occupancy is lower. Combining the 9.84x total compression in the abstract and the 19.9KB final model in Table I, the compression chain can be reconstructed: The 180KB original TA action matrix is reduced to 19.9KB through block compression, grouped compression, and direct row index storage.

**Scheduling Ablation** (Fig. 15): Comparing execution cycles and PE utilization across three levels: Initial state, Stage 1 (inter-round swap), Stage 2 (intra-round PE swap); after two rounds of optimization, PE utilization reaches 63.1%, saving 1.2k cycles, supporting 400kHz operation. The underlying logic of this ablation is that deterministic sparsity allows offline simulation for load balancing—this is direct evidence of the structural advantage of "state-driven" over "event-driven."

**Class Scaling Analysis** (Discussion Section): This paper's CTM does not share parameters across classes, so when the number of classes decreases, the model size shrinks linearly—when only 2 keywords are needed, the logic operations per frame drop to 150k. This explains why the design oriented towards 12 classes remains cost-effective when cropped to a dual-keyword wake-up scenario.

## Main Contributions

The paper states three contributions, which, combined with system-level integration, can be summarized into four points: First, algorithmic improvement—proposing the MFSC-SF feature extraction scheme and spectral convolution operation, enabling CTM to achieve competitive accuracy of 87.35% on the 12-keyword detection task for the first time, marking the first head-to-head competition between CTM and edge-deployed NNs on a standard benchmark. Second, CTM-dedicated accelerator—designing a state-driven architecture that simultaneously unlocks high data reuse and full sparse utilization, achieving excellent power consumption of 16.58µW (0.7V) within a 0.63mm² core area. Third, edge-optimized deployment—proposing the OG-BCSR compression algorithm (9.84x model reduction) and a two-stage scheduling scheme (PE utilization improved by 12.2%), the former turning extreme sparsity into a storage advantage, and the latter moving load balancing for unstructured sparsity offline. Fourth (system level), this is the first complete framework for algorithm-hardware co-design of CTM, replacing the common practice in TM literature of "comparing with unoptimized NNs" with strict normalized SoTA chip-level comparisons, establishing credible energy efficiency evidence for the CTM route; all RTL and processes are open-source, ensuring reproducibility.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**Accuracy Ceiling from Single-Layer Structure.** 87.35% is the lowest among the five compared chips, trailing SoTA by 2-5.7 percentage points. The root cause is that CTM currently only supports single-layer models, limiting feature extraction capability; the paper explicitly admits in the discussion: If accuracy requirements exceed 90%, a larger CTM model is needed, and model size and storage access will become issues that future CTM research must face. **Noise Robustness is a Hard Boundary.** Training only dared to add 20-40dB light white noise because single-layer logical CTM cannot handle high background noise; the paper states directly that this system is only suitable for near-microphone scenarios, and no accuracy under any noise levels is reported (opponents [6][7] all report 10dB/20dB/5dB). **Model Storage is a Power Bottleneck.** The 27.25KB model SRAM accounts for 42% of total power, determining system energy efficiency more than the computation logic itself; CTM models (19.9KB) are naturally larger than NN opponents (4.4-14.8KB); the paper admits this is a collateral cost of insufficient feature extraction capability in single-layer models. **Feature Frontend Unoptimized.** The FFT module dominates feature extractor power consumption, and its scheme is not optimal for real-valued audio; the paper explicitly states FFT optimization is out of scope for this paper. **Inherent Cost of Batch-Based Paradigm.** 16ms latency and 191nJ inference core energy still lag behind frame-based opponents (6.9ms, 27nJ); the paper also admits that frame-based architectures are currently more power-efficient for KWS inference; the positioning of this paper is "comparable" rather than "surpassing."

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

First, the title power of 16.58µW at 0.7V is extrapolated, not measured—measurements were only done at 1.2V (40.8µW), with low-voltage values calculated via voltage scaling formulas; although the paper argues for its conservativeness, the rigor of the title metric is compromised. Second, there are minor discrepancies in Table I: Total power 16.58µW minus feature extractor 4.22µW and feature library 1.76µW equals 10.6µW, which does not match the inference core 11.94µW; the paper does not explain the source of the difference. Third, cross-process normalization (e.g., multiplying 22nm power by 8) is a linear extrapolation assumption; real process migration will not strictly follow this scaling, limiting the precision of comparison conclusions. Fourth, lack of fine-grained result reporting: No per-class accuracy, confusion matrix, variance over multiple runs, or confidence intervals; the feature ablation in Fig. 7 only gives a curve sketch and a sentence about 4 percentage points, without listing a precise numerical table for each configuration; the REDRESS scaled comparison only says "lower" without giving numbers. Fifth, the motivation repeatedly emphasizes TM's suitability for on-device learning, but the deliverable is an inference-only accelerator; on-device learning remains at the level of architectural potential (deterministic sequential access facilitates storage optimization), with no experimental verification.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper itself points out four directions: First, flexible class scaling (linear reduction characteristic brought by no cross-class parameter sharing) can be further explored; Second, storage optimization, including memory bank division, setting up near caches, and customizing dedicated storage blocks, directly attacking the model SRAM which consumes 42% of power; Third, switching to more robust feature extraction algorithms; the paper names bio-acoustic features as proven superior to MFCC in robustness, and combining them with CTM is an effective option to counter noise shortcomings; Fourth, larger models and more aggressive compression for accuracy demands exceeding 90%. Beyond the paper, several natural extensions can be derived from the full text logic: Once multi-layer CTM matures, the combination of spectral convolution and MFSC-SF needs re-validation (the argument against DCT conflict is built on the single-layer assumption); There is significant room for real-value optimization of the FFT frontend (e.g., using real-valued FFT or replacing FFT directly with filter banks); The combination of state-driven architecture and on-device learning—deterministic sparsity allows TA state flips during training to be analyzed offline as well—is the most anticipated next step for this route; Additionally, the long-term stability of the combination of dynamic mean threshold and fixed SF threshold in extreme environments (microphone drift due to temperature, aging) is not discussed by the paper and deserves systematic verification.

**Terminology Quick Reference**: Tsetlin Machine (TM) — Machine learning paradigm built on propositional logic and learning automata, boolean input, clause matching inference, no backpropagation; Tsetlin Automaton (TA) — Basic decision unit of TM, autonomously decides whether a literal is included in a clause; clause — Logical AND of several literals, contributes weight to class confidence when satisfied; included/excluded TA — TA included/excluded from clause, excluded means "don't care"; CTM (Convolutional Tsetlin Machine) — Convolutional extension of TM where clauses share across sliding windows; CSR (Compressed Sparse Row) — Classic sparse matrix storage format; BCSR/OG-BCSR — Block Compressed/Optimized Grouped Block-Compressed Sparse Row, storage format proposed in this paper; MFSC — Mel-Frequency Spectral Coefficients (Mel spectrum without DCT); spectral flux (SF) — Change in spectrum between adjacent frames; GSCD — Google Speech Commands Dataset; batch-based/frame-based — Batch-based/Frame-based KWS acceleration paradigms; PE — Processing Element; Pand — Partial logical AND register.
