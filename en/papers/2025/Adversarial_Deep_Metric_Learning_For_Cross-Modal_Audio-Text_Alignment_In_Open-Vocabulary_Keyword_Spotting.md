- **Date**: May 2025 (arXiv:2505.16735, v2 updated on 2025-05-23)

- **Keywords**: open-vocabulary keyword spotting, deep metric learning, modality adversarial learning, phoneme-level matching, cross-modal alignment, SphereFace2, text enrollment

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the task of detecting specific trigger words from a continuous audio stream, serving as a permanent entry point for voice assistants like "Alexa," "Hi Bixby," and "Okay Google." Based on the source of detectable words, KWS is divided into two categories: fixed-vocabulary KWS allows users to use only a preset set of keywords provided by the factory; open-vocabulary KWS (also known as flexible or user-defined KWS) allows users to register any custom keywords. The latter is the inevitable direction for personalized competition among voice assistants—manufacturers cannot preset all wake-up words desired by every user.

There are two routes for registration in open-vocabulary KWS: speech registration (query-by-example, where the user reads the keyword aloud to the device, and the system extracts a template from the spoken samples) and text registration (where the user directly types in the keyword). Text registration is significantly more convenient: it does not require the user to read example sentences multiple times, resulting in low interaction costs. Therefore, recent research focus has gradually shifted towards text registration. The standard form of a text registration system is a dual-encoder structure: during registration, a text encoder encodes the keyword into a text embedding (TE) as a template; during inference, an acoustic encoder encodes the audio into an acoustic embedding (AE), and their similarity is compared in a shared embedding space. The two encoders are jointly optimized using Deep Metric Learning (DML)—pulling together embeddings of the same keyword and pushing apart those of different keywords. Common objectives include triplet loss, AsyP (Asymmetric Proxy) loss, and RP (Relation Proxy) loss. This paradigm is further divided into two granularities: sentence-level matching compares only the single pooled vector of the entire utterance with that of the entire text string; phoneme-level matching aligns audio frame sequences and phoneme sequences one-to-one, performing fine-grained supervision at the phoneme granularity. Existing works have implemented this using attention mechanisms, dynamic programming algorithms (DSP), and CTC alignment.

The fundamental pain point of such systems is cross-modal heterogeneity: audio and text are signals with completely different physical properties. The AE comes from ECAPA-TDNN processing Mel-filterbank features, while the TE comes from a bi-LSTM processing discrete phoneme indices. The two encoders share no parameters, and their output embeddings naturally reside in different regions of the shared space with different statistical structures. DML can only pull together same-word samples at a "point-to-point" level but cannot eliminate the systematic gap between the overall distributions of the two modalities. It is worth noting that this pain point differs in magnitude from the difficulties of the single-modal speech registration route: single-modal registration only requires comparing similarity within the same acoustic space, with no domain gap issue; cross-modal registration forces representations from two heterogeneous spaces into the same metric space, making heterogeneity structural and pre-existing before training.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper reviews existing work from two perspectives, and the shortcomings can be summarized in four points.

First, the supervision granularity of sentence-level matching methods is too coarse. Sentence-level DML losses such as triplet, AsyP, and RP only constrain the single vector after sentence pooling, completely ignoring the fine-grained correspondence between audio frames and phonemes. These losses cannot see which phoneme within a keyword is mispronounced or misaligned, leading the model to learn "overall contour similarity" rather than "phoneme-by-phoneme alignment."

Second, existing phoneme-level alignment methods each have their costs. The DSP algorithm (Nishu et al., Interspeech 2023) uses dynamic sequence partitioning to align the length of the acoustic sequence to the text sequence length. After alignment, a contrastive loss is calculated using the average pairwise L2 distance between the aligned sequences. This hard partitioning alignment is not an end-to-end differentiable learning objective, and alignment errors are directly propagated into the loss. The CTC alignment route requires additionally training a CTC acoustic model to provide alignment information.

Third, the only explicit solution for cross-modal heterogeneity comes at too high a cost. The audio-compliant text encoder proposed by Nishu et al. (ICASSP 2024) is a representative work that positively addresses modality mismatch: it uses an untrained text encoder, pre-trains the audio encoder on large-scale speech data using CTC loss, and obtains phoneme-level text embeddings by averaging the audio frame embeddings mapped to the same phoneme across the entire training set. This scheme is effective but has obvious problems: it requires training a sufficiently robust audio encoder on large-scale speech data; the text embedding extraction process is complex (aggregating statistics over the entire training set); and it only covers phoneme-level embeddings, completely ignoring sentence-level matching.

Fourth, there is a lack of systematic research on combining DML with phoneme-level matching. The paper explicitly points out that although DML has been widely used in phoneme-level matching, "research integrating DML with phoneme-level matching remains limited," especially lacking a horizontal comparison of various DML objective functions in phoneme-level scenarios—there was no answer to which pairwise aggregation function (ELSE, MSP, InfoNCE) is most effective at the phoneme granularity.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, this paper aims to answer four questions simultaneously: First, how to unify sentence-level and phoneme-level DML into a single training framework, making fine-grained alignment an auxiliary supervision for coarse-grained matching rather than a replacement; Second, how to narrow the domain gap between audio and text modalities without increasing any inference overhead—this is the most emphasized point in the paper's self-positioning, namely, introducing adversarial learning to the audio-text modality mismatch problem in KWS for the first time; Third, the hyperparameters (margins, scales) of phoneme-level DML losses are difficult to tune manually; can they be automatically learned by phoneme category? Fourth, when the training vocabulary reaches 210,000 word classes, traditional softmax multi-class auxiliary losses are inefficient under the closed-set assumption; what kind of classification supervision is needed to enhance the intra-modal discriminability of the acoustic encoder?

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall structure of ADML (Adversarial Deep Metric Learning) is shown in Figure 1, consisting of three parts: a dual-encoder backbone, four embedding learning losses, and one adversarial loss. Training data is organized into triplet batches $\{(a_i, t_i, y_i)\}$, where $a$ is audio, $t$ is the text input (a phoneme index sequence generated by a pre-trained G2P system), and $y$ is the word class label.

The input features are 40-dimensional log-Mel filterbank coefficients, with a frame length of 25 ms and a frame shift of 10 ms, normalized by mean per utterance. The acoustic encoder adopts ECAPA-TDNN with 256 channels and 1.8M parameters in total—this is the de facto standard backbone in the speaker verification domain, adopted by many previous open-vocabulary KWS works. The motivation for choosing it is the maturity of its temporal modeling capabilities and pooling layer design. The text encoder is a two-layer bi-LSTM (256 hidden units): phoneme indices are first mapped to 256-dimensional embeddings via a trainable lookup table, then passed through the bi-LSTM, and finally output as a 256-dimensional text embedding via global average pooling plus a fully connected layer. The embedding dimension is $d=256$. Neither encoder contains downsampling layers, so the output sequence length equals the input length ($T_a$ equals the number of audio frames, $T_t$ equals the number of phonemes), which ensures that no temporal compression ambiguity within the encoder needs to be handled during subsequent phoneme-level alignment.

The total embedding learning loss during training is (Equation 3): $L_{emb} = L_{utt} + L_{key} + L_{MM} + \lambda_{phn} \cdot L_{phn}$, where $\lambda_{phn}=0.1$. The roles of the four components are: $L_{utt}$ is the sentence-level RP metric loss (main loss), $L_{key}$ is the keyword classification auxiliary loss, $L_{MM}$ is the monotonic matching loss (constraining the alignment structure), and $L_{phn}$ is the phoneme-level AsyP metric loss (auxiliary, with weight reduced by a factor of 10). In addition, the modality adversarial loss $L_{adv}$ is added, jointly performing min-max optimization with $L_{emb}$.

The most important design decision of this architecture is the "separation of training and inference components": during inference, only the two encoders and pooling layers are retained, and final matching only calculates the cosine similarity between $E_a^{utt}$ and $E_t^{utt}$. Cross-attention layers, monotonic matching loss, and modality classifiers exist only during training. Why design it this way—phoneme-level matching and adversarial learning are essentially auxiliary signals for representation learning; they shape the quality of representations within the encoder, rather than being part of the inference pipeline. Isolating them in the training phase means that all benefits from fine-grained alignment and modality invariance are沉淀 (沉淀 implies "precipitate" or "accumulate" in this context, effectively meaning "stored" or "embedded") in the parameters of the two encoders at zero inference cost. This stands in sharp contrast to many multi-task systems (which still run auxiliary branches during inference).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Phoneme-level Alignment (Equation 1)**. Since the number of audio frames is almost always greater than the number of phonemes ($T_a > T_t$), the paper uses cross-attention to aggregate the acoustic sequence onto the phoneme grid: $E_t^{phn}$ serves as the query (Q), while $E_a^{phn}$ serves as both key (K) and value (V). The affinity matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{T_t \times T_a}$ is calculated, and the aggregated phoneme-level acoustic embedding is $\hat{E}_a^{phn} = \text{Softmax}(QK^T/\sqrt{d_k}) \times V = A \times V \in \mathbb{R}^{T_t \times d}$. The reason for using text as the query is straightforward: the target grid is the phoneme sequence, which is the side with shorter length and clearer semantic alignment. Letting each phoneme "query" its corresponding audio frame interval results in two sequences of unified length $T_t$, enabling position-wise metric learning.

**Monotonic Matching Loss $L_{MM}$**. To ensure that $A$ exhibits a monotonic diagonal structure when pairing same-word samples (phonemes correspond to audio frames in pronunciation order, which is a physical prior of speech), the paper adopts the monotonic matching loss proposed by CMCD to constrain the shape of $A$. Unlike the original work, the paper no longer constructs target matrices with random noise patterns for mismatched samples, simplifying the approach—a "less is more" decision: random noise targets essentially artificially prescribe an unstructured alignment pattern for mismatched samples. Removing it allows the model to accept only monotonicity as a hard linguistic prior, avoiding extra randomness introduced by noise targets.

**Phoneme-level DML Loss (Equation 2)**. After obtaining length-consistent $\hat{E}_a^{phn}$ and $E_t^{phn}$, `tf.ragged.boolean_mask` is used to flatten the two 3D tensors ($N \times T_{t\_max} \times d$) along the batch and sequence length dimensions into 2D ragged tensors ($N^* \times d$), where $N^* = \sum T_{t,i} \le N \times T_{t\_max}$. The text is also synchronized and flattened into $N^*$ phoneme labels. The motivation for this step is to apply the DML loss directly to "individual phoneme instances" rather than "entire sequences," while using ragged tensors to prevent padding positions from mixing into the loss. After flattening, the batch is reorganized into $N^*$ data triplets. The phoneme-level loss is defined using the AsyP loss:

$L_{phn} = \frac{1}{N^*} \sum_i [ \text{ELSE}_{j \in P_i} \alpha(\lambda - S(\hat{E}_{t,i}, \hat{E}_{a,j})) + \text{MSP}_{k \in N_i} \beta(S(\hat{E}_{a,i}, \hat{E}_{t,k}) - \lambda) ]$

where $P_i$ is the set of positive sample indices with the same phoneme as the anchor, $N_i$ is the set of negative sample indices with different phonemes, $S$ is cosine similarity, ELSE is the extended pairwise log-sum-exp function, and MSP is the mean soft-plus function. The meaning of this asymmetric structure is: the anchor-positive term uses ELSE to pull text phonemes towards audio frames of the same phoneme, while the anchor-negative term uses MSP to push audio phonemes away from text frames of different phonemes. The positive and negative sides use different aggregation functions and different intensities ($\alpha, \beta$), which is consistent with the existing trend in sentence-level DML that "AsyP outperforms symmetric losses" (the paper verifies in the experimental section using Table 1 that this holds true at the phoneme level as well). The hyperparameters $\alpha, \beta, \lambda$ control the boundaries and penalty strength for out-of-bound embeddings in the embedding space, which originally required manual tuning; the paper adopts the AdaMS framework to learn them automatically. The key modification is: the original AdaMS defined these hyperparameters at the word level, while the paper changes them to learnable parameters for each phoneme category. The motivation is that the confusability of different phonemes is naturally different (phoneme pairs with similar pronunciations require larger margins), making it more reasonable to learn margins separately by phoneme class rather than using a global set of hyperparameters.

**Sentence-level Matching**. Sentence-level embeddings $E_a^{utt}$ and $E_t^{utt}$ are obtained by pooling $E_a^{phn}$ and $E_t^{phn}$ respectively: the audio side uses CCSP (Channel and Context-dependent Statistics Pooling, the native pooling layer of ECAPA-TDNN), and the text side uses global average pooling. The reason for using different pooling methods on both sides lies in the difference in signal properties—the temporal statistics on the audio side (mean, standard deviation weighted along channels and context) carry speaker, speed, and channel information, requiring statistical pooling for stability; the text sequence is short, and each phoneme embedding is itself a complete semantic unit, so average pooling is sufficient. The sentence-level DML loss $L_{utt}$ uses the RP loss (author's previous work, Interspeech 2024), simultaneously using all three variants: distance RP ($L_{RPL-D}$), angle RP ($L_{RPL-A}$), and prototype RP ($L_{RPL-P}$). The core idea of RP is to transfer the distance and angle relationships (structural information) between text embeddings to the acoustic embedding side, so that the AE space retains the geometric structure of the TE space, rather than just doing point-to-point pulling and pushing.

**Modality Adversarial Loss (Equations 4, 5)**. The modality classifier $M$ consists of two fully connected layers (256 hidden nodes). It takes phoneme-level embeddings ($\hat{E}_a^{phn}, \hat{E}_t^{phn}$) and sentence-level embeddings ($E_a^{utt}, E_t^{utt}$) from a batch as input to predict the modality label (audio or text) for each vector. Both levels of adversarial loss are in the standard cross-entropy form: $L_{adv}^{phn} = -\frac{1}{N^*} \sum_{m \in a,t} \sum_i o_i \log(M(\hat{E}_{m,i}))$, and $L_{adv}^{utt}$ is calculated similarly by batch mean. The total adversarial loss is the sum of the two levels. Training is organized as a min-max game (Equation 5): embedding-side parameters $\theta_{emb}$ (including the two encoders, cross-attention layer, CCSP layer, and keyword classifier) minimize $L_{emb} - \lambda_{adv} \cdot L_{adv}$, while modality classifier parameters $\theta_M$ maximize the same objective, with $\lambda_{adv}=0.1$. The implementation uses a Gradient Reversal Layer (GRL): identity mapping during forward propagation, and gradient multiplied by $-1$ during backward propagation. The benefit of GRL is that it compresses the two-party game into a single forward-backward pass, eliminating the need for alternating training of two sets of parameters, making the training process almost identical to standard multi-task training.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Modality Adversarial Learning (MAL)

MAL is the most core incremental contribution of this paper and is claimed by the paper as the "first work to apply adversarial learning to reduce audio-text modality mismatch in KWS." Its motivation chain is worth expanding: the distance-reducing operation of DML only occurs between "paired samples"—audio-text pairs of the same word are pulled together, but the overall manifolds of the audio modality and the text modality can still occupy different regions of the shared space, manifesting as a systematic shift between modalities. An ideal cross-modal matching space should make the modality label itself unpredictable (modality invariance), retaining only keyword content information. MAL writes "modality unpredictability" into the objective function: the classifier strives to distinguish audio and text, while the encoder receives reverse gradients through GRL and strives to erase modality traces that can be distinguished. The equilibrium point of the game is a representation that contains "only content information, no modality information."

Three specific design decisions each have a "why." First, adversarial learning occurs at two granularities (phoneme-level and sentence-level) rather than just one—heterogeneity exists at both granularities, and the modality gap at the phoneme level and the sentence level are two different ailments; treating only one place does not cure it cleanly. Ablation in Table 2 confirms this: adding MAL only at the sentence level raises AP from 75.10% to 81.41%; adding phoneme-level MAL on top raises it from 85.54% to 86.12%, and finally, applying both levels reaches 86.23%. Second, the modality classifier has only two fully connected layers—a too-strong classifier would make the game an unstable source of adversarial training (the classifier wins easily, and the encoder learns nothing); a small classifier is an intentional weak opponent design. Third, the classifier is discarded during inference, resulting in zero inference overhead, which is a prerequisite for adversarial methods to be accepted for edge-side deployment.

MAL also has value beyond this method itself: the paper attaches it to two third-party baselines (CMCD and PhonMatchNet, both using binary cross-entropy loss and producing only phoneme-level embeddings, so only $L_{adv}^{phn}$ is added). CMCD's AP rises from 46.17% to 49.60%, and PhonMatchNet from 69.97% to 72.45% (Table 2). This shows that MAL is a plug-and-play universal component, independent of other parts of ADML, which greatly contributes to the persuasiveness of the paper's claims.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Cross-Attention Phoneme-Level Matching + AsyP/AdaMS

The second innovation is upgrading phoneme-level matching from "hard alignment + contrastive loss" to "soft alignment + DML loss," and systematically comparing the performance of various DML objectives at the phoneme level. Compared to the dynamic programming alignment of DSP, cross-attention alignment is fully differentiable, with alignment weights learned from data, and can additionally inject monotonicity priors via $L_{MM}$; compared to CTC alignment, it does not require pre-training an additional CTC acoustic model. Compared to the original AdaMS [20], the paper refines the adaptive margins and scales from the word level to the phoneme category level, making "how large a safety margin each phoneme should leave" a learnable quantity. The horizontal comparison in Table 1 (under an isolated setting excluding $L_{key}$ and $L_{adv}$) provides the answer for function selection: RP baseline (sentence-level only) 75.10% AP; adding Proxy-BD (MSP aggregation) 83.72%; adding clat (InfoNCE) 83.59%; adding Proxy-MS (ELSE aggregation) 84.32%; adding AsyP (ELSE anchor-positive + MSP anchor-negative asymmetric combination) 84.42%; AsyP paired with AdaMS reaches 85.54%—the asymmetric combination of ELSE for anchor-positive and MSP for anchor-negative is also the optimal recipe at the phoneme level, corroborating existing conclusions for sentence-level DML.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: SphereFace2 Keyword Classification Loss

The third line of innovation is enhancing the intra-modal discriminability of the acoustic encoder. The authors' previous work found that adding a keyword classification auxiliary loss to the acoustic encoder significantly improves open-vocabulary KWS performance. This paper advances this idea to SphereFace2 (ICLR 2022, proposed for face recognition, migrated to speaker verification in ICASSP 2023). The reason for choosing it is directly related to the scale of the training vocabulary: the training set has 210,000 word classes. Traditional softmax normalized multi-class losses require normalization over 210,000 classes under the closed-set assumption, and the constraint of "being separated from all other classes" is inherently invalid in an open-vocabulary scenario—what is encountered during inference are unseen words. SphereFace2 uses a set of binary classifiers for pairwise discrimination, rewriting "multi-classification" into "a series of binary classifications of 'is this class or not'," which naturally fits open-set semantics. In terms of engineering, the paper transcribed the PyTorch speaker verification implementation provided by the authors into TensorFlow. This loss is added only to the acoustic encoder—the audio side needs to possess discriminative capability during inference, while the identity of keywords registered on the text side is known.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Compared to DSP-based methods (Nishu et al. 2023): The latter uses dynamic programming for hard alignment followed by average pairwise L2 distance calculation; ADML uses differentiable cross-attention for soft alignment followed by modern DML objectives, with alignment and metric learning jointly optimized end-to-end. Compared to the audio-compliant text encoder (Nishu et al. ICASSP 2024): The latter indirectly bypasses modality mismatch via a heavy-equipment route of "untrained text encoder + CTC pre-trained audio encoder + full training set phoneme statistics," requiring large-scale speech data and robust audio encoders, with complex text embedding extraction processes, and only covering phoneme-level; ADML directly governs the mismatch itself via adversarial learning during training, with a simple process, plug-and-play capability, and coverage of both phoneme and sentence levels. Compared to CMCD (Shin et al. 2022): CMCD uses binary cross-entropy and builds only phoneme-level embeddings; ADML is a complete framework with joint multi-losses, and MAL can benefit CMCD. Compared to the authors' previous work on RP loss (Interspeech 2024): RP is only the sentence-level component of ADML; ADML stacks phoneme-level DML, two-level MAL, and SphereFace2 as three incremental layers on top of it. The span from the pure RP baseline to the complete ADML in Table 2 is from 75.10% to 86.23%.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Training uses King-ASR-066 (a commercial dataset from DataOceanAI), following the configuration and data augmentation of previous work [13], resulting in 4.6 thousand hours of word-level speech segments and 210,000 different word classes. Evaluation uses two benchmarks. First, the Wall Street Journal corpus (WSJ): 3.2 thousand different words, 18 thousand word-level speech segments, augmented with synthetic Room Impulse Responses (RIR) and MUSAN noise to simulate real-world conditions; the test set consists of 18 thousand positive pairs plus randomly sampled 50 times negative pairs (approximately 900 thousand pairs), deliberately made into a highly imbalanced retrieval scenario to approximate the real-world deployment form where "negative samples far outnumber positive samples." Second, LibriPhrase: official protocol splits into an Easy set (LPE) and a Hard set (LPH). All models are trained only on King-ASR-066, and the LibriPhrase training set is not touched during testing, to examine cross-corpus generalization ability. Training details: AdamW optimizer, initial learning rate 1e-4, halved every 20 epochs, weight decay 1e-5, trained for 100 epochs, taking about one day on dual A100 GPUs; each mini-batch contains 250 keywords, 2 audio samples per keyword, totaling 500 samples—this "multi-class, multi-sample" batch structure is a standard configuration for metric learning, ensuring sufficient positive and negative pairs within the batch. The initial values for AsyP loss are $\alpha=0.01, \beta=1.5, \lambda=0.01$, which are then dynamically adjusted by AdaMS.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Average Precision (AP) is used on WSJ—in an extremely imbalanced retrieval task with a positive-to-negative ratio of 1:50, accuracy would be dominated by the negative class (predicting all negatives yields 98% accuracy), while AP comprehensively examines precision at different recall levels, making it the standard metric for imbalanced retrieval, adopted by many previous open-vocabulary KWS works. On LibriPhrase, Equal Error Rate (EER) and Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) are used according to the official protocol: EER is the operating point where the false alarm rate equals the miss rate, and AUC is a measure of ranking ability across the full threshold range. Neither is affected by prior positive/negative ratios, making them suitable for the official fixed test protocol.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Horizontal comparison of phoneme-level matching functions (Table 1, WSJ, isolating $L_{key}$ and $L_{adv}$, all methods sharing data and architecture, only changing phoneme-level loss): Pure sentence-level RP baseline 75.10% AP; adding phoneme-level Proxy-BD (MSP) 83.72%; adding clat (InfoNCE) 83.59%; adding Proxy-MS (ELSE) 84.32%; adding AsyP (ELSE+MSP) 84.42%; AsyP paired with AdaMS reaches 85.54%. The paper marks the improvement of this configuration relative to the RP baseline as 8.40% (calculated directly as $(85.54-75.10)/75.10$ is approximately 13.9%, which differs from the marked value, suspected to be due to differences in statistical口径 or a typo). All phoneme-level configurations significantly exceed the sentence-level baseline, proving that the fine-grained supervision brought by cross-attention alignment is itself the main source of gain.

MAL Ablation (Table 2, WSJ): The step-by-step stacking path is 75.10% (pure sentence-level DML) $\rightarrow$ 81.41% (sentence-level DML + sentence-level MAL) $\rightarrow$ 85.54% (sentence-level + phoneme-level DML, no MAL) $\rightarrow$ 86.12% (adding phoneme-level MAL) $\rightarrow$ 86.23% (completing sentence-level MAL), with an improvement relative to the RP baseline of 14.82% (this value is consistent with the direct calculation of $(86.23-75.10)/75.10$). The incremental contribution of MAL alone within the full framework is 0.69 percentage points, but when placed alone in the sentence-level configuration, the contribution is as high as 6.31 percentage points (75.10 $\rightarrow$ 81.41), indicating that modality mismatch causes greater harm to weak configurations; MAL migrated to third-party baselines, CMCD from 46.17% to 49.60%, PhonMatchNet from 69.97% to 72.45%, verifying its plug-and-play universality.

Keyword Classification Loss Comparison (Table 3, WSJ, "ADML (ours)" refers to the best configuration in Table 2): Without classification loss 86.23%; adding triplet (choice in previous work) only 86.25%, almost no gain, which is contrary to the conclusion in previous work [13] that classification loss is significantly effective; the paper honestly reports this anomaly but gives no explanation; switching to AAM-Softmax reaches 87.44%; switching to SphereFace2 reaches 90.24%, with an improvement relative to the RP baseline (75.10%) of 20.16% (consistent with direct calculation). The 3.79 percentage point increment brought by SphereFace2 is the largest single-point loss function gain, consistent with existing conclusions in face recognition and speaker verification fields that "pairwise binary classification outperforms multi-classification."

LibriPhrase Cross-Corpus Generalization (Table 4, all models trained only on King-ASR-066): ADML+SF2 (complete method) achieves EER 1.33%, AUC 99.86% on the Easy set LPE, and EER 20.09%, AUC 88.71% on the Hard set LPH, comprehensively outperforming three baselines—CMCD (LPE EER 4.20%, LPH EER 25.94%, AUC 99.12%/81.14%), PhonMatchNet (2.33%/24.11%, 99.70%/83.39%), and the RP loss previous work (1.54%/22.45%, 99.84%/85.31%). It is worth noting that the margin of leading over baselines is amplified on the hard set (AUC leads the RP previous work by 3.4 percentage points, while on the easy set it leads by only 0.02 percentage points), indicating that the benefits of the complete framework are mainly reflected in difficult samples.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The evidence chain串联 (connected) by the four tables has three threads. First, the granularity evidence chain (Table 1): phoneme-level supervision is the main gain, and the last mile of gain comes from "choosing the right function + adaptive hyperparameters"—AsyP is about 0.8 percentage points higher than InfoNCE-like methods, and AdaMS contributes another 1.12 percentage points. Second, the mismatch evidence chain (Table 2): modality adversarial learning yields huge benefits in weak configurations and narrowed benefits in strong configurations (0.69 percentage points), indicating that phoneme-level DML and MAL govern different sides of the same problem, with overlapping benefits; but keeping both levels of MAL is necessary to reach the optimum, and it remains effective when migrated to third-party methods. Third, the classification supervision evidence chain (Table 3): at the scale of 210,000 word classes, pairwise binary classification SphereFace2 significantly surpasses softmax-based losses, and the failure of triplet in the new framework is an anomaly worth warning about.

## Main Contributions

1. First introduction of adversarial learning to the audio-text modality mismatch problem in open-vocabulary KWS: Using GRL to train the modality classifier and dual encoders in a min-max game, producing modality-invariant embeddings at both phoneme and sentence levels, with zero overhead during inference.
2. Systematic introduction of DML to phoneme-level matching: Based on cross-attention for soft alignment + AsyP loss (ELSE anchor-positive + MSP anchor-negative) + AdaMS hyperparameters learnable by phoneme category, and providing a horizontal comparison of various DML objectives at the phoneme level (Table 1), filling the gap in "which DML to use at the phoneme level."
3. Migration of SphereFace2 from face recognition and speaker verification to the keyword classification auxiliary loss in open-vocabulary KWS, verifying the superiority of pairwise binary classification at the training scale of 210,000 word classes (Table 3, 90.24% vs. 86.23%).
4. Verification of the universality of method components: MAL attached to CMCD and PhonMatchNet brings improvements of 3.43 and 2.48 percentage points respectively (Table 2), indicating that the contributions are not limited to their own framework.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

The net increment of MAL within the complete framework is small: from 85.54% to 86.23% is only 0.69 percentage points, of which completing sentence-level MAL contributes only 0.11 percentage points—there is a clear overlap between the benefits of adversarial learning and phoneme-level DML, indicating that the two paths govern a significant intersection of mismatches. $\lambda_{adv}$ is fixed at 0.1 with no scheduling strategy, while adversarial training literature generally believes that adversarial intensity needs to be adjusted with the training process. First-order Gradient Reversal (GRL) itself is also a weaker alignment means, which may explain the narrowed benefits. The inference link only uses sentence-level cosine similarity; all benefits of phoneme-level matching are transmitted indirectly through encoder parameters. Phoneme-level models (such as false alarm localization, partial matching interpretability) are not utilized at the product level. Performance on the hard set is still not high: EER for LPH is 20.09% (Table 4), meaning that for the most difficult negative samples (words with pronunciations close to the registered word), there is one false alarm in every five retrievals, still far from practical use. The acoustic encoder with 1.8M parameters is somewhat heavy for always-on edge-side wake-up scenarios. Although the paper claims the architecture has been optimized for the computational constraints of the deployment environment, no latency or memory data is provided.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

All results are from single training runs; the paper does not report variance and significance tests for multiple random seeds. The 0.02 percentage point difference in triplet loss in Table 3 is right at the noise level, casting doubt on the persuasiveness of single-point numbers. Hyperparameter sensitivity is completely unanalyzed: $\lambda_{phn}=0.1, \lambda_{adv}=0.1$, the capacity of the modality classifier, GRL strength—these key knobs have not been scanned. The 8.40% relative improvement marked in Table 1 is inconsistent with the approximately 13.9% calculated directly by the standard formula, and the paper does not explain the口径 (caliber/method). There is a lack of direct comparison with the most direct competitor, the audio-compliant text encoder (Nishu et al. ICASSP 2024), which is precisely a method targeting cross-modal heterogeneity. Evaluations are all in English (King-ASR-066 training, WSJ and LibriPhrase testing); multi-language scenarios are not verified; the noise augmentation in the WSJ test set is synthetic RIR and MUSAN, with no evaluation of real far-field, multi-speaker interference. Training requires dual A100 GPUs for about one day, and the reproduction threshold is not low.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The direction given by the paper itself is to explore more advanced adversarial learning techniques to strengthen MAL. Following the logic of this work, there are at least four paths worth taking: First, replace the fixed $\lambda_{adv}$ with adversarial intensity scheduled with the training process, or use more modern domain alignment methods (such as optimal transport, contrastive modality alignment loss) to replace GRL, to test whether the narrowed benefits of adversarial learning are the fault of the method itself; Second, utilize phoneme-level representations during inference, such as performing phoneme-level false alarm analysis and partial matching of registered words, upgrading fine-grained alignment from a training regularization to a product capability; Third, test the entire framework under multi-language and real far-field conditions, especially where MAL may show greater benefits in scenarios with larger modality mismatch between languages; Fourth, orthogonally combine with edge-side deployment technologies (quantization, distillation) to verify the performance retention after compressing the 1.8M parameter ECAPA-TDNN backbone, which is an unavoidable step for Samsung-related works towards product landing. Additionally, the anomaly of triplet loss failure in Table 3 contradicting previous conclusions is worth investigating separately—it implies that there may be a substitution relationship between phoneme-level DML and classification auxiliary losses. Clarifying this point has universal value for designing multi-task KWS training recipes.
