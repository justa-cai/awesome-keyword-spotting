## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) has been the core entry point for voice interaction in smart devices over the past decade: wake words like "Ok, Google" and "Hey, Siri" on mobile phones, activation phrases like "Alexa" for Amazon smart speakers (paper introduction [1][2]), keywords triggering driving assistance functions in modern vehicles ([3][4]), and smart home assistants ([5][6]). The paper further predicts that KWS is about to enter more resource-constrained application scenarios—wearable devices and low-cost sensors (Section I).

The technical route for deploying KWS on MCU-level platforms is already quite mature: Zhang et al. [7] deployed an 8-bit fixed-point quantized CNN model on an MCU, with no loss in accuracy during the compression process; LiCoNet [8] improves computational efficiency during inference while maintaining classification performance by utilizing int8 linear operators; Cerutti et al. [9] went further by using Binary Neural Networks (BNN) for KWS, compressing both model weights and activations to 1 bit on a RISC-V MCU. Compared to full-precision equivalent models, the accuracy loss is negligible, yet it achieved a 71-fold reduction in overall energy consumption (Section I, Section II-A citation). At the chip level, there are extreme low-power implementations such as a 510 nW wake-word chip [17] and a KWS processor with dual computation modes consuming 10.8/15.1 µW on a 22 nm process [18]. These solutions make real-time voice processing possible on battery-powered, cost-sensitive embedded and IoT devices.

However, the paper keenly points out a common blind spot in this technical route: **all these models are static—they do not change after their initial deployment** (original text: "they are limited to static models that do not change after their initial deployment"). The cost of static models in real-world product operations is: every time a new wake word or command word needs to be added, the full process of "collecting data, retraining the entire model in the cloud, and performing a full firmware update" must be followed. For IoT devices with huge deployment volumes and extremely low unit prices, the operational costs and iteration cycles of this process are mismatched with the resource positioning of the devices themselves. Continual Learning (CL) is the technology prepared for "learning after deployment": the model is trained continuously from the data stream in units of single samples or small batches, while maintaining performance levels comparable to "training on all data at once" (definition from paper [19]). Typical applications of CL include providing new classes, new samples of learned classes, and changes in environmental conditions.

The paper selects the most complete "new instances and classes" setting among the three CL scenarios: the model simultaneously faces new data from known classes and entirely new classes (Section II-B), on the grounds that this scenario provides the most comprehensive and realistic learning environment closest to actual usage.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper reviews the gaps in existing work from two directions:

**First, CL methods specifically designed for KWS have high memory and complexity overheads, making them unsuitable for MCUs.** The introduction lists three representative works: Huang et al. [10] proposed a CL algorithm for small-sized KWS, consisting of a network instantiator, a shared memory block, and a set of classification sub-networks; Michieli et al. [11] extracted enhanced temporal information from speech features using statistical pooling, then modeled class representations in the enhanced space using a Gaussian classifier with a shared covariance matrix; Yang et al. [12]'s replay-based CL algorithm adopts a dual-memory multimodal structure, adaptively learning old and new knowledge using short-term and long-term models, and improves generalization and robustness with a class-balanced selection strategy based on confidence scores. The paper's evaluation of these methods is: **to effectively adapt to new keywords, they either introduce considerable model complexity or suffer from high memory consumption due to the need to store training data, which is not ideal for resource-constrained MCUs**. Earlier classic CL schools—regularization-based methods (EWC [21], Synaptic Intelligence SI [22], [11]), rehearsal methods (rehearsal, Gradient Episodic Memory GEM [23]), and generative replay (iCaRL [24])—also suffer from complex learning processes and the need for large amounts of data storage (original text in Section II-B).

**Second, the efficiency advantages of BNNs have never been utilized in the KWS continual learning scenario.** Binarization is the most extreme form of quantization, reducing the precision of both weights and neuron activations to 1 bit [15][16], yielding the maximum storage and computational gains. Several resource-constrained systems have used BNNs for KWS with significant results [17][18], and [9] achieved a 71-fold energy reduction with only a 2% accuracy drop. However, the paper authors state (best of our knowledge): **these advantages of BNNs have never been explored in the KWS CL scenario before**. In other words, the "most compute-efficient model form" and the "usage need most requiring updates after deployment" have never been studied within the same framework.

**Third, there is a lack of engineering basis for algorithm selection.** The family of resource-efficient CL algorithms (only adjusting the parameters of the last layer of the model [25]) already has multiple members such as the TinyOL family, LwF family, and CWR, but no work has answered three selection questions on a BNN KWS backbone: what accuracy each algorithm can achieve, how much training data is required, and how much additional computation is needed for backpropagation. Engineers have no way to judge when making technical selections.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

The core problem the paper aims to solve is: **how to enable a BNN KWS model deployed on a resource-constrained device to continuously integrate new keywords after deployment, without losing old knowledge, while simultaneously quantifying the three engineering unknowns of this route (upper limit of accuracy, data requirements, computational increments)**. Specifically, it involves: in the "new instances and classes" scenario, moving seven resource-efficient CL algorithms that only adjust the last layer onto the MCU-optimized BNN backbone from [9], (a) evaluating classification accuracy in layers from 1 to 4 new classes; (b) scanning the number of training samples in the CL stage exponentially from 64 to 16,384 to evaluate the data sensitivity of each algorithm; (c) calculating the backpropagation FLOPs per new sample using analytical formulas (Table I, derived from [25]) and comparing them with the forward computation volume to determine if the computational difference constitutes a selection constraint.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system can be summarized as a two-stage structure of "**static binary feature extraction backbone + uniquely trainable fully connected output head**".

**Backbone Network** (Paper Fig. 2, following the architecture of Cerutti et al. [9]): The first and last convolutional layers retain full-precision inputs and weights, while all intermediate convolutional layers are binarized; each convolutional layer is followed by Batch Normalization and ReLU activation; the network ends with global average pooling over the entire feature map to generate predictions for each class; finally, a fully connected layer is appended as the model output layer. This layer is the only part trained during the CL stage, while the rest of the model is frozen (original text from Fig. 2 description). The prior performance of this binarized architecture (cited from [9] in Section III-B): accuracy is approximately 90%, only 2% lower than the full-precision equivalent model, while achieving a 71-fold reduction in overall energy consumption through model compression.

Pre-training configuration: Adam optimizer, initial learning rate $10^{-4}$, learning rate multiplied by 0.1 if no improvement for 10 consecutive epochs; loss function is categorical cross-entropy; framework TensorFlow 2.15.

**Why freeze the backbone and only train the last layer?** This is the cornerstone of the paper's efficiency argument, and the reasons can be inferred from three pieces of evidence in the paper:
1. **Computationally**: Backpropagation only involves the last layer. Table I shows that the backpropagation cost per new sample is only a few hundred FLOPs, while the entire model's forward pass is approximately 291 MFLOPs (Section IV-C)—the incremental computation of CL is negligible compared to the forward pass (approximately 0.00024%, calculated by this paper as 707/291M). If more layers are unfrozen, backpropagation must penetrate the binarized convolutional layers, causing gradient estimation (STE) and computational volume to expand sharply, invalidating the "efficient" claim in the title.
2. **Anti-forgetting**: With the feature extractor unchanged, the intermediate feature representations of old classes are not crashed by the new data stream. The risk of catastrophic forgetting [20] is compressed to the class boundaries of the output layer, which can be locally handled by lightweight mechanisms such as parameter isolation in TinyOL v2, replica layer loss in LwF, and consolidation layer weighted updates in CWR.
3. **Engineering-wise**: On the MCU side, only the optimizer state, gradients, and activation cache for the last layer need to be prepared. The memory footprint is linearly related to the layer width, facilitating budget control. This is also the common design philosophy of the "resource-efficient CL" algorithm family from [25] (Section II-B: "A common approach in these algorithms is to only adjust parameters in the last model layer").

**Why retain full precision for the first and last convolutional layers?** The paper only states the practice (Fig. 2 description) without expanding on the argument. The common explanation in BNN literature is: the first layer directly faces the input feature distribution, and the last layer determines output precision; these two locations are most sensitive to quantization noise, so retaining full precision stabilizes overall accuracy. Note that the paper does not report corresponding ablation experiments; this explanation belongs to background inference in this paper.

**Why choose a BNN backbone?** Because under the power and storage budget of an MCU, 1-bit weights plus 1-bit activations are the most aggressive and cost-effective form: weight storage is compressed to about 1/32 of 32-bit floating point, and multiply-accumulate operations can be replaced by bitwise XNOR plus popcount (common practice in BNN foundational literature [15][16]), and [9] has already proven that this route loses only 2% accuracy in KWS. Adding CL on such a backbone ensures that the act of "learning new words" does not break the premise of "saving power and memory".

**CL Stage Settings** (Paper Fig. 3): After pre-training is complete, the model receives labeled samples in a real-time data stream manner—both new samples from known classes and new class samples, randomly shuffled. Upon receiving a new sample (online algorithm) or filling a batch (batch algorithm, batch size fixed at 32), a backpropagation update based on categorical cross-entropy loss is performed on the weights and biases of the last layer. The learning rate in the CL stage is set to 0.05, more than two orders of magnitude larger than the $10^{-4}$ in the pre-training stage, reflecting the need for "rapid adaptation within a small number of update steps"; the paper does not perform sensitivity analysis on this hyperparameter.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

All seven algorithms belong to the "only adjust the last layer" resource-efficient CL family. Let $M$ be the number of pre-trained classes (in this experiment $M=12$), $N$ be the total number of classes after adding new classes (growing from 13 to 16 as 1~4 new classes are added), and the parameters of the last layer be weights $W$ and biases $b$. The objective function is uniformly categorical cross-entropy. The differences between algorithms lie in "which parameter rows are updated, when they are updated, and on how many heads the loss is applied":

**TinyOL**: The most naive online SGD—after inferring a sample, the prediction error is calculated, and a gradient descent update is immediately performed on the last layer's $W$ and $b$. The backpropagation FLOPs per new sample are $2MN + M + 3N$ (Table I). It is the basic building block for the other algorithms: TinyOL v2 and CWR internally reuse the "standard TinyOL method" for per-sample adjustments.

**TinyOL with batches**: The objective function remains unchanged, but backpropagation is executed only after filling a batch (32 samples), spreading the fixed overhead of per-sample processing across the entire batch: FLOPs = $2N + 2MN + (3MN + 3N + 4)/\text{batch size}$. With 4 new classes and batch=32, it requires only 436 FLOPs, the lowest among the seven algorithms (Table I).

**TinyOL v2**: The update rule is the same as TinyOL, but **only the parameters corresponding to new classes (weights and biases) are updated, and the parameters of the initial classes are not modified at all**—directly preventing old classes from being rewritten through "parameter isolation" at the mechanism level: $2MN + 2M + 3N$. It also has single-sample and batch variants.

**LwF (Learning without Forgetting)**: Uses two parallel final classification layers—the **training layer** continuously receives new data and updates, while the **replica layer** remains unchanged throughout the training process (a snapshot of old knowledge); during prediction, the loss function is applied to both layers, constraining the learning of new tasks from erasing what the model previously learned. Because the loss must be calculated separately on two layers, its FLOPs are the highest among the seven: $3MN + M + 7N + 1$, which is 701 for 4 new classes.

**LwF with batches**: The idea is the same as LwF, but the replica layer is no longer frozen throughout; instead, the value of the replica layer is updated once after each batch is completed: $3MN + M + 7N + 1 + (MN + N)/\text{batch size}$, which is 707 for 4 new classes, the highest in the table.

**CWR (Copy Weight with Reinit)**: Also a combination of dual classification layers and weighted backpropagation—the **training layer** is continuously updated at each training step, while the **consolidation layer** is updated only after each batch is completed; predictions during training are made only by the training layer, whose weights and biases are adjusted using the standard TinyOL method. The paper points out that this approach helps the model balance between short-term and long-term knowledge: $2MN + 3N + M + (5MN + 10N)/\text{batch size}$.

**Self-consistency verification of Table I formulas (verified by this paper)**: Taking $M=12$, $N=13/14/15/16$ (corresponding to adding 1/2/3/4 new classes respectively), and batch size=32, substituting into the formulas one by one reproduces almost all values in the table—for example, TinyOL at $N=13$ is $2\times12\times13+12+3\times13=363$; LwF at $N=16$ is $3\times12\times16+12+7\times16+1=701$; CWR at $N=16$ is $384+48+12+(960+160)/32=479$, all consistent with Table I. The only exception is TinyOL with 4 new classes, which calculates to 444 by formula but is recorded as 445 in the table, likely a rounding or typo error by the paper. It should be noted that the paper text writes "N denotes the total number of classes (16)", but actually $N$ is the "total number of classes after adding new classes" and only equals 16 when 4 new classes are fully added—a detail easily misunderstood when reading the table, indicating that the FLOPs in Table I are dynamic quantities that grow with the number of new classes.

**Magnitude sense of backpropagation overhead** (calculated by this paper, not in the original table): The most expensive, LwF with batches, requires 707 FLOPs per new sample. Divided by the forward pass of approximately 291 MFLOPs, this is about 0.00024%. This is the quantitative footnote for the paper's Section IV-C conclusion that "the computational complexity of backpropagation is negligible," and again confirms the correctness of the "only adjust the last layer" design.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: First Systematic Combination of BNN and Continual Learning in KWS

The core increment of the paper is opening a previously untapped intersection: connecting the MCU-optimized BNN KWS backbone from [9] with the seven resource-efficient CL algorithms from the [25] lineage. Previous BNN KWS works [9][17][18] were all static models, while KWS CL works [10][11][12] were all non-BNN and generally involved data storage or complex structures. On this intersection, the paper does not invent new algorithms but rather **establishes the first reproducible evaluation baseline**: a unified 16-class data protocol, unified pre-training process, and unified CL data stream settings, allowing the seven algorithms to be comparable under the same conditions. The value of this "systematic evaluation" contribution lies in turning a collection of scattered methodological choices into an engineering selection table.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Three-Axis Evaluation Protocol for Deployment Selection

The evaluation protocol designed by the paper breaks down "whether continual learning is possible" into three orthogonal engineering problems, each corresponding to a scanning axis:

- **Class Number Axis** (Fig. 4/5): Adding 1~4 keywords (digits One/Two/Three/Four), traversing all possible class combinations—4 combinations for 1 new class, 6 for 2 new classes, 3 for 3 new classes (as recorded in the paper, though combinatorially $C(4,3)=4$, this appears to be a typo in the paper, consistent in both PDF and arXiv HTML), and 1 combination for 4 new classes (all selected). Average accuracy is taken for multi-combination scenarios to avoid conclusions being dominated by luck or misfortune from a single combination.
- **Data Volume Axis** (Fig. 6): CL training samples grow exponentially from 64 to 16,384, fixing the worst-case scenario (4 new classes)—directly answering the product-level question of "how many voice samples does a user need to collect for new words to be sufficient".
- **Computation Axis** (Table I): Analytical formulas and numerical values for backpropagation FLOPs per new sample, compared with the 291 MFLOPs forward total—answering "how much more compute is needed for continual learning".

This "accuracy × data × compute" three-axis protocol itself is a transferable evaluation template applicable to algorithm selection for any on-device CL scenario (such as online learning for visual sensors [25]).

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

- **Compared to KWS-specific CL methods ([10][11][12])**: No network instantiators, dual-memory multimodal structures, replay buffers, or higher-order statistical pooling are introduced, resulting in **zero additional training sample storage**; the cost is that adaptability is limited to the last layer, with new class accuracy systematically lower than old class accuracy (observation from Section IV-A of the paper itself).
- **Compared to the three classic CL schools (EWC [21], SI [22], GEM [23], iCaRL [24])**: No importance-weighted regularization, no storage of historical samples, no training of generators; all learning computation is concentrated in the output layer, fitting the memory and compute budget of MCUs.
- **Compared to static BNN KWS ([9])**: The backbone network and quantization scheme are completely identical (full precision for first and last layers, binarization for intermediate layers, BN+ReLU, global average pooling), but an online trainable fully connected head is appended. The model is upgraded from a "firmware frozen after burning" to a "classifier that can be incrementally expanded", and this upgrade does not change the inference path—inference still involves only a BNN forward pass.
- **Compared to [25]'s gesture/visual sensor online learning**: The algorithm family and FLOPs formulas are同源 (derived from [25]), but this paper migrates this algorithm family to the 16-class setting of audio KWS and verifies its feasibility on a binarized backbone for the first time.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

**Google Speech Commands V2** [26]: 105,000 speech samples, 35 keywords, each 1 second long, sampled at 16 kHz. Following the convention of previous studies, the paper selected 16 classes: 10 command words "Yes", "No", "Up", "Down", "Left", "Right", "On", "Off", "Stop", "Go"; 4 digit words "One", "Two", "Three", "Four"; and 2 special classes—"silence" (no speech, composed of 1-second segments of random background noise) and "unknown" (the remaining 21 keywords packaged together). The reasons for class selection given by the paper are threefold: considering complexity, covering core voice commands for smart devices, and consistency with previous work for benchmark comparison. The total number of selected samples is **61,487** (Section III-A).

**Data Split** (Fig. 1, Sankey diagram showing): 3% of the data (1,845 samples) serves as the test set; the remaining 97% is divided into two subsets—the first subset is used to generate the pre-trained model and contains only 12 classes (excluding the 4 digit words); the second subset contains samples of all 16 classes and is used for the CL task. **Pre-training Data Budget Experiment**: Initial experiments showed that using only 40% of the available data for pre-training is sufficient to generate an accurate model—achieving 91.14% accuracy, only 0.5% lower than using full data; of course, at this point, the model can only classify 12 out of the 16 classes. The remaining samples are reserved for the CL stage. This conclusion of 40% is itself a practical engineering discovery: the static stage does not need to consume the entire data budget, leaving room for subsequent increments.

**Feature Extraction**: Time-domain audio is converted to log-mel frequency domain, with a 25 ms window length, 10 ms frame shift, 64 Mel filters, covering 50–7500 Hz, to reduce data dimensions. The paper specifically points out that this signal preprocessing step can be executed by an external low-power analog frontend specifically designed for embedded systems [27], providing an efficient implementation path for actual deployment—a rare hardware landing detail in the entire paper.

**New Class Setting**: The added classes are the 4 digit words, with the number of added classes ranging from 1 to 4; all class combinations are evaluated (4 combinations for 1 class, 6 for 2 classes, 3 recorded in the original text for 3 classes, 1 for 4 classes), and the average accuracy of combinations is reported.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Classification Accuracy (accuracy)**: The paper explicitly states "because the dataset is balanced, we use accuracy as the evaluation metric" (Section IV-A). Accuracy is reported in two ways: **Old 12-class accuracy** (Fig. 4) measures knowledge retention (degree of forgetting), and **All-class accuracy** (Fig. 5) measures the ability to integrate new classes. The average value of all combinations is taken for multi-combination scenarios.
- **Backpropagation FLOPs per New Sample** (Table I, formula derived from [25]): Measures the computational increment of online updates, which is a key constraint for MCU selection.
- **Number of Samples Required for CL Convergence** (Fig. 6): Measures the data collection budget.
- **Unused Metrics**: Dedicated measures for catastrophic forgetting (such as Backward Transfer BWT), per-class accuracy decomposition, confusion matrices, variance or confidence intervals of multiple runs—the paper does not report any of these.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The baseline is the pre-trained static BNN model itself (91.14% accuracy for the 12-class scope; the gap between binarization and full precision is approximately 2% according to [9]). It is important to emphasize: the paper **does not** perform numerical comparisons with existing KWS CL methods like [10][11][12] under the same protocol (these works were not reproduced under the same settings); comparisons occur internally among the seven algorithms and against the pre-trained baseline. Specific numbers:

**When adding 1 new class** (Fig. 4, old 12-class scope): Differences between algorithms are most significant. TinyOL, LwF, and LwF with batches achieve the highest accuracy, **exceeding 95%, even surpassing the accuracy of the pre-trained model** (original text from Section IV-A: "which even surpasses the accuracy of the initial pre-trained model"). The worst performer is CWR, at 90.3%, 1% lower than the pre-trained model. The phenomenon of surpassing is noteworthy (interpretation by this paper): the CL data stream contains new instances of old classes, which is equivalent to simultaneously performing supplementary fine-tuning on the output head. With only 1 new class, the interference is small, so the accuracy of old classes does not drop but rises.

**When adding 4 new classes (worst case)** (Fig. 4, old 12-class scope): The best algorithm is TinyOL with batches, **88.3%**; the worst is LwF with batches, **86.4%**. The paper's interpretation is: the difference between algorithms is small, with a performance drop of about 3~5 percentage points relative to the pre-trained model—even with 4 new classes added, all methods can keep the old 12 classes at a high accuracy, indicating that last-layer CL is effective in protecting old knowledge.

**All-class scope** (Fig. 5): The overall trend is consistent with Fig. 4. With 4 new classes, TinyOL with batches and CWR are the best performing algorithms; with 1 new class, TinyOL, LwF, and LwF with batches are best. **Overall, all-class accuracy is mostly lower than the old 12-class scope accuracy**—the paper admits "the new classes are not integrated into the model as well as the initial classes" (original text). The worst-case all-class accuracy is **82.9%**; the abstract scope gives a maximum of approximately **86%** for 4 added classes.

**Behavioral Divergence between Sample-level and Batch-level** (Horizontal patterns in Fig. 4/5): Algorithms based on single samples achieve higher accuracy when there is only 1 new class, but their performance drops faster as the number of new classes increases; conversely, batch algorithms are insensitive to the number of classes. Interpretation by this paper: Single-sample SGD has high update frequency and fast adaptation, but large noise per step, leading to intensified competition in the output layer as new classes increase; batch updates average the gradients of a batch, making each step more stable but with fewer steps, so the marginal impact of class number changes is actually smaller.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have traditional architecture-level ablations (such as control experiments for "full-precision backbone vs BNN backbone for CL" or "unfreezing more layers"); its ablation-like analysis manifests in four dimensions:

**(1) Pre-training Data Volume** (Section III-A): Using only 40% of available data for pre-training achieves 91.14%, only 0.5% lower than full-data training—the data budget for the static training stage can be cut by more than half with almost no loss in accuracy.

**(2) New Class Number Scan** (Fig. 4/5): The accuracy of all seven algorithms monotonically decreases as the number of new classes increases, but the old 12-class accuracy remains in the 86.4%~88.3% range under 4 new classes (Fig. 4), a drop of about 3~5%; while the all-class scope drops to a worst case of 82.9% (Fig. 5). The gap between the two scopes reveals the bottleneck position: **the forgetting problem is basically controlled by the last-layer CL, and the real loss occurs on the new class side**—the frozen feature extractor has no specific representation capability for unseen classes like digit words.

**(3) CL Training Sample Volume Scan** (Fig. 6, 64→16,384, fixing 4 new classes as worst case): The results in Fig. 4/5 are the "best case" (using all CL samples in the dataset); in actual deployment, CL samples may be limited, so sensitivity is a key parameter. The results show: TinyOL, TinyOL v2, and LwF (including batch variants) stabilize in accuracy at approximately **2,048 samples**; batch methods (except LwF with batches) require **more** samples—but their specific lower limit values are missing in the original paper text (the sentence in both PDF and arXiv HTML versions is "require at least .", with the numerical position blank, which is a typesetting omission in the original text), only confirming that it is higher than the magnitude of 2,048. From this, the paper concludes that "batch algorithms require more data in practice." In the data-constrained interval, TinyOL and TinyOL v2 consistently achieve higher accuracy; CWR performs worst with few samples but improves significantly as the sample volume increases (interpretation by this paper: CWR's consolidation layer is updated only after each batch is completed; few samples mean few batches, leading to under-training of the consolidation layer; with more samples, this lagging update dual-layer structure can fully exert its stabilizing effect).

**(4) Backpropagation FLOPs Comparison** (Table I, complete values): Taking 4 new classes as an example, from low to high: TinyOL with batches 436 < TinyOL 445 < TinyOL v2 with batches 452 < TinyOL v2 456 < CWR 479 < LwF 701 < LwF with batches 707. The two variants of LwF are **about 55% higher** than the other algorithms (436~479), because LwF calculates the loss once on both the replica layer and the training layer. However, the paper emphasizes: compared to the entire model's forward pass of approximately **291 MFLOPs**, these differences are all negligible—the forward computation volume is the same for all algorithms, so **computational complexity should not be used as a selection criterion** (Conclusion 4).

## Main Contributions

According to the three contributions stated by the paper, plus the selection conclusions summarized by this paper:

1. **First (to the authors' knowledge) use of BNN for the continual learning scenario in KWS**: Implemented and evaluated seven SOTA resource-efficient CL algorithms (TinyOL, TinyOL with batches, TinyOL v2, TinyOL v2 with batches, LwF, LwF with batches, CWR) on a BNN KWS model optimized for resource-constrained MCUs. Comparison dimensions cover both classification accuracy and backpropagation FLOPs, aiming for a scalable, energy-saving, continuously learnable new words, and old-knowledge-preserving KWS solution.
2. **Systematic evaluation of the impact of new class numbers**: Quantified the trade-off between "generalizing to new keywords" and "maintaining old keyword performance" across all combinations of 1~4 new classes—accuracy exceeds 95% (surpassing pre-training) with 1 new class, and reaches a maximum of about 86% and a worst case of 82.9% with 4 new classes—providing data support for the design of scalable KWS models.
3. **Quantification of the impact of CL training sample volume**: Identified the sensitivity of each algorithm to training set size, finding that batch algorithms are more sensitive to data volume and require more samples to perform well, providing direct reference for data collection budgets in actual deployment.
4. **(Summarized by this paper) Complete Algorithm Selection Guide** (Four conclusions from Section V of the paper): All seven algorithms can be used for BNN CL solutions, with accuracy still above 82% in the worst case; algorithm selection depends on the number of new classes—choose TinyOL or LwF for 1 new word, choose the more consistent TinyOL v2 or CWR for multiple new words; avoid batch algorithms in data-constrained scenarios (TinyOL family performs consistently better with small samples); computational complexity can be removed from selection criteria.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Ceiling of only training the last layer**: The feature extractor is frozen throughout, so new classes can only be squeezed into the existing feature space. The paper's own experiments show the cost—overall accuracy is systematically lower than the old 12-class accuracy (Section IV-A), and new classes are not fully integrated. If the added keywords have large feature differences from old classes (new languages, dialects, special acoustic environments), this ceiling will be even lower. The paper does not attempt any form of partial unfreezing or representation adaptation.
- **Impact of BNN on CL is not isolated and measured**: The paper does not have a control experiment for "full-precision backbone vs binarized backbone under the same CL protocol"; the additional loss in continual learning accuracy brought by binarization is unquantified (not reported by the paper). The cited "only drops 2%" is a number from [9] in a static training scenario and cannot be directly extrapolated to the CL scenario.
- **Missing mechanism details for adding classes at the end**: How the output neurons (weight rows) for new classes are initialized, where the "new class parameters" of TinyOL v2 come from, and when batch algorithms expand the output dimensions, are not described by the paper (not reported), affecting the completeness of reproduction.
- **Single run, no statistical testing**: No variance or confidence intervals are reported for all accuracy numbers; the 1~2 percentage point gaps between algorithms (e.g., 88.3% vs 86.4% with 4 new classes) are questionable in statistical significance without repeated experiments.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Not deployed and measured on a real MCU**: Energy consumption, latency, and memory usage are not measured in practice; all experiments are completed in server-side simulation with TensorFlow 2.15, and the computation comparison is based on FLOPs analytical formulas rather than measured cycle counts. The paper itself lists "deployment evaluation on resource-constrained MCUs" as future work in the conclusions—this is the biggest gap between this paper and the title's promise, after all, the "71-fold energy reduction" is a static inference number from [9]. Although the CL overhead of this paper is theoretically negligible, the real costs introduced by online training, such as memory read/write, optimizer state management, etc., have not been validated on the ground.
- **Small test set scale**: Only 1,845 samples (3%), with limited discrimination power for accuracy in the 82%~95% range.
- **Lack of head-to-head comparison with existing KWS CL methods**: [10][11][12] were not reproduced under the same protocol, and the relative advantage of "resource efficiency" was not quantified, only qualitatively criticized.
- **Lack of joint training upper bound anchor**: The upper bound accuracy of training all 16 classes together is not reported (91.14% is for the 12-class scope), and the true cost of CL lacks an upper bound reference, so readers cannot calculate "how far continual learning is from one-time retraining".
- **Two flaws in the original text**: The paper records the number of combinations for 3 new classes as 3 (combinatorially $C(4,3)=4$, likely a typo); the numerical value for the lower limit of batch algorithm sample volume is missing in the original text ("require at least .", blank in both PDF and HTML versions), only able to judge from Fig. 6 that it is higher than 2,048.
- **Single scenario**: Single dataset (GSC V2), close-talking clean speech, no noise/ far-field/ multi-speaker robustness evaluation; two key hyperparameters, CL learning rate 0.05 and batch size 32, have no sensitivity analysis.
- **Long-sequence continual learning not evaluated**: CL is executed only in one stage (adding 1~4 classes at once), without multi-stage sequence experiments of "arriving multiple times, continuing for months", so the cumulative effect of forgetting is unknown.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Directions stated by the paper (Section V): More detailed research on the minimum scale of CL datasets, especially the impact of batch size and the reuse of the same batch of data over multiple epochs; and deployment evaluation on resource-constrained MCUs. Prospects supplemented by this paper: **Progressive partial unfreezing**—gradually relaxing from only adjusting the last layer to fine-tuning the end convolutional blocks with a low learning rate, exchanging new class accuracy while controlling forgetting; **Introducing binarized operators into backpropagation**—utilizing XNOR/popcount to accelerate end-layer gradient calculation, making "efficiency" run through training, not just inference; **Combining with int8 quantization or low-rank updates**—finding a CL-friendly intermediate point between 1 bit and full precision; **Real product vocabulary validation**—scalability and limit testing of class number growth in scenarios with hundreds of custom wake words; **Completing evaluation scopes**—introducing forgetting measures like BWT, per-class accuracy decomposition, multi-stage CL sequences, and statistical reports of repeated experiments; **Edge-cloud collaboration**—using cloud distillation to constrain edge-side CL drift, balancing personalization and consistency.
