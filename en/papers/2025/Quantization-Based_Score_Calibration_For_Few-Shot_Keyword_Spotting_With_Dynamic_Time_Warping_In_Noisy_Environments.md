# Quantization-Based Score Calibration for Few-Shot Keyword Spotting with Dynamic Time Warping in Noisy Environments

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Kevin Wilkinghoff, Alessia Cornaggia-Urrigshardt, Zheng-Hua Tan (Department of Electronic Systems, Aalborg University / Pioneer Centre for AI; Fraunhofer FKIE, Germany)
- **Date**: October 2025 (arXiv:2510.15432; v2 updated on January 21, 2026)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15432
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, few-shot learning, dynamic time warping, score calibration, vector quantization, threshold estimation, HF channel noise

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The task of Keyword Spotting (KWS) is to detect the occurrence of specific spoken words or phrases within a continuous audio stream. Typical applications include wake-up voice assistants, large-scale database content retrieval, and (radio) communication monitoring. This task has several inherent difficulties: First, KWS is essentially an open-set classification problem—the vast majority of words and non-speech sounds in recordings do not belong to any target keyword, and the system must be capable of rejecting an endless stream of unknown classes. Second, in many scenarios, there are extremely few training samples for each keyword (few-shot); the KWS-DailyTalk training set used in this paper contains only 5 isolated samples per class, with a total duration of just 39 seconds. Third, users often require the precise start and end points (on-offset) of the detected keywords. Fourth, many KWS applications are deployed on small devices with limited computational resources. These constraints combined make KWS far from solved, and these problems are further amplified in noisy environments.

On top of all these difficulties, the paper focuses on a环节 (aspect) that is rarely discussed in isolation but directly determines the success or failure of the system: **the estimation of detection thresholds**. Any KWS system must eventually binarize continuous detection scores into "detected/not detected," which requires a (or a set of) detection threshold(s). The conventional approach is to greedily optimize a performance metric on a validation set to select the threshold. This greedy selection often yields highly suboptimal thresholds when there is a mismatch between the validation set and the test environment—especially under acoustic environment changes, additive noise, and few-shot scenarios—leading to a significant collapse in test performance. The experimental section of the paper will demonstrate that an embedding system performing excellently on a clean validation set can perform worse than traditional hand-crafted feature baselines if the threshold is mismatched under noisy conditions (F-score 18.7 vs. 26.8 at 6 dB SNR, Table 1).

The application context is also noteworthy: the motivation for this paper comes from mission-critical communications, where analog radio stations (shortwave HF radio) still play an important role. Therefore, the experiments use noise data constructed by superimposing additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) on HF channel simulations based on the Watterson model. This determines that the noise type in the paper is a mixture of channel-colored noise and white noise, rather than the common additive environmental noise or reverberation.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper systematically outlines three gaps in existing work in the introduction:

- **Misalignment of KWS score calibration work**: Existing KWS score calibration research (References [15–18]) focuses on "aligning thresholds across different keyword classes," which is necessary for models that do not rely on discriminative training and do not explicitly distinguish target keywords. However, for discriminatively trained SOTA systems, the model itself is trained to output well-calibrated posterior probabilities, reducing the need for cross-class threshold alignment. More problematically, in an open-set setting, the system also needs to provide the start and end points for each detected event. Since different samples have different alignment methods and on/offsets, it is difficult to decide which auxiliary score to use for normalization—this directly blocks the path of "score-level normalization."
- **Threshold estimation in noisy environments is unaddressed**: None of the aforementioned calibration works address the problem of "difficulty in estimating thresholds under noisy conditions," which is one of the main sources of performance collapse in noisy KWS (as confirmed by experiments in Section 4.4 of the paper).
- **Quantization technology is used in another direction**: Existing work on quantization in the KWS field is almost entirely used for compressing model size (References [23–26]), and no one has used it to calibrate detection scores. The paper points to two pieces of indirect evidence suggesting that quantization is beneficial for the representation itself: quantization has historically been used for speech enhancement (References [20,21]), and there is evidence that quantization can produce more meaningful speech representations (Reference [22], i.e., the discretization mechanism of wav2vec 2.0). This is the knowledge gap the paper aims to fill.

Additionally, traditional DTW-based template KWS uses frame-level hand-crafted acoustic features such as MFCC or HFCC (Reference [30]), which have weaker discriminative power than deep learning representations. This is also the reason the paper chooses learned embeddings as DTW features (following their previous work [29,31]).

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

The core challenge can be summarized in one sentence: **How to make the detection scores of DTW-based template KWS insensitive to threshold selection in noisy environments without retraining the embedding model and without introducing additional auxiliary scores.** Broken down, this involves three layers: First, the scores themselves must be stable under noise (small drift in score distribution under different noise conditions), so that thresholds estimated on the validation set can be transferred to test conditions. Second, the score scales across different keywords must be comparable, avoiding the fragility of "each keyword requiring a precise threshold." Third, the method must be compatible with the DTW pipeline and the few-shot setting—requiring no large amounts of labeled data or additional training stages. The paper's answer is to reuse the center (centers) codebook learned during the embedding model's training, performing a two-step calibration of "quantization + quantization error normalization" at the embedding level.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system is a template matching pipeline. The new module proposed in this paper is inserted after embedding extraction and before DTW scoring:

1. **Preprocessing**: All waveforms are resampled to 16 kHz, high-pass filtered at 50 Hz, and amplitude-normalized to 1;
2. **Feature Extraction**: log-mel spectrograms, 64 mel bands, STFT with a Hanning window of length 1024 and a frame shift of 256;
3. **Embedding Model**: A modified ResNet with 713,486 parameters (excluding loss parameters). The structure consists of 4 groups, each with 2 residual blocks (8 residual blocks in total). Each residual block contains a 3×3 convolution, max-pooling along the frequency dimension, and 20% dropout. After the blocks, a global max-pooling layer and a linear embedding layer outputting 128 dimensions are applied (separately for the frequency and channel dimensions);
4. **Score Calibration Proposed in This Paper**: For each frame's embedding, first quantize (replace with the nearest center), then normalize the quantization error (scale by the distance to the codebook), and add the two paths together (see equations (3)–(5) below);
5. **Construction of Templates and Test Sequences**: Spectrograms are cut into short overlapping segments. Embeddings of each segment are aggregated (averaged) according to the overlap relationship, mapping any length of recording to an embedding sequence aligned with frames (Fig. 1);
6. **DTW Backend**: Multi-sample DTW (Reference [29]), allowing step sizes (1,1), (2,1), (1,2). The cost matrix is defined as $cost(query, test)_{i,j} = 1 - \langle \phi(query)_i, \phi(test)_j \rangle$, with all embeddings $\ell_2$-normalized (Equation (2)). Subsequence DTW allows the start and end points of a path in one sequence to move freely, naturally providing the on/offset of detected events;
7. **Post-processing**: First, shorten mutually overlapping detections (keep only the event with the highest score at each time step), then discard detections shorter than half the length of the corresponding query template.

Design motivation analyzed item by item:

- **Why choose the DTW template route instead of end-to-end classification**: The few-shot setting (5 samples per class) is naturally suitable for template matching—no need to train a classification head for each new keyword; subsequence DTW directly produces on/offset, meeting the output requirements of open-set tasks; the computational form of template matching is also suitable for resource-constrained devices.
- **Why use learned embeddings instead of hand-crafted features**: The embedding model is discriminatively trained using the TACos loss (Reference [31]), learning frame-level representations that evolve over time and are discriminative for keywords, making them more suitable as DTW templates than MFCC/HFCC. The paper provides evidence in Table 1 on the validation set (thresholds approximately optimal): the average F-score for embedding templates is 46.1, while for HFCC templates it is only 25.2, a gap of over 20 percentage points.
- **Why calibration is placed at the embedding level rather than the score level**: As stated in the problem statement, in open-set scenarios, the on/offsets of different samples vary, making it impossible to find a unified auxiliary score for score-level normalization; operations at the embedding level take effect once for any subsequent pairwise comparison.
- **Why the idea of using quantization for calibration**: Two pieces of indirect evidence (quantization can be used for speech enhancement [20,21]; quantization can produce more meaningful speech representations [22]) plus one direct intuition—centers learned during training aggregate information from a large number of training samples and are insensitive to noise in individual samples. Therefore, pulling noise-perturbed embeddings back to the nearest center is equivalent to smoothing out single-sample noise using the "average opinion of all training samples."

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

**TACos Training Objective (Background System)**. The TACos loss extends the AdaCos loss (Reference [32]) from single embeddings to embedding sequences with the same temporal resolution as the input spectrogram. Let the number of keyword classes be $N_{kw}$ and the number of position classes be $N_{pos}$ (representing the position of a segment within a keyword sample). Each (keyword, position) class corresponds to a set of trainable centers $C_{i_{kw}, i_{pos}} \subset \mathbb{R}^D$. The spectrogram segment $x^{(seg)} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times M}$ is mapped to an embedding sequence $\phi(x^{(seg)}) \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times D}$. During training, "set similarity" is calculated for each class (Equation (1)):

$$sim\_sets(\phi(x^{(seg)}), C) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_t \max_{c \in C} sim(\phi(x^{(seg)})_t, c)$$

That is, take the maximum over the time dimension and the center dimension first, then average over time. This scalar serves as the input to the AdaCos loss. The model thus learns to predict both "which keyword" and "which position in the keyword this segment is located at," producing discriminative embeddings that evolve over time. Negative samples are also introduced during training: a no-speech class based on background noise from SpeechCommands, and time-reversed speech segments, which do not belong to any keyword or position class. The number of centers per class is set to $N_C = 16$.

**DTW Cost (Equation (2))**: $cost_{i,j} = 1 - \langle \phi(query)_i, \phi(test)_j \rangle$, where similarity is uniformly defined as the cosine similarity/inner product between unit-normalized embeddings, and cost is the non-negative scalar used by DTW.

**Step 1: Template Quantization (Equation (3))**. Replace each frame's embedding with its nearest center in the entire codebook:

$$\kappa(\phi(x^{(seg)})_t) := \arg\max_{i_{kw}, i_{pos}, c \in C_{i_{kw}, i_{pos}}} \langle \phi(x^{(seg)})_t, c \rangle$$

The $\arg\max$ is expanded over all keyword classes, all position classes, and all 16 centers per class, executed frame-by-frame for $t = 1, \dots, T$.

**Step 2: Quantization Error Normalization (Equation (4))**. Scale the embedding itself by the degree to which it is explained by the codebook:

$$\nu(\phi(x^{(seg)})_t) := \phi(x^{(seg)})_t / (1 + \max_{i_{kw}, i_{pos}, c} \langle \phi(x^{(seg)})_t, c \rangle)$$

Since all embeddings are unit-normalized, the maximum inner product takes values in the $[-1, 1]$ interval, and the denominator falls in the $[0, 2]$ interval; the closer the embedding is to a certain center (better explained by the codebook), the greater the magnitude of reduction.

**Combination (Equation (5))**: $\gamma(\phi(x^{(seg)})_t) := \kappa(\phi(x^{(seg)})_t) + \nu(\phi(x^{(seg)})_t)$, the two paths are added frame-by-frame before aggregation. The key observation is the **linearity of inner products**: the similarity between any two frames after modification $\langle \gamma_a, \gamma_b \rangle = \langle \kappa_a, \kappa_b \rangle + \langle \kappa_a, \nu_b \rangle + \langle \nu_a, \kappa_b \rangle + \langle \nu_a, \nu_b \rangle$. The contributions of the quantization and normalization paths are directly added in the final similarity score entering DTW, without needing to modify any mechanism of DTW. This is also the reason why the two steps are "separable and combinable"—each step can be enabled individually.

Understanding the role of these two steps from a mechanism perspective (this paragraph is the author's interpretation; the original paper only provides qualitative discussions): The quantization step discretizes the continuous embedding space into a finite set of codewords. As long as small perturbations of noise on embeddings do not change the winner of the $\arg\max$, the quantization output will not change, and pairwise similarities are thus pinned to a discrete grid determined by "codeword pairs." The score distribution becomes piecewise stable against noise perturbations, reducing the drift in score distribution between the validation set and the test set, making thresholds naturally more transferable. The normalization step is an adaptive gain—the greater the noise, the farther the embedding is from the codebook (smaller maximum inner product), the smaller the denominator $1+s_{max}$, the larger the scaling coefficient $1/(1+s_{max})$, partially compensating for the attenuation of the original inner product caused by noise, pulling the score scale under noisy conditions back to that of clean conditions. This is exactly what the paper means by "similar to local density anomaly score normalization [34], aiming to reduce domain mismatch caused by different noise conditions."

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Embedding Quantization Reusing Training Centers

The first innovation itself is only one line, Equation (3), but there are three notable decisions in its design. First, **zero-cost codebook acquisition**: The centers are not trained additionally but are a natural byproduct of training the embedding model with the TACos loss (16 centers per class). The entire calibration process requires no additional training stages, strictly satisfying the resource constraints of few-shot learning. Second, **symmetric quantization on query and test sides**: As seen in the middle column of Fig. 2, both query embeddings and test embeddings are mapped to their respective nearest centers. DTW actually compares the similarity of "center-to-center," suppressing noise on both sides simultaneously. Third, **the codebook has positional structure**: The $\arg\max$ covers all centers of keyword $\times$ position, meaning the quantization retains not only "what the content is" but also "which stage of the keyword it is at," cooperating with DTW's time alignment mechanism. The effectiveness of this mechanism is directly verified in Table 2: using only the quantization step, the average F-score on the noisy test set increased from 27.9 (uncalibrated) to 31.3 (Table 1, Table 2 test average column), proving that the intuition that "scores become more thresholdable" holds.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Normalization Driven by Quantization Error

The second innovation (Equation (4)) uses "how close to the nearest center" as a per-frame quality/local density indicator to normalize the scale of the embedding. It complements the quantization step: quantization answers "which codeword does this frame belong to" (discrete, robust but loses precision), while normalization answers "how well is this frame explained by the codebook" (continuous, retains original information but re-weights according to credibility). The paper gives a clear conclusion in Section 4.5 regarding the division of labor between the two: quantization mainly contributes "scores that are more robust to noise and easier to set thresholds for," while normalization mainly "reduces the mismatch between decision thresholds of different keywords." This explains why normalization performs better on the validation set and high SNR (greater than 24 dB) conditions (Table 2: validation set average 47.0 vs. quantization's 40.4; 30 dB test 64.0 vs. 55.5), because the validation set thresholds match the data conditions, at which point the performance bottleneck shifts to cross-keyword threshold mismatch, which is exactly what normalization aims to solve. Meanwhile, the paper admits: since the embedding model is discriminatively trained, its scores are already relatively aligned across classes, so the improvement of normalization relative to no calibration is marginal (validation set average from 46.1 to 47.0, comparing Table 1 and Table 2)—this honest analysis of "pointing out that the benefit of one of its own components is limited" is one of the highlights of the paper's experimental section.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Difference from KWS score calibration literature [15–18]**: Those methods perform cross-class threshold alignment at the score level, usually requiring the selection of auxiliary scores or cohorts; this paper operates at the embedding level, requires no auxiliary scores, and expands the goal from "cross-class alignment" to "robustness across noise conditions."
- **Difference from KWS quantization literature [23–26]**: Those methods quantize model weights to compress storage and accelerate inference; this paper quantizes activations (embedding vectors), with the purpose not being to save model volume but to calibrate scores. The two are orthogonal and can theoretically be stacked.
- **Relationship with local density anomaly score normalization [34]**: The idea is同源 (of the same origin) (normalizing scores using the "typicality" of the sample in the model's view), but the object of action has shifted from anomaly scores in anomaly sound detection to embedding similarities in KWS, and the normalization signal has been changed to quantization error.
- **Relationship with speech enhancement quantization [20,21]**: It is an inspirational citation rather than the same mechanism—this paper does not reconstruct the signal, but merely borrows the empirical fact that "quantization has a noise suppression effect."
- **Difference from traditional DTW-KWS [30]**: Replaces hand-crafted features like MFCC/HFCC with discriminatively trained learned embeddings as templates, while keeping the DTW backend unchanged, allowing the variable of "feature/representation" to be cleanly ablated (the HFCC baseline uses the exact same DTW backend).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The experiments use the open-set few-shot KWS dataset **KWS-DailyTalk** (constructed based on the DailyTalk conversational speech dataset, References [31,35]). Unlike common KWS datasets such as Speech Commands, KWS-DailyTalk's goal is not only to predict the correct keyword but also to determine the start and end points of the detected keyword, making the task more difficult. Data composition: Detect 15 keywords (afternoon, airport, cash, credit card, deposit, dollar, evening, expensive, house, information, money, morning, night, visa, yuan) in clean recordings of English conversations. The training set has 5 isolated keyword samples per class (5-shot), with a total duration of only 39 seconds; the validation set has 156 sentences, and the test set has 157 sentences, each about 10 minutes long. Each keyword class appears approximately 12 times in each set, with sentences containing several or zero keywords.

Noise simulation: Driven by mission-critical communication applications, it adopts HF channel simulation based on the **Watterson model** (Reference [38]) superimposed with additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN). Specifically, it adopts the medium condition of ionospheric wave propagation in ITU standards (ITU-R F.1487, Reference [39]): multipath differential delay 1 ms, Doppler spread 0.5 Hz. SNR ranges from -12 dB to 30 dB, with a step of 3 dB, generating 15 SNR levels, creating 15 versions of the dataset. The embedding model training uses background noise samples from SpeechCommands as the no-speech negative class, supplemented by time-reversed segments as negative samples.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric used is the **micro-averaged event-level F-score**. The rationale for selection: KWS-DailyTalk requires the time boundaries of detected events to be correct, and event-level metrics (rather than frame-level) can reflect the quality of on/offset; micro-averaging aggregates detections across all classes, reflecting the overall detection quality of the system and not amplifying noise for classes with few occurrences. The decision threshold for all experiments is selected on the validation set to maximize the F-score—this is exactly the "greedy threshold selection" process that the paper scrutinizes. Each configuration undergoes 5 independent trials, and the 95% confidence interval is reported. The paper does not report runtime, real-time factor, or computational overhead metrics.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

There are two baselines: an HFCC feature template + the exact same DTW backend as the embedding system (40 ms window, 10 ms frame shift), and the uncalibrated embedding system itself (i.e., the TACos system from Reference [31], representing the current SOTA process on this dataset). Table 1 gives the F-scores of the three systems on the validation/test sets across 15 SNRs. It can be interpreted from four levels:

- **Quality of the representation itself (Validation Set Column)**: Validation set thresholds basically match the data conditions. At this point, the average F-score for embedding templates is 46.1 (uncalibrated) / 45.1 (calibrated), significantly exceeding HFCC's 25.2 (Table 1 average row). This shows that the representation quality of learned embeddings is indeed far superior to hand-crafted features, consistent with the conclusions of References [29,31]. On the test set at high SNR (27/30 dB), embeddings are also significantly better (e.g., at 27 dB: 61.6 vs. 44.7).
- **Destructive power of threshold mismatch (Test Set Column)**: Without calibration, the embedding system's test average is only 27.9, leading HFCC's 25.3 by only 2.6 percentage points—the 20 percentage point representation advantage on the validation set is almost swallowed by threshold mismatch. The most striking is at 6 dB: uncalibrated embedding 18.7±11.6, which is actually lower than HFCC's 26.8; at 3 dB it is only 13.1±11.1. Moreover, the confidence intervals are extremely wide (±11), indicating that performance fluctuates drastically across trials.
- **Repair effect of calibration**: After adding the proposed calibration, the test average rises to 33.4 (an increase of +5.5 over uncalibrated, +8.1 over HFCC), and it surpasses or matches HFCC under all noise conditions (6 dB: 30.0±4.6; 3 dB: 27.0±7.2, nearly doubling compared to uncalibrated); the variance is also significantly narrowed (3 dB from ±11.1 to ±7.2, 6 dB from ±11.6 to ±4.6), changing the system behavior from "luck-based" to "stable." Calibration only incurs marginal losses under matched conditions (validation set) and high SNR: validation set average drops from 46.1 to 45.1, 27 dB test from 61.6 to 60.7, 30 dB test from 62.3 to 61.2 (Table 1)—exchanging a cost of less than 1 percentage point under clean conditions for a gain of more than 5 percentage points under noisy conditions and halving the variance.
- **Extreme cases**: At -12 dB, all methods collapse (embedding method F-score 0.4, HFCC only 1.9, Table 1), indicating that this calibration cannot save extreme SNRs; it fixes the threshold transfer problem, not the upper limit of the representation's noise robustness.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper conducts two sets of mechanism verification experiments, which are the most solid part of the evidence chain in the entire paper.

**Ablation 1: Contribution of each step (Table 2)**. Using quantization alone (step 1), normalization alone (step 2), and both steps combined, the average F-scores on the validation/test sets are: Validation set 40.4 / 47.0 / 45.1, Test set 31.3 / 29.1 / 33.4 (Table 2 average row). Three findings:

- **Quantization focuses on noise robustness**: On the test set (where threshold mismatch exists), quantization alone (31.3) is significantly stronger than normalization alone (29.1), and the gap is largest in noisy segments—6 dB test 29.9±5.1 vs. 18.3±12.4, 3 dB test 26.6±7.7 vs. 13.0±9.8. Quantization stabilizes the score distribution, making thresholds estimated on the validation set still effective under test conditions.
- **Normalization focuses on threshold alignment**: Under validation set (47.0) and high SNR (greater than 24 dB; 30 dB test 64.0 vs. 55.5, 24 dB test 53.4 vs. 48.8) conditions, normalization alone is clearly stronger. But as mentioned before, because the model is discriminatively trained and cross-class scores are already relatively aligned, the net increment of normalization relative to no calibration (test average 27.9, validation average 46.1) is limited.
- **Complementarity of the two steps**: On the test average, combining both (33.4) is better than any single step (31.3, 29.1), proving that the two steps solve different problems. An interesting detail is: on the validation set, normalization alone (47.0) is actually higher than combined (45.1)—under conditions where thresholds match the data, quantization has a slight precision loss, and the advantage of combining is fully reflected in mismatched scenarios; this suggests that in practice, if condition matching can be ensured, using only normalization is sufficient, but real-world scenarios cannot guarantee matching.

**Ablation 2: Direct evaluation of threshold quality (Fig. 3)**. This is the key experiment answering "whether the performance gap is indeed caused by thresholds": comparing the difference between the threshold estimated on the validation set $\theta_{est}$ and the oracle optimal threshold on the test set $\theta_{opt}$ (left plot), and the performance difference $F(\theta_{opt}) - F(\theta_{est})$ brought by the two thresholds (right plot), comparing calibrated and uncalibrated cases. Conclusion: Without calibration, the deviation between the estimated threshold and the optimal threshold is not large itself (left plot y-axis magnitude is about ±0.1), but this small deviation brings a performance loss of nearly 25 percentage points (right plot)—the system is highly sensitive to thresholds, making it difficult to estimate accurate thresholds; after calibration, the magnitude of threshold deviation is similar or even slightly smaller, but the performance difference remains consistently small—the system becomes insensitive to thresholds, making it easier to estimate a "good enough" threshold. This experimental design cleanly separates the two confounding variables of "representation quality" and "threshold quality," advancing the core argument of this paper from correlation to causality: the main cause of the collapse of embedding systems under noise is suboptimal thresholds, and calibration fixes this by making scores insensitive to thresholds.

## Main Contributions

1. **Reveals and quantitatively confirms an overlooked failure mode**: The performance of representation-based KWS in noisy environments strongly depends on the quality of decision threshold estimation; greedy validation set threshold selection is highly suboptimal under condition mismatch, and can even cause strong representations to lose to hand-crafted features (6 dB: 18.7 vs. 26.8, Table 1; Fig. 3's oracle control experiment provides causal evidence).
2. **Proposes a training-free, embedding-level, two-step separable score calibration method**: Reuses the center codebook naturally produced by TACos training, first quantizes embeddings (Equation (3)), then normalizes quantization error (Equation (4)), and combines them by directly adding inner products (Equation (5)), without modifying the DTW backend at all, requiring no additional data or training.
3. **Validates effectiveness on few-shot open-set KWS with HF simulated channels**: The test average F-score increases from 27.9 to 33.4, with general improvement under noise conditions and significantly narrowed trial variance, at the cost of a marginal loss of less than 1 percentage point under matched conditions (Table 1); and stepwise ablation proves that quantization contributes noise robustness, normalization contributes threshold alignment, and the two are complementary (Table 2).
4. **Methodological transfer value**: Introduces the idea of local density score normalization from the anomaly detection field (Reference [34]) and the experience that "quantization is regularization/enhancement" (References [20–22]) into the KWS threshold estimation problem, pointing out a second use for quantization in KWS besides compression, filling the gap left by References [23–26].

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Threshold gap is not completely eliminated**: As stated in Section 5 of the paper, there is still a performance gap between the estimated threshold and the oracle threshold; calibration only makes the system insensitive to thresholds, significantly narrowing but not eliminating this gap.
- **Dependence on codebook quality**: The entire calibration relies on the center codebook learned during training, which was learned on a few-shot training set of only 39 seconds, and naturally has limited coverage of noise conditions; quantization-aware training under noise simulation was not performed during training (one of the improvement directions proposed by the paper), which requires clean training samples—usually unavailable in practice, forming a realistic constraint.
- **Additional computation and storage not reported**: The quantization step requires performing $\arg\max$ on all $N_{kw} \times N_{pos} \times N_C$ centers for each frame's embedding, and the normalization step requires the same maximum inner product, bringing additional computation and storage proportional to the codebook size; the paper does not report the specific numbers for this overhead, nor does it report the specific value of the number of position classes $N_{pos}$ (only reporting the number of centers per class $N_C = 16$).
- **Numerical boundary cases not discussed**: The denominator $1 + \max \langle \phi, c \rangle$ in Equation (4) approaches zero or becomes negative when the embedding is close to orthogonal or anti-parallel to all centers, at which point normalization will diverge or change sign; the paper does not discuss this boundary or any protective measures.
- **Small loss under clean matched conditions**: Calibration loses about 1 percentage point on the validation set average (46.1 vs. 45.1, Table 1), and slightly drops on 27/30 dB tests; this is not a free lunch, but the paper argues that in the target scenario (noise, mismatch), the benefits far outweigh the costs.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Single noise type**: All conclusions are built on a single simulated noise of Watterson HF channel + AWGN; there is no verification with real recordings, reverberation, other channel models, or environmental noise. The paper itself admits in Section 5 that more experiments under other noise conditions are needed to strengthen the conclusions.
- **Lack of horizontal comparison with similar calibration methods**: The introduction cites score calibration/normalization methods such as keyword-specific thresholds, sum-to-one normalization (Reference [10]), and cohort normalization (Reference [11]), but none of them are used as comparison baselines in the experiments—readers cannot judge how much net gain this paper's method has over these simpler score-level methods (although the introduction argues from principles the difficulties of these methods in open-set on/offset scenarios).
- **Estimating thresholds per SNR condition does not fit reality**: The paper explicitly admits in Section 5 that in the experiments, each SNR version estimates the threshold separately, while in real scenarios, the signal SNR prior is unknown and difficult to estimate; the setting of "one global threshold working well under all noise conditions" is not experimented with, and the performance under a single threshold is not reported.
- **Annotation cost of threshold estimation itself is ignored**: Although keyword samples are few-shot (5 per class), threshold estimation relies on a validation set of about 10 minutes, containing 156 sentences and per-keyword annotations; in the narrative of true few-shot deployment, how to compress this annotation overhead and how large the validation set needs to be are not discussed by the paper.
- **Limited statistical power**: The 95% confidence intervals from 5 trials are still quite wide under some conditions (e.g., 0 dB test 15.8±6.3, Table 1), and differences between some conditions are not cleanly separated by the confidence intervals.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

- **Noise simulation + quantization-aware training during training**: The preferred direction proposed by the paper—simulate different noise conditions and execute quantization during the embedding model training phase, expected to further narrow the gap between estimated and optimal thresholds; the cost is the need for clean training samples (usually unavailable in practice, as admitted by the paper).
- **Threshold estimation without validation sets**: The ideal goal proposed by the paper—saving even the step of optimizing performance on a validation set to estimate thresholds, for example, making the score distribution after calibration naturally adapt to a fixed threshold; and designing a single threshold that is robust under all unknown noise conditions.
- **Extended verification**: The paper admits the need to supplement experiments under other noise conditions, other datasets, and other embedding-based KWS systems to strengthen conclusions.
- **Candidate directions supplemented by the author** (not mentioned in the paper): Controlled comparison with score-level calibration baselines (keyword-specific thresholding, sum-to-one, cohort normalization); ablation on codebook size ($N_C$, $N_{pos}$) and quantization granularity; stacking quantization calibration with model weight quantization [23–26] to simultaneously obtain compression and calibration; studying numerical stability protection for the denominator in Equation (4); generalizing this method to closed-set KWS that does not require on/offset to check if the gain still exists.

---

**One-sentence summary**: The true contribution of this paper is not some complex new network, but a problem skipped by most KWS works—"if the threshold is estimated incorrectly, even the best representation is useless"—and an extremely lightweight answer: pull noise-perturbed embeddings back to the codebook centers learned during training (quantization), then scale them by the "degree of explanation by the codebook" (normalization), so that DTW scores become insensitive to thresholds; on few-shot open-set KWS with HF simulated noise, the test average F-score increases from 27.9 to 33.3 (Table 1), variance is significantly narrowed, and the cost is less than 1 percentage point under clean conditions.
