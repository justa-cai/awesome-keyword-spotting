## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as a persistent entry point for human-machine voice interfaces: the system continuously listens and activates immediately upon detecting a predefined wake-word. With the rapid proliferation of voice devices, KWS has extended into unmanned scenarios such as the Internet of Things (IoT) and security surveillance [1][2]. In these contexts, Multi-channel Intelligent Surveillance Systems integrate multiple audio channels into a single real-time monitoring framework, requiring KWS to process large-scale audio data simultaneously, quickly, and accurately under various acquisition conditions [3]–[5]. However, most existing KWS technologies are designed for single-channel input. They are largely ineffective when facing the massive data volume, noise adaptation requirements, and real-time (latency) constraints imposed by multi-channel inputs. This represents a structural gap that this work aims to address.

In edge environments, low power consumption and high energy efficiency are rigid design goals [6]–[9], with no room for compromise. A typical KWS pipeline consists of two parts: a frontend (Feature Extraction, FE) and a backend (classifier). Lightweight classifiers such as DS-CNN [10] and TC-ResNet8 [11] can only partially mitigate performance degradation in multi-channel scenarios. The frontend, long recognized as the primary bottleneck in single-channel KWS [12], faces particularly severe computational pressure in multi-channel settings. The paper makes a crucial engineering judgment: rather than increasing classifier complexity to withstand noise, it is far more cost-effective for energy-constrained edge devices to handle various types of noise at the signal acquisition stage (i.e., the frontend). Therefore, how to efficiently perform feature extraction for multi-channel audio has become the key bottleneck for deploying multi-channel KWS.

Fig. 1 clearly illustrates this contradiction: a single-channel IIR-FBank frontend requires completing feature extraction for only 1 channel within one $T_{audio}$ time window (throughput requirement of $1/T_{audio}$); an N-channel system must complete N channels within the same $T_{audio}$ (throughput requirement of $N/T_{audio}$). The computational demand scales linearly with the number of channels, while the real-time budget remains unchanged. The paper even describes this pressure as "exponential growth in computational burden with the number of channels"—mechanistically, the load per channel is linearly additive, but digesting this sum within the original budget is equivalent to a sharp increase in requirements for hardware parallelism and energy efficiency.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper compares existing FE approaches in Table I, revealing fatal flaws in four main routes:

1.  **Analog Feature Extraction**: Analog schemes such as ring-oscillator Band-Pass Filters (BPF) [13] and transconductance-capacitor ($g_m$-C) BPF [14] typically achieve accuracies around 90% or lower. They are highly sensitive to noise and cannot handle multi-channel KWS under complex noise conditions.
2.  **FFT-based MFCC Digital Schemes**: Digital approaches offer higher accuracy than analog ones. The mainstream method involves FFT-based time-frequency transformation followed by MFCC feature extraction. However, the overhead of FFT on edge processors is high [12][15]–[17]. In multi-channel scenarios, this overhead must be paid repeatedly for each channel.
3.  **Streaming IIR Filter Bank (streaming IIR-FBank) [18]**: This approach uses a set of IIR BPFs to fit Mel-frequency bands directly in the time domain, bypassing FFT. It has low hardware computational cost and achieves 96.49% accuracy, comparable to MFCC. However, the recursive nature of IIR filters (current output depends on historical output, see Eq. (1)) dictates that they can process only one input stream at a time (Fig. 1(a)). Frame-level parallelism is impossible, and directly applying this to multi-channel systems would overwhelm the real-time budget.
4.  **Segmented IIR Scheme [19]**: This method cuts the input into frames (Fig. 3(b)) but does not overlap them. Phoneme transition information is lost at frame boundaries, causing accuracy to drop to approximately 92.5% (Table I, a drop of about 4 percentage points relative to the streaming scheme). Furthermore, it does not achieve parallel frame processing.

Theoretically, there is a fifth route—fully overlapped framing (Fig. 3(c)), common in MFCC pipelines: frame overlap preserves phoneme transition clues, but the overlapping regions undergo redundant filtering, wasting computational power. Moreover, this scheme has not been widely used in IIR routes. The conclusion row in Table I is clear: MFCC has 96.1% accuracy but is bottlenecked by FFT and lacks parallelism; streaming IIR [18] has 96.49% accuracy but lacks parallelism; segmented IIR [19] has only 92.5% accuracy and lacks parallelism. No route simultaneously achieves "high accuracy + low overhead + parallelizability."

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

The paper converges the problem into two objectives: (1) reduce the amount of data requiring filtering; (2) utilize parallel filtering to shorten execution time and improve energy efficiency. The constraint is that accuracy loss must be kept within an industry-acceptable range—the paper cites a threshold of approximately 1.4% accuracy loss, referencing [21][22] (KASP processor from ISSCC 2024, Spiking-HDC from ISCAS 2024) as the "passing line." The final implementation requirement is that the design cannot remain at the algorithm simulation stage; it must be validated on a complete hardware stack including full RTL, FPGA prototypes, and 45 nm synthesis, verifying the latency-energy-accuracy triangle. Additionally, it must provide a parameterizable hardware configuration method that can be scaled according to the number of channels and power budget.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall pipeline of ASAP-FE (Agile Sparsity-Aware Parallelized-Feature Extractor) is shown in Fig. 2: Raw Audio → Pre-emphasis → Half-overlapped IIR Framing → IIR Filter Bank (incorporating Sparsity-aware Data Reduction: Frame Skipping + Variable-Stride Filtering) → $\log_2$ → Classifier; alongside this runs a Dynamic Parallel Processing scheduling path (Priority Scheduling Algorithm + Parameterizable Filter Clusters).

The foundation of this design lies in a key observation (Fig. 2 and Introduction): In IIR filtering FE, except for the $\log_2$ transformation which aligns with human auditory characteristics, almost all other operations—pre-emphasis, IIR stride-1/stride-2 bandpass filtering, and low-pass filtering before stride-2—are repeated combinations of the same filtering structure. This means that in hardware, filters can be made into replicable module arrays, allowing multiple filter modules to execute the same operations simultaneously, significantly boosting throughput. This is the structural prerequisite for why "parallelization" is feasible, and explains why $\log_2$ is implemented as a separate LUT operator rather than participating in parallel scheduling—it is not a filtering structure, and replicating it is meaningless.

The logic of combining these three technologies forms a clear subtraction chain: First, half-overlapped framing cuts approximately 25% of redundant filtering (preserving phoneme transition clues); second, sparsity-aware data reduction cuts approximately 50% (skipping noise-dominated frames + downsampling secondary frames). Together, these two steps reduce the workload by 62.73%. With less data, the number of filter modules required for parallelism and the overall energy consumption decrease synchronously. Finally, dynamic parallel processing squeezes the latency into the multi-channel real-time budget. These three are not three并列 techniques, but a causal chain of "reduce data → fewer modules → lower energy consumption." The paper explicitly states that relying solely on 62.73% data reduction is still insufficient to support real-time KWS for more than three channels; parallelization is the necessary second wheel.

The hardware architecture (Fig. 9) consists of four parts: Scratchpad Memory (SPM) handles storage for raw waveforms, pre-emphasis waveforms, and final features; the Filter Cluster uses integer input/output and fixed-point arithmetic internally (fractional bit width parameterized by $\alpha$ frac. bits) to avoid floating-point overhead. The filters are 4th-order IIR (annotated in Fig. 9), with a five-stage pipeline supporting high-throughput processing for pre-emphasis, stride-1/stride-2 bandpass filtering, and low-pass filtering; a Sparsity-aware Stride Calculator is parallelized with the pre-emphasis stage, calculating the stride pattern frame-by-frame for the filter scheduler; the Filter Scheduler generates tasks (stride value + frame number) based on stride values, inserts them into a priority queue, and dispatches them in parallel to idle filter modules; after filtering, frame energy passes through a lookup-table-based $\log_2$ operator to output the final features.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**IIR Bandpass Filtering (Eq. 1)**:

$$y[n] = -\sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k\, y[n-k] + \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k\, x[n-k]$$

where $x[n]$ and $y[n]$ are the input and output of the filter at discrete time $n$, $a_k$ and $b_k$ are the feedback and feedforward coefficients, respectively, and $N$ and $M$ are the orders of the feedback and feedforward paths. IIR-FBank uses a set of IIR BPFs to cover the Mel-frequency band densely at low frequencies and sparsely at high frequencies, simulating human auditory perception. In action: the current output must be calculated together with historical outputs—this is precisely why IIR filters have low order and low computational cost, but are naturally serial and difficult to parallelize.

**Short-Time Energy (Eq. 2)**: $STE = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]^2$, the sum of squares of samples within a frame, measuring the energy level of that frame, serving as the criterion for frame skipping.

**Zero-Crossing Count (Eq. 3)**: $ZCC = \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} [\text{sign}(x[n]) \cdot \text{sign}(x[n-1]) < 0]$, counting the number of sign flips between adjacent samples. In speech, the zero-crossing rate of voiceless fricatives (e.g., /s/) and high-frequency noise is significantly higher than that of voiced sounds. It serves as a cheap proxy indicator for intra-frame high-frequency activity.

**Amplitude Difference (Eq. 4)**: $Adiff = \max(x[n]) - \min(x[n])$, for $n \in \text{frame}$, the difference between the maximum and minimum values of samples within a frame, reflecting the severity of amplitude fluctuations.

**Frame Importance Score (Eq. 5)**: $S_{frame} = \alpha \cdot ZCC + \beta \cdot Adiff$, with empirical values $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta = 0.5$. Neither metric requires multiplication or division, making them virtually free in hardware. This is the practical reason for selecting them for importance scoring.

**Two Thresholds**: The frame skipping threshold $th_1 = 0.5^n \cdot \max(STE)$. For each audio input, the maximum STE is normalized, and $n \in \{1, \dots, 10\}$ is scanned. The optimal point is at $th_1 = 0.56 \cdot \max(STE)$; the variable stride threshold $th_2 \in \{0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.875\} \cdot \max(S_{frame})$, with the optimal point at $th_2 = 0.5 \cdot \max(S_{frame})$. Frames with $S_{frame} < th_2$ undergo stride-2 filtering, while those $\ge th_2$ undergo stride-1 filtering.

**Two Sub-problems for Parallelization (P1/P2)**:

P1 (Find minimum module count): $latency(m_{min}) \le T_{audio}/N$, and $m_{min} \le m_{max}$. $latency(m)$ is the time required for FE-specific hardware with $m$ filter modules to complete one feature extraction pass, and $m_{max}$ is the maximum number of modules allowed by the power budget.

P2 (Find the energy-optimal point): $m_{opt} = \arg\min_{m \in [m_{min}, m_{max}]} energy(m)$. $energy(m)$ is the energy consumed by running the FE with $m$ modules, assuming the accelerator can be power-gated during the idle interval $T_{audio}/N - latency(m)$, thereby reducing total system energy consumption.

**Mechanism of Energy Convexity**: Latency decreases linearly with $m$ (spreading fixed workload across more modules, with diminishing marginal returns), while power consumption rises nearly linearly with the number of active modules. The product of the two (Energy $\approx$ Power $\times$ Latency) must therefore decrease first and then increase, existing at a minimum point—this provides theoretical guarantee for the local search in Algorithm 1, which is also confirmed by experimental results (Fig. 11(b)).

**Multiplicative Decomposition of Data Reduction**: Half-overlapped framing reduces by 24.9% → remaining 75.1%; Frame skipping further reduces by 34.42% → remaining approx. 49.25%; stride-2 further reduces by 24.2% → remaining approx. 37.3%. The total workload after multiplying these three factors is approximately 37.3% of the original, i.e., a total reduction of 62.7%, consistent with the 62.73% repeatedly cited in the abstract and experimental sections—this number is the compound interest of three independent mechanisms, not a single-point optimization.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Half-overlapped IIR Framing

**Why it is needed**: Streaming IIR can only consume one stream at a time, making frame-level parallelism impossible; to parallelize, framing is required. However, existing segmented schemes [19] do not overlap frames, causing phoneme transition information to be lost at boundaries, dropping accuracy by about 4 points; fully overlapped schemes preserve information but waste computing power on redundant filtering in overlapping regions. This is a dilemma between "information integrity" and "computational redundancy."

**How it works**: The half-overlapped scheme in Fig. 3(d) keeps adjacent frames overlapping to preserve speech clues in the time dimension—phoneme transition is a discriminative feature for distinguishing similar keywords—while halving the overlap amount to avoid excessive redundancy. Compared to fully overlapped framing, the total computational load is reduced by about 25%, while the fidelity of phoneme transitions remains sufficient to support accuracy.

**Evidence**: Fig. 4 scans four framing methods under 5–40 filter banks on GSCD using a fixed TC-ResNet8 (PyTorch, Adam, cross-entropy loss, cosine annealing scheduler, hyperparameters kept constant throughout to isolate model differences from framing comparisons). The baseline of 40 MFCC filter banks achieves 96.1% accuracy [11]; the segmented method drops by more than 3 points; the half-overlapped method catches up to or exceeds the baseline when the number of filter banks exceeds 20. Table I summarizes: MFCC 96.1%, Streaming [18] 96.49%, Segmented [19] 92.5%, ASAP-FE 96.48%, and ASAP-FE is the only scheme among them marked as Parallel-Processing Feasible. Frame length is 16 ms; specific step/overlap millisecond parameters for half-overlap are not reported in the paper and must be reverse-engineered for reproduction.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Sparsity-aware Data Reduction

**Why**: Although audio inputs cover low and high frequencies, a large number of frames are either drowned out by noise or contain no effective signal, contributing very little to KWS [28]. Eliminating or downgrading these frames saves computing power for frames with actual information content—essentially an engineering utilization of the prior knowledge that "the discriminative value distribution of audio frames is uneven."

**Level 1: Frame Skipping**. Calculate STE frame-by-frame; frames below $th_1$ are directly discarded. Frames with very low STE are basically dominated by noise and contribute almost nothing to KWS. Fig. 5 scans $th_1$ ($0.5^n \cdot \max(STE)$, $n = 1 \dots 10$). Under the constraint of allowing a maximum 0.3% accuracy loss relative to the no-skipping baseline, three models (TC-ResNet8, DS-CNN, KWT-1, all equipped with half-overlapped framing + 40 Mel filter banks) achieve the optimal balance at $th_1 = 0.56 \cdot \max(STE)$, skipping an average of 34.42% of frames, with accuracy loss controlled within 0.3%.

**Level 2: Variable-Stride Filtering**. For the remaining frames, categorize them further: use $S_{frame}$ (weighted sum of ZCC and amplitude difference) from Eq. (5) to measure a frame's importance to KWS. Frames below $th_2$ undergo stride-2 filtering (using only even-indexed samples, effectively halving the sampling rate and data volume), while those not lower than $th_2$ undergo stride-1 filtering (fine processing of all samples). There are two necessary signal processing remedies here, both indispensable:

First, **Anti-aliasing**. Stride-2 downsampling halves the Nyquist frequency. To avoid spectral aliasing, stride-2 frames must use the original waveform after low-pass filtering; while stride-1 frames use the regular pre-emphasis waveform. The preprocessing paths for the two types of frames are different, which is why LPF and pre-emphasis exist as two separate paths in the hardware.

Second, **STE Amplitude Compensation**. Halving the number of samples causes the STE of stride-2 frames to be systematically lower (the summation term is halved). A compensation factor derived from adjacent stride-1 frames must be used to pull the STE of stride-2 frames back to the amplitude scale of stride-1. Fig. 6 shows that after compensation, the STE curve of sparse filtering realigns with the stride-1 reference curve—ensuring feature scales do not drift, so the backend model does not see a systematic shift in feature distribution due to downgrading.

Fig. 7 scans five levels of $th_2$ (0.125–0.875 times $\max(S_{frame})$). Under the constraint of accuracy loss $\le 0.5\%$, the optimal $th_2 = 0.5 \cdot \max(S_{frame})$, reducing intra-frame data by another 24.2%. Two levels combined: Frame skipping 34.4% + Variable stride 24.2%, resulting in a total reduction of 50.3% in data volume by sparsity-aware data reduction.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Dynamic Parallel Processing (DPP)

**Why not blindly stack modules**: Edge processors have limited resources and power budgets; the number of filter modules $m$ cannot be increased indefinitely. Increasing $m$ generally shortens latency, but resource occupation and power consumption grow approximately linearly; spreading a fixed workload across more modules leads to diminishing marginal latency gains per added module. Experiments confirm that the energy curve is convex (decreasing first, then increasing), with a minimum point. The task of DPP is to find these two inflection points.

**Algorithm 1 (DPP Framework)** contains two functions: `Find_m_MIN` linearly searches from $i = 1$ to $m_{max}$, finding the first $i$ that satisfies $latency(i) \le T_{audio}/N$ as $m_{min}$ (solving P1); `Find_m_OPT` starts from $m_{min} + 1$, sequentially comparing $energy(j)$ with $energy(j-1)$. Once energy consumption rises, $j-1$ is defined as $m_{opt}$ (solving P2, utilizing convexity for local search). The key point is that $latency(m)$ and $energy(m)$ are not analytical models, but are obtained by measuring on a prototype processor after implementing the scheduler and filter cluster in hardware—this ensures configuration decisions are based on real hardware behavior rather than paper estimates.

**Algorithm 2 (Filter Scheduling Algorithm)** core is "priority round-robin" dispatching, aiming to ensure no module is idle and no single module becomes a bottleneck at any time, thereby achieving minimum latency and corresponding lowest energy consumption for each $m$. Scheduling applies only to the two core operations of IIR stride-1 and stride-2 bandpass filtering—pre-emphasis runs exactly once per frame, and low-pass filtering is preemptively called only before stride-2 processing; neither requires explicit priority. Priority rules (Fig. 8):

- Frames with stride = 0 have been skipped and do not enter the queue;
- stride = 1 and adjacent frames contain stride = 2 frames → Priority 1 (Highest). Reason: The STE compensation factor must be derived from adjacent stride-1 frames, so these frames must complete both stride-1 and stride-2 operations within the same frame, and must complete stride-1 before the corresponding stride-2 frame, otherwise the compensation chain breaks;
- stride = 2 frames → Priority 2;
- Isolated stride = 1 frames (no stride-2 neighbors on either side, no need to participate in compensation) → Priority 3, can be processed independently.

The three priority stacks form a single Priority_Queue, and the SCHEDULING loop takes the highest priority task and dispatches it to an idle filter module. Fig. 8 gives a complete example of 12 frames: STRIDES = [0,0,0,1,1,2,2,2,1,1,0,0]. Frames 4 and 8 are split into groups (2,[4])(1,[4]) and (2,[8])(1,[8]) due to adjacency with stride-2 regions and enter Priority 1; frames 5/6/7 enter Priority 2; frames 3/9 enter Priority 3—the scheduling design directly serves the dependency relationships of feature compensation, rather than generic load balancing.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Compared to existing routes in Table I, the difference of ASAP-FE is structural: MFCC routes have high accuracy but are locked by FFT power consumption; streaming IIR [18] eliminates FFT but is locked by recursiveness preventing parallelism; segmented IIR [19] performs framing but loses overlap information. ASAP-FE integrates "half-overlapped framing + two-level sparsity-aware reduction + parameterizable parallel filter clusters" into a complete processor RTL: the framing method is designed specifically for parallelism (the only one in Table I marked Parallel-Processing = Feasible), data reduction acts on two granularities of frame-level and sample-level, and it provides a two-stage solution framework for $m$ (feasibility first, then energy efficiency) rather than a fixed configuration.

It also differs from model compression routes such as quantization/binarization/pruning in orientation: those methods compress the computation of the backend classifier, while ASAP-FE compresses the computation of the frontend feature extraction. In multi-channel monitoring scenarios, the frontend is the throughput bottleneck, and handling noise at the acquisition stage is more power-saving than strengthening the classifier (a clear assertion in the paper's introduction). The two routes act on orthogonal objects and can theoretically be used in combination.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

All experiments use the Google Speech Commands Dataset (GSCD): 65,000 audio clips of 1-second duration sampled at 16 kHz, covering 10 target keywords (yes, no, up, down, left, right, on, off, stop, go) plus silence and unknown, totaling 12 classes; random noise augmentation is performed using the GSCD noise set; training/validation/testing splits are 8:1:1. Framing length is 16 ms; feature extraction uses a Mel-scale filter bank aligned with human perception (threshold experiments fixed at 40, Fig. 4 scans 5–40). Three backend models are used: TC-ResNet8 (Temporal Convolution route), DS-CNN (Depthwise Separable CNN route), and KWT-1 (Self-Attention Transformer route [20]), covering three mainstream lightweight architectures to ensure conclusions are not bound to a single model family. Training uses PyTorch + Adam + cross-entropy loss + cosine annealing scheduler.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Accuracy (%)**: 12-class classification accuracy, used to fix the lower bound of "saving cannot be excessive";
- **Latency (ms)**: Completion time for a single FE pass. The baseline is the measured single-channel fully overlapped IIR filtering at 32 ms/pass, based on which $T_{audio} = 32$ ms is set; N-channel real-time is equivalent to $latency(m) \le T_{audio}/N$, which is the conversion bridge translating "number of channels" into "latency budget";
- **Energy (nJ)**: Power simulation after 45 nm synthesis, combined with the "power-gating during idle periods" assumption to calculate single-pass energy consumption;
- **Remaining Frames Ratio (remaining frames, %)**: The proportion of frames remaining after skipping/downgrading, directly measuring the intensity of data reduction, serving as an intermediate metric connecting algorithmic decisions with hardware load;
- **Resource Occupation (LUT/FF/mW)**: FPGA tool and logic synthesis reports, measuring hardware cost.

Rationale for selection: The constraints of multi-channel edge KWS are the triangle of Accuracy, Speed, and Savings. Data reduction techniques must additionally prove "how much was actually cut." Each metric acts as a hard constraint for the others (accuracy loss of approx. 1.4% is the passing line, real-time budget is a hard line), avoiding self-indulgence in single metrics.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

**Accuracy (Table III)**: On the fully overlapped IIR filtering baseline, TC-ResNet8 / DS-CNN / KWT-1 achieve 96.35% / 97.13% / 97.28%; ASAP-FE achieves 95.43% / 96.22% / 96.48%. The drops are 0.92 / 0.91 / 0.80 percentage points respectively, all below 1%, and also below the approx. 1.4% acceptable threshold given in [21][22].

**Latency (Fig. 11(a))**: The baseline single-channel fully overlapped FE takes 32 ms per pass. ASAP-FE, relying on 62.73% data reduction, reduces latency to 11.97 ms with a single filter module ($m = 1$); $m = 2 \rightarrow 6.21$ ms; $m = 4 \rightarrow 3.35$ ms; $m = 8 \rightarrow 1.92$ ms; $m = 23 \rightarrow 0.99$ ms; $m = 30 \rightarrow 0.88$ ms. Converting according to $latency \le T_{audio}/N$: after $m \ge 23$, the system can support up to 32 channels in real-time within the same $T_{audio}$; the minimum configuration correspondence for each channel count is 2 channels $m=1$, 4 channels $m=2$, 8 channels $m=4$, 16 channels $m=8$, 32 channels $m=23$ (annotated in Fig. 11(a)).

**Energy (Fig. 11(b))**: Energy decreases first and then increases with $m$, consistent with the convexity prediction of DPP. If the system needs to support more than 25 channels, $m_{min}$ itself is the optimal choice ($m_{opt} = m_{min}$). The paper does not provide a direct energy-dimensional comparison with MFCC hardware accelerators.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

1.  **Framing Method Ablation (Fig. 4)**: Fixed TC-ResNet8, scanned 5–40 filter bank counts, compared four framing methods under identical conditions. The segmented method consistently dropped by more than 3 points (the direct cost of losing phoneme transition information); the half-overlapped method caught up to or exceeded the 96.1% baseline when the number of filter banks > 20. This indicates that half-overlap is the sweet spot between "preserving information" and "reducing redundancy," and this conclusion is insensitive to the number of filter banks (after exceeding 20).
2.  **Frame Skipping Threshold Ablation (Fig. 5)**: The stricter $th_1$ (larger) cuts more frames and saves more computing power, but increases accuracy risk. Under the 0.3% accuracy loss constraint, all three models consistently converged to $th_1 = 0.56 \cdot \max(STE)$, skipping an average of 34.42% of frames—three backends with different architectures yielding the same optimal point indicates that this threshold is robust to model selection.
3.  **Variable Stride Threshold Ablation (Fig. 7)**: As $th_2$ rises from 0.125 to 0.875 times $\max(S_{frame})$, more frames are downgraded to stride-2, the number of remaining frames decreases, and accuracy slowly drops. Under the 0.5% accuracy loss constraint, the optimal $th_2 = 0.5 \cdot \max(S_{frame})$, reducing intra-frame data by another 24.2%. Note that the two levels of thresholds use different accuracy budgets (0.3% vs 0.5%); the paper does not explain why different quotas were allocated.
4.  **STE Compensation Ablation (Fig. 6)**: Compared sparse filtering with stride-1/stride-2 short-time energy trajectories on a single audio input: without compensation, stride-2 frame STE is systematically lower than the stride-1 reference; after compensating with factors derived from adjacent stride-1 frames, the curves realign—this is the key patch to prevent feature scale drift after downsampling, and also the reason for the existence of Priority 1 in the scheduler.
5.  **Filter Module Count Ablation (Fig. 11)**: Scanned $m$ from 1 to 30. Latency decreases sub-linearly (11.97 → 0.88 ms), energy is convex, decreasing first then increasing (minimum point $m = 15$, 30.4 nJ). This ablation simultaneously validates the practical significance of the two functions in Algorithm 1: $m_{min}$ determines the upper limit of channel count, $m_{opt}$ determines the energy-efficiency sweet spot.
6.  **Combined Effect Accounting**: Half-overlap 24.9% + Frame skipping 34.4% + stride-2 24.2% multiplied together, total workload drops to approx. 37.3%, i.e., an average reduction of 62.73% (consistent across Abstract, Introduction, and Section V-B).

## Main Contributions

1.  **Half-overlapped IIR Framing**: An IIR framing scheme redesigned for parallelism, reducing redundant filtering by approx. 25% compared to fully overlapped while preserving phoneme transition clues, resolving the dilemma of "segmented losing information, fully overlapped wasting computing power" (Table I, Fig. 3, Fig. 4).
2.  **Sparsity-aware Data Reduction**: Two-level granularity data cropping via Frame Skipping (STE threshold) + Variable-Stride Filtering (ZCC/Amplitude Difference score threshold), accompanied by two necessary patches: anti-aliasing low-pass and STE amplitude compensation, cutting another approx. 50% of data volume, with optimal points for both levels consistent across three backend models (Fig. 5–7).
3.  **Dynamic Parallel Processing**: Formalizing multi-channel FE parallelization into two sub-problems P1 (minimum module count) and P2 (energy-optimal module count), providing a two-stage solution framework in Algorithm 1 and Algorithm 2 priority scheduling (including Priority 1 design driven by STE compensation dependencies), and replacing analytical models with hardware-measured $latency(m)/energy(m)$ for decision-making.
4.  **Full-stack Hardware Verification**: Verilog RTL implementation of parameterizable filter clusters ($1 \le m \le 30$), integrated into an edge processor with RISC-V Rocket core (50 MHz), 64 KB on-chip SRAM, and low-power µNoC interconnect; FPGA (Kintex Ultrascale+) verification for functionality, 45 nm synthesis for power measurement—a complete evidence chain from algorithm to board-level power is rare in KWS frontend work.
5.  **System-level Conclusions**: Average workload reduced by 62.73%; real-time support for up to 32 channels ($m \ge 23$); energy efficiency optimal for up to 25 channels ($m = 15$, 30.4 nJ, 1.25 ms/pass); accuracy loss for all three backend models is <1% (Table III), below the approx. 1.4% industry-acceptable threshold.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

-   **Thresholds Depend on Segment Maximums**: $th_1$ and $th_2$ are normalized using the max(STE) and max(Sframe) of each audio input itself. Calculating the maximum requires seeing the entire input segment. This is fine for offline 1-second clips, but in true streaming multi-channel monitoring, there is a "chicken and egg" contradiction—the paper does not discuss alternative solutions for online estimation or recursive maximums.
-   **Score Weights are Purely Empirical**: The $\alpha = 1, \beta = 0.5$ in $S_{frame} = \alpha \cdot ZCC + \beta \cdot Adiff$ are empirically set; the paper does not report sensitivity analysis; it is unknown whether these coefficients remain optimal when transferred to far-field, strong noise, or different sampling rate data domains.
-   **Only Classification Accuracy Reported**: The hard metrics for wake-word systems are False Alarm Rate and Miss Detection Rate; the paper only reports 12-class accuracy; the amplification effect of a 0.9 percentage point accuracy loss on the False Alarm Rate is not evaluated, yet monitoring scenarios are extremely sensitive to false alarms.
-   **High-Frequency Damage from stride-2 is Covered by Compensation**: Downsampling halves the Nyquist frequency, and potential damage to high-frequency transient consonants (broadband noise bursts like /s/, /t/) is only countered by STE amplitude compensation; the compensation factor relies on adjacent stride-1 frames, and the boundary calibration quality of continuous large stride-2 regions is not evaluated separately.
-   **Energy Model Contains Assumptions**: $energy(m)$ is based on the assumption that "idle periods can be fully power-gated"; the overhead of gating switching and leakage recovery costs are not included.
-   **Only Addresses Frontend**: In multi-channel scenarios, the backend classifier load is also multiplied by N; ASAP-FE does not touch the backend; end-to-end system-level latency is not reported.
-   **Half-overlap Parameters are Opaque**: Frame length of 16 ms is explicitly reported, but the millisecond values for overlap ratio/step are not given in the paper; third-party reproduction requires reverse-engineering.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

-   **Multi-channel is "Reuse" not "True Array"**: All experiments use GSCD—a single-channel close-talk dataset; the so-called 32 channels involves stuffing 32 independent inputs into the same accelerator for time-division processing. There is no real multi-microphone array recording, no channel-correlated noise, reverberation, beamforming, or other core acoustic issues of monitoring scenarios. The claim of "multi-channel KWS" is not truly tested at the acoustic level, only at the throughput level.
-   **No Positive SOTA Comparison for Energy Caliber**: Table I only compares accuracy dimensions; there is no direct energy comparison with MFCC hardware accelerators (serial-FFT type chips), and the 62.73% reduction is only relative to the IIR frontend baseline itself.
-   **Simulation, Not Tape-out**: Power comes from 45 nm synthesis + power simulation, no chip measurement; the magnitude contradiction between the annotations in Fig. 11(b) and the text's 30.4 nJ is also not clarified.
-   **Averages Mask Worst Cases**: 62.73% is the average workload reduction; for inputs with high speech activity (dense speaking segments), the frame skipping rate is low, and the load is close to the upper limit. Whether the worst case still satisfies $T_{audio}/N$ is not reported, which is exactly the life-and-death line for real-time systems.
-   **Backend Robustness Not Expanded**: The three backends only tested accuracy, without analyzing the differentiated impact of FE output distribution changes (time discontinuity caused by frame skipping, amplitude scale differences caused by stride-2) on different backend structures.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

-   **Online Adaptive Thresholds**: Replace segment max with sliding window maximum or exponential moving average, truly transplanting sparsity-aware reduction into streaming pipelines. This is the first hurdle towards productization.
-   **Frontend-Backend Joint Optimization**: Pass frame-level sparse signals to the classifier for dynamic computation (training with frame-dropping awareness, early-exit, allocating backend computing power according to frame importance), allowing the power savings of FE to propagate through the entire link.
-   **True Arrays and Inter-channel Redundancy Utilization**: Re-calibrate $th_1/th_2$ and $\alpha/\beta$ on real multi-microphone far-field data; further utilize highly correlated frame sharing across multiple channels (joint downgrading of multi-channel same frames), potentially cutting another layer of data volume on top of the existing 62.73%.
-   **More Aggressive Numerical Compression**: The filter cluster already uses fixed-point integer arithmetic; low-bit quantization or approximate multiplication can be added to further compress dynamic power.
-   **Process and Scenario Scalability**: Verify the absolute level of 30.4 nJ by tape-out on more advanced nodes; generalize the configuration method of parameterizable filter clusters (two-stage solution for $m_{min}/m_{opt}$) to audio-intensive scenarios beyond IoT and security (conference pickup, industrial acoustic monitoring, multi-channel voice entry).
