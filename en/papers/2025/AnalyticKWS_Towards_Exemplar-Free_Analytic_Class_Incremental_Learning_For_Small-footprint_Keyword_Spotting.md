## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the core voice interface for edge intelligent devices (robots, autonomous systems, smart speakers, in-car assistants, etc.). It must identify specific keyword phrases within continuous audio streams while satisfying three hard constraints: fast response, low power consumption, and high accuracy. The paper emphasizes two key realistic backgrounds: first, cloud-based solutions are suboptimal in these scenarios—sending users' private voice data to remote servers violates privacy red lines, and the real-time requirement of "reacting immediately upon utterance" often necessitates on-device local adaptation; second, with the surge in voice interaction demands in the era of embodied AI, devices such as smart home hubs and in-car assistants must support increasingly personalized keyword sets—users may want to use "turn on the light" as a wake-word today, but switch to "turn on the desk lamp" tomorrow. The set of labels is continuously evolving, which constitutes the classic setting of Class Incremental Learning (CIL).

Faced with new keywords, there are two traditional engineering approaches: either retrain the model from scratch using full data, which is time-consuming and resource-intensive on edge devices and thus impractical; or adopt few-shot fine-tuning (e.g., Awasthi et al. 2021, Mazumder et al. 2021, Parnami and Lee 2022), adapting the existing model to new words using very few samples. However, the cost of fine-tuning is catastrophic forgetting (McCloskey and Cohen 1989): as the model learns new words, the discriminative knowledge of old words is directly overwritten by new gradients, causing a cliff-like drop in accuracy for old classes. Baseline Finetune in Table 1 quantifies this clearly: on GSC-v1, the average accuracy across 6-task sequences drops to only 26.84%, and to 9.59% for 21 tasks; on SC-100, it falls to 3.30% for a 51-task sequence (Table 1), essentially meaning the model "forgets" every time it learns a new word.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

Mainstream solutions in the Continual Learning (CL) literature can be divided into two camps, both of which have specific shortcomings as dissected in this paper:

- **Three fatal flaws of rehearsal-based methods**. Existing works on KWS incremental learning (e.g., RK's diverse sampling selection, DE-KWS's dark experience distillation) belong to the rehearsal category: they keep a small batch of samples (exemplars) from old tasks and mix them in during the training of new tasks. The paper points out their problems: (1) **Privacy and compliance risks**—storing user voice samples directly violates the user's right to "delete personal data" granted by regulations such as the European GDPR, and sensitive data like medical voice is a forbidden zone; (2) **Memory overhead is unacceptable on edge devices**—taking a rehearsal buffer of 500 samples as an example, storing raw waveforms of 1 second at 16 kHz results in approximately 16000×1×1×500 ≈ 8M tensor elements (Section 5.3), which is a heavy burden for resource-constrained devices. Moreover, methods like RK and Rwalk require saving the entire set of model weights; (3) **Performance is highly sensitive to buffer size and collapses as the number of classes increases**—increasing the buffer from 500 to 1000 raises Rwalk's accuracy on SC-100 from 76.78% to 84.49% and DE-KWS from 54.34% to 63.70% (Table 1 vs. Table 6), indicating that the accuracy of rehearsal methods is "bought." The task sequence curves in Figures 3 and 4 are even more straightforward: iCaRL shows a gentle decline at 11 tasks but a steep drop at 21 tasks, precisely because a fixed-size buffer cannot hold representative samples for an increasing number of classes.
- **Exemplar-free methods are under-explored and not lightweight enough in the KWS domain**. EFCIL methods in the visual domain (such as ADC, FeTrIL, FeCAM, etc.) indeed do not store old data, but many rely on complex optimizers or dynamic network structures, which are not friendly to edge devices with weak computing power that cannot run large-scale gradient updates. Furthermore, these methods are generally effective only under the premise that the "initial feature representation quality is very high," and their accuracy generally lags behind rehearsal-based methods. Regularization-based methods like EWC are exemplar-free but require calculating the Fisher Information Matrix and adding quadratic penalties to the loss, still involving multi-round gradient training. Their training time is even longer than Finetune (average 373.54 seconds per epoch vs. 262.08 seconds on GSC-v1, Table 2).

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

Condensing the above pain points into one sentence: How to design a KWS class incremental scheme that **stores absolutely no old data or old predictions** (privacy-safe, memory increases only by O(d²)), **runs no backpropagation** (single-epoch closed-form update, training completed in seconds), yet allows new keyword knowledge to seamlessly merge into the model with almost no drop in old word accuracy—and achieves accuracy that is not just "close to" but **surpasses** rehearsal-based methods that store 500~1000 samples (previously EFCIL usually cannot beat rehearsal methods), remaining stable at a scale of up to 100 keywords and 51 incremental tasks.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The system skeleton of AnalyticKWS is very restrained: a CNN acoustic feature extractor + an Acoustic Feature Expansion (AFE) random projection layer + an analytic linear classifier (ridge regression closed-form solution). The backbone adopts TC-ResNet-8 (Choi et al. 2019)—a small network designed for computationally constrained devices, containing three residual blocks, each composed of 1D temporal convolution, batch normalization, and ReLU, with channel numbers {16, 24, 32, 48} (including the first convolutional layer). The input is 40-dimensional MFCC with a frame shift of 160 samples (Section 4.2). The 'X' in the suffix AnalyticKWS-X represents the feature space dimension after AFE.

The entire training process is divided into three stages (Figure 1):

1.  **Feature Extraction Pre-training** (Figure 1a): The entire network is trained for multiple rounds using standard stochastic gradient descent on Task 0 data to obtain a strong feature extractor $\theta_{cnn}$ and classifier $\theta_{cls}$, after which the **CNN is frozen**. The motivation for freezing has three layers: (1) The mathematical prerequisite for analytic recursion—the subsequent derivations assume that the old task features $S_i'$ remain unchanged; once the feature extractor is updated, historical statistics become invalid; (2) Edge efficiency—freezing means there is no backpropagation during the incremental phase; (3) Privacy—knowledge from old tasks is compressed and saved in the form of second-order statistics (AFAM), rather than raw data.
2.  **Feature Recalibration** (Figure 1b): The gradient-trained classifier is **replaced** by an analytic classifier, which is retrained for one epoch on Task 0 data using analytic learning. Simultaneously, the AFE expands the feature dimension, and the Acoustic Feature Autocorrelation Matrix (AFAM) is saved. This step is the bridge that switches the network from the "gradient learning paradigm" to the "analytic learning paradigm"—without recalibration, the starting point for subsequent recursive formulas would not exist.
3.  **Incremental Keyword Adaptation** (Figure 1c): For each new task, features are extracted via forward pass, AFAM is updated, and classifier weights are updated recursively, each taking one epoch, looping until Task T.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Closed-form solution of the analytic classifier**. Let the expanded feature matrix for Task 0 be $S'_0$ and the label matrix be $y_0$ (one-hot). The analytic classifier solves the least squares problem with Frobenius norm regularization (ridge regression):

$$\arg\min_{\theta_{cls}^{(0)}} \| y_0 - S'_0 \theta_{cls}^{(0)} \|_F^2 + \gamma \| \theta_{cls}^{(0)} \|_F^2 \tag{2}$$

Its closed-form solution is:

$$\hat{\theta}_{cls}^{(0)} = (S_0^{\prime\top} S'_0 + \gamma I)^{-1} S_0^{\prime\top} y_0 \tag{3}$$

where $\gamma$ is the regularization coefficient, serving two purposes: preventing overfitting and ensuring that $S'S + \gamma I$ is strictly invertible (ablation study in Table 3: removing regularization with 512-dimensional expansion causes accuracy to drop slightly from 89.68% to 89.64%). The reason a closed-form solution can replace backpropagation is that the classifier is purely linear—the least squares solution for linear models does not require iterative optimization; a single matrix inversion suffices.

**Joint learning form and recursive equivalence**. If data from all tasks $D_0 \dots D_{t-1}$ were available, Eq. (2) could be generalized to ridge regression for all tasks jointly (Eq. 4), where $Y_{0:t-1}$ is a block-diagonal matrix composed of label matrices from each task. Its closed-form solution can be written in an accumulative form (Eq. 5). The core proposition of AnalyticKWS is: **Using only the current task's $S'_t, y_t$ and two quantities passed down from the previous task (old weights $\hat{\theta}^{(t-1)}$ and AFAM $A_{t-1}$), the recursively calculated $\hat{\theta}^{(t)}$ is strictly equal to the joint analytic solution**. The proof is in Appendix A, and the key tool is the Woodbury matrix identity:

$$(A + UCV)^{-1} = A^{-1} - A^{-1}U(C^{-1} + VA^{-1}U)^{-1}VA^{-1}$$

Let $A = A_{t-1}^{-1}$, $U = S_t^{\prime\top}$, $V = S'_t$, $C = I$, yielding the recursive formula for AFAM. The complete set of update formulas is:

$$A_{t-1} = \left(\sum_{i=0}^{t-1} S_i^{\prime\top} S'_i + \gamma I\right)^{-1} \tag{6}$$

$$\hat{\theta}_{cls}^{(t)} = \hat{\theta}_{cls}^{(t-1)} - A_t S_t^{\prime\top} S'_t \hat{\theta}_{cls}^{(t-1)} + A_t S_t^{\prime\top} y_t \tag{7}$$

$$\Delta = A_{t-1} S_t^{\prime\top} (I + S'_t A_{t-1} S_t^{\prime\top})^{-1} S'_t A_{t-1}, \quad A_t = A_{t-1} - \Delta \tag{8, 9}$$

Eq. (7) is the gain correction form of Recursive Least Squares (RLS): it first predicts the current task using old weights (the second term is the residual correction direction), then uses $A_t$ as the gain matrix to absorb new information. The AFAM (acoustic feature autocorrelation matrix) defined in Eq. (6) is the key to the scheme's "memory without storing data"—it is a compressed summary of the second-order statistics ($\Sigma S'S' + \gamma I)^{-1}$ of all historical task features. Its dimension is only $d \times d$ (where $d$ is the expanded feature dimension); at 128 dimensions, this is $128 \times 128 = 16K$ tensor elements (Section 5.3). The number of columns in the classifier weights grows with the number of tasks (i.e., classes); with each new task, columns corresponding to the new classes are concatenated.

**Why "recursive equals joint" implies "no forgetting"**: At the end of any task $t$, the classifier weights are mathematically equivalent to the solution of joint ridge regression performed on all data from tasks $0 \dots t$. Old class knowledge is not "approximately" preserved but is precisely retained (ACIL paper calls this absolute memorization). This also explains the phenomenon of BWT being close to zero in Table 1: on SC-100, AnalyticKWS-256's 51-task BWT is only -0.003. The residual tiny negative value is not caused by weight drift (weights are strictly consistent with the joint solution); a more reasonable explanation is that after the number of classes increases from $t$ to $T$, old tasks face more confusable classes during inference, causing a natural slight drop in accuracy (the author's inference, not elaborated in the paper).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Acoustic Feature Expansion (AFE)

An additional linear projection layer $\theta_{afe}$ is inserted after the frozen CNN features $S_0$ to map features to a **higher-dimensional** space, obtaining $S'_0$. Each element of $\theta_{afe}$ is randomly initialized from a normal distribution and **remains fixed** throughout the training process; the expansion dimension is deliberately chosen to be larger than the original dimension of $S_0$.

There are three layers of "why" for this design: (1) **Why increase dimensionality**—the analytic classifier is essentially a (linear) classifier in the expanded space, and its expressive power is constrained by the linear separability of features. In speech signals, many fine discriminative differences (similar phonemes, confusable words) may not be linearly separable in original low-dimensional features. Random high-dimensional projection is equivalent to expanding the capacity of the linear classifier. The paper's original words state that AFE "transforms original features into richer representations without significantly increasing computational cost, preserving finer distinctions in speech signals." Ablation evidence (Table 3) is very decisive: without AFE, accuracy is 86.57%; adding 64 dimensions raises it to 87.19%, 128 dimensions to 88.72%, 256 dimensions to 89.23%, and 512 dimensions reaches the peak of 89.68%. Monotonic gains indicate that capacity was indeed bottlenecked by feature dimensions. (2) **Why random and fixed rather than learned**—if $\theta_{afe}$ is learned via gradients, backpropagation and multi-round iterations are reintroduced, destroying the foundation of "single-epoch closed-form update." Moreover, if the projection layer varies with tasks, recursive equivalence (which relies on historical features $S_i'$ remaining unchanged) immediately fails. Random fixed projection concentrates all "learning" in the analytic classifier layer, leaving only forward matrix multiplications for computation, which perfectly matches the computing profile of small-footprint devices. (3) **Controllable cost**—dimensionality increase occurs only in the linear layer; the extra overhead for inference and training is one or two matrix multiplications, far cheaper than enlarging the CNN.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: AFAM Recursive Update—Compressing "Memory" into a $d \times d$ Matrix

Rehearsal-based methods remember "the data itself," while AnalyticKWS remembers "the second-order statistics of the data." The Woodbury recursion in Eqs. (8)(9) ensures that $A_t$ is calculated only from $A_{t-1}$ and the current task's features; no old samples participate in the entire process. The compression ratio of this information compression is crushing: in a 128-dimensional configuration, AFAM occupies $128 \times 128 = 16K$ tensor elements, whereas a 500-sample rehearsal buffer stored as raw waveforms requires approximately 8M elements, a difference of about 500 times; RK and Rwalk also require saving the entire set of model weights (Section 5.3). A more practical benefit lies in **scalability**: rehearsal buffers have a fixed size; as the number of classes continues to grow, the representative samples allocated to each class are constantly diluted (the collapse of iCaRL at 21 tasks in Figure 4 stems from this). In contrast, the $d \times d$ size of AFAM is independent of the number of tasks—memory usage remains constant whether there are 51 tasks or more. This is also implicit evidence in Table 6: after doubling the baseline buffer from 500 to 1000, the accuracy of various rehearsal methods changes significantly, whereas AnalyticKWS's numbers remain exactly consistent with Table 1 (85.77/85.66/85.55 and 87.99/87.85/87.63 under three task splits of SC-100), because it has no buffer hyperparameter.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Single-epoch, Backpropagation-Free Incremental Adaptation

The computational flow of analytic updates (Algorithm 1) contains only: forward feature extraction $\rightarrow$ stacking feature matrices $\rightarrow$ updating AFAM via Eqs. (8)(9) $\rightarrow$ updating weights via Eq. (7). There is no gradient iteration of the loss function in the entire process—the closed-form solution is reached in one go. Therefore, **only one epoch of data traversal is needed for each new task** (used to accumulate the two sets of statistics $S_t^{\prime\top}S'_t$ and $S_t^{\prime\top}y_t$). This is directly reflected in training time (Table 2, measured on NVIDIA RTX 3090): the average training time per epoch for AnalyticKWS-128 is only 5.09/5.97/9.31 seconds on GSC-v1/GSC-v2/SC-100, and 5.49/6.48/10.47 seconds for 256 dimensions; compared to Finetune's 262.08/277.75/433.29 seconds, DE-KWS's 270.82/350.42/576.85 seconds, and RK's 956.55/1239.46/1771.76 seconds, it is faster by approximately two orders of magnitude (about 190 times faster than RK and 62 times faster than DE-KWS on SC-100). For battery-powered edge devices with limited CPU clock speeds, "learning a new word takes only a few seconds without running backpropagation" is the watershed for deployment feasibility.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Vs. Rehearsal-based methods (iCaRL, BiC, Rwalk, RK, DE-KWS)**: These methods rely on "replaying old data + distillation/regularization" to combat forgetting. AnalyticKWS relies on "closed-form solution strictly equivalent to joint training" to eliminate the source of forgetting mathematically. Therefore, it requires neither a 500~1000 sample buffer nor saving old logits (DE-KWS's approach) or old model weights (RK/Rwalk's approach), offering advantages in both privacy and memory, and reducing training time by another two orders of magnitude.
- **Vs. EWC-style regularization methods**: EWC uses the Fisher Information Matrix to estimate parameter importance and adds quadratic penalties to the loss to constrain weight drift. It still requires multi-round gradient training and can only "softly constrain" forgetting. AnalyticKWS simply does not work at the loss function level, replacing classifier learning entirely with a one-time recursive least squares.
- **Vs. Analytic learning lineage in the visual domain (BRMP, ACIL)**: BRMP (Zhuang et al. 2021) proposes block-recursive Moore-Penrose inverse, and ACIL (Zhuang et al. 2022) first applies analytic learning to class incremental learning, proving that linear classifiers can reach joint training accuracy. However, both remain in image classification. The difference of AnalyticKWS is **bringing analytic CL into the speech domain for the first time and redesigning it according to KWS engineering constraints**: adding AFE random expansion to address the insufficient linear separability of acoustic features; selecting the small-footprint TC-ResNet-8 as the backbone instead of large models; validating on speech benchmarks (up to 100 keywords, 51 tasks); and additionally measuring training time and extra memory, two key metrics for edge deployment—metrics that are usually not reported in visual CL papers.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

The paper uses three datasets of increasing scale (Table 4), deliberately covering incremental scenarios from small to large, which differs from previous KWS incremental learning studies that only used a single dataset:

- **GSC-V1** (Google Speech Commands v1): 30 classes, 64,727 samples, 1 second per sample, keywords such as "yes", "no", "up", "down";
- **GSC-V2**: 35 classes, 105,829 samples, adding 5 new words "Backward", "Forward", "Follow", "Learn", "Visual" to the original 30 words, with richer speaker and data diversity;
- **SC-100** (Song et al. 2024): 100 classes, 313,951 keyword utterances, constructed by mining words and timestamps from the LibriSpeech corpus using the KeywordMiner tool, and then segmenting single words from complete sentences using a segmenter. Keywords include "change", "turn", "light", "door". The actual utterance duration in SC-100 ranges from 0.4 to 1 second, padded with random leading/trailing zeros to 1 second, and retains precise start/end timestamps for keywords (usable for finer-grained early decision analysis); the GSC series only pads or truncates at the end and does not provide time boundaries.

All audio is resampled to 16 kHz, and training/validation sets are split 80%/20% (Section 4.1). Task splits follow the format of "initial task + several incremental steps" (Table 5): GSC-V1 uses 15+(5×3), 10+(10×2), 10+(20×1) corresponding to T=6, 11, 21; GSC-V2 uses 15+(5×4), 15+(10×2), 15+(20×1); SC-100 uses 50+(10×5), 50+(25×2), 50+(50×1) corresponding to T=11, 26, 51—i.e., first use 50 keywords for base pre-training, then incrementally add up to 50 steps, with 1 new word per step, specifically stress-testing large-scale incremental learning. Training configuration: TC-ResNet-8 backbone, 40-dimensional MFCC, frame shift 160, 50 epochs per task for training (referring to baseline gradient training; AnalyticKWS's incremental adaptation requires only 1 epoch by design). (Note: Section 4.1 in the text gives an example stating "for T=11, pre-train with 10 words first, and split the remaining data into 20 tasks with 1 new word each," which contradicts Table 5's 10+(10×2) corresponding to T=11. This is suspected to be a typo; Table 5 should be taken as correct.)

### Definition and Rationale of Evaluation Metrics

- **Average Accuracy ACC**: $ACC = (1/(T+1)) \sum_{t=0}^{T} A_t$, where $A_t$ is the average accuracy of the model on the test set $D^test_{0:t}$ of all completed tasks after training on Task $t$. It measures the overall performance of both new and old classes and is the overarching metric for class incremental learning.
- **Backward Transfer BWT** (Lopez-Paz and Ranzato 2017): $BWT = (1/T) \sum_{t=1}^{T} (A_T - A_t)$, measuring the drag of learning new tasks on old tasks. Closer to zero means less forgetting; positive values indicate that learning new tasks helps old ones. The reason for selecting it is direct: catastrophic forgetting is exactly the target of this paper, and a metric that directly quantifies "amount of forgetting" is required.
- **Task Training Time TT**: The average training time per epoch for all tasks, measured on NVIDIA RTX 3090. This metric is added because the target scenario of the paper is resource-constrained edge devices; "whether adaptation can be completed in seconds" is as important as accuracy.
- **Extra Memory**: The memory occupied by storing rehearsal data or extra model weights. Used to quantify the memory dividends of the exemplar-free design (privacy benefits are guaranteed by the method design itself).

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

There are six baselines (Appendix C): EWC (Fisher Information Matrix + quadratic penalty), Rwalk (path integral + mini-batch rehearsal), iCaRL (fixed buffer + nearest mean classifier + distillation), BiC (bias correction layer two-stage training), RK (diverse sampling + data augmentation + distillation, designed for online KWS), DE-KWS (dark knowledge distillation storing and replaying pre-softmax logits, previously the strongest KWS incremental baseline); methods marked with an asterisk use a 500-sample buffer. Finetune serves as the lower bound, and Joint training serves as the upper bound (94.93%/94.76%/95.32% on GSC-v1/GSC-v2/SC-100, Table 1).

**Main Comparison (Table 1, Buffer 500)**: On GSC-v1, AnalyticKWS-256 achieves 89.51/85.83/85.60 under T=6/11/21, slightly surpassing the strongest baseline DE-KWS's 88.82/85.59/85.53 and Rwalk's 87.03/85.38/84.55; on GSC-v2, the advantage widens: 89.48/89.53/89.50 vs. DE-KWS's 87.78/85.34/82.38 (+7.12 percentage points at T=21). The magnitude of the gap is truly evident on SC-100: under T=11/26/51, AnalyticKWS-256 reaches 87.99/87.85/87.63, while the best rehearsal baseline Rwalk only achieves 76.93/77.21/76.78 (a difference of about 11 percentage points), RK drops to 68.72/61.62/59.54, DE-KWS drops to 67.71/59.78/54.34, and iCaRL collapses from 69.3% to 23.70%. That is, the larger the task scale and the more classes, the harder it is for rehearsal-based methods to hold up, while the buffer-free AnalyticKWS hardly drops (87.99→87.63, a total drop of only 0.36 percentage points across three settings). BWT is also clean: on SC-100, AnalyticKWS-256 is -0.017/-0.007/-0.003, comprehensively outperforming all baselines (iCaRL is -0.067/-0.047/-0.038, Finetune is the worst at -0.264). A gap that needs to be honestly pointed out: AnalyticKWS-256 is still 5~7 percentage points away from the Joint upper bound (89.51 vs. 94.93 on GSC-v1 T=6, 87.99 vs. 95.32 on SC-100 T=11). This loss mainly comes from two structural constraints: the feature extractor is frozen after Task 0, and the classifier is a (linear) classifier in the random expanded space.

**Increasing the buffer cannot catch up (Table 6, SC-100, Buffer 1000)**: Rwalk rises to 84.61/83.95/84.49, RK to 77.27/74.18/72.37, but still falls below AnalyticKWS-128's 85.77/85.66/85.55 and AnalyticKWS-256's 87.99/87.85/87.63; DE-KWS is only 74.91/67.61/63.70, and iCaRL continues to collapse to 26.05%. The TT column is also crushing: AnalyticKWS-128 takes only 15.35/6.77/5.82 seconds, while the slowest RK takes 1141.46/691.95/439.79 seconds.

**Per-task Heatmaps and Curves (Figures 2, 3, 4)**: The GSC-v2 six-task heatmap (buffer 500) shows that RK has high diagonal values when learning new tasks (95.72/98.64/97.60/97.29/96.26/97.88 for each task immediately after learning) but suffers severe forgetting—after learning all tasks, accuracy for Task 1 drops from 95.72 to 61.92, and Task 2 is only 74.25, a typical "learn fast, forget fast"; DE-KWS balances both ends using dark experience (final row 62.13/74.35/78.16/86.09/79.27/93.14); AnalyticKWS-256's final behavior is 88.17/78.65/81.49/80.47/71.80/72.12—old tasks are maintained best (Task 1 drops only from 96.24 to 88.17), at the cost of lower diagonal accuracy for new tasks themselves (Task 5 and 6 are only 74.86 and 72.12, respectively), intuitively exposing the limitations of frozen features + linear classifier on the expressive power of later new classes. The 11/21 task accuracy-task number curves in Figures 3 and 4 further show: EWC, BiC, and RK decline rapidly with the number of tasks; iCaRL drops steeply at 21 tasks because its fixed buffer cannot hold enough representative samples; DE-KWS is better but trends downward; AnalyticKWS-256 maintains the highest and flattest curve in both settings.

**Training Time and Memory (Table 2, Section 5.3)**: TT has already been cited in the Methodology section—on GSC-v1, AnalyticKWS-128 (5.09 seconds/epoch) is about 51 times faster than Finetune (262.08 seconds) and about 188 times faster than RK (956.55 seconds); on SC-100, 9.31 seconds vs. RK's 1771.76 seconds is about 190 times faster. In terms of memory, AnalyticKWS only stores AFAM: 128 dimensions means 16K tensor elements, reducing the memory of a 500-buffer (approx. 8M elements) by about 500 times, and it does not require saving the entire set of model weights like Rwalk/RK.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

Ablations focus on two components (Table 3): **AFE expansion dimension** and **regularization $\gamma$**. Without AFE, only with regularization, accuracy is 86.57%; adding 64-dimensional AFE raises it to 87.19% (+0.62), 128 dimensions to 88.72% (+2.15), 256 dimensions to 89.23% (+2.66), and 512 dimensions reaches the peak of 89.68% (+3.11); removing regularization at 512 dimensions causes a slight drop to 89.64%. Two conclusions: (1) The contribution of AFE is monotonic and significant—a gain of three to four percentage points validates the design judgment that "acoustic features need dimensionality expansion to feed the linear analytic classifier." The larger the expansion dimension, the higher the accuracy, and no saturation point has been seen yet (512 dimensions is still the best result), at the cost of AFAM becoming $d \times d$, with computation and memory growing quadratically with $d$; (2) The gain from regularization is small in this setting (0.04 percentage points), but its role in ensuring numerical stability for matrix invertibility cannot be reflected by accuracy differences. A notable omission: Table 3 does not specify the dataset and task split (not explicitly stated in the paper), and the main experiments only used two levels, 128/256; the better-performing 512 dimensions did not enter the main table—this is speculated to be due to trade-offs in edge memory/computation, but the paper does not report the rationale for this choice.

## Main Contributions

1.  **Introducing analytic learning to class incremental KWS in the speech domain for the first time**: Replacing backpropagation for classifier updates with Recursive Least Squares (derived via Woodbury identity), mathematically guaranteeing that the recursive solution at any moment is strictly consistent with the joint analytic solution of all seen data (proof in Appendix A), eliminating catastrophic forgetting from a mechanistic rather than heuristic perspective—this is an effective extension of the visual conclusions of ACIL/BRMP to the speech small-model scenario.
2.  **Truly exemplar-free and privacy-friendly**: Storing no old audio, old logits, or old model weights, all historical knowledge is compressed into a single $d \times d$ AFAM (128 dimensions is only 16K tensor elements, approximately 1/500 of the 8M elements of a 500-sample rehearsal buffer), naturally avoiding GDPR-like compliance risks, and memory usage is decoupled from the number of tasks, naturally adapting to large-scale class incremental learning (SC-100 100 classes, 51 tasks).
3.  **Extreme reduction in training overhead**: Gradient-free, single-epoch adaptation, with training time per epoch in the range of 5~10 seconds, two orders of magnitude faster than the strongest rehearsal baseline (about 190 times faster than RK on SC-100), while simultaneously achieving first place in accuracy across three datasets and nine task splits—breaking the convention that "EFCIL accuracy cannot beat rehearsal-based methods."
4.  **Effective component-level design**: AFE random fixed dimensionality expansion brings a gain of up to +3.11 percentage points (Table 3), forming a lightweight combination of "frozen random features + closed-form linear readout" with the analytic classifier, providing a complete, reproducible solution for continuous learning on edge devices for small-footprint KWS (dataset splits, task divisions, backbone configurations, and baseline details are all provided in the main text and Appendices B/C).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

-  **Feature extractor is frozen once, limiting plasticity**: The CNN is trained with gradients only on Task 0 and remains unchanged thereafter. The discriminative information of new classes must be squeezed into the representation learned by the base task + random projection + linear readout through a narrow channel. The diagonal accuracy of later tasks in Figure 2's heatmap is only 74.86%/72.12% (Tasks 5, 6), which is direct evidence; the 5~7 percentage point gap from the Joint upper bound also mainly stems from this. The paper itself admits in Limitations that it "needs to enhance the plasticity of the method in scenarios with rapid task switching."
-  **AFE is random and fixed**: The projection direction does not perceive tasks or data distributions. Whether the gains of expansion beyond 512 dimensions saturate, and how large the variance caused by randomness is, are not reported by the paper; the expansion dimension $d$ is a triangular trade-off between accuracy-memory-computation (AFAM is $d \times d$), and the main table only reports 128/256, without providing a selection criterion.
-  **Classifier head grows linearly with the number of classes**: The number of weight columns is proportional to the number of tasks. When the number of classes is extremely large, the overhead of the linear layer itself will gradually become apparent (acceptable in the 100-class scenario, not discussed for larger scales in the paper).
-  **Expressive ceiling of linear readout**: The beautiful properties of the analytic framework (closed-form solution, recursive equivalence) are entirely built on "classifier linearity." Any non-linearization (e.g., kernelization, multi-layer readout) would destroy the existing derivations, and the mathematical path toward evolving to stronger readout functions is not yet clear (not discussed in the paper).
-  **Multi-language KWS not verified**: Explicitly listed as an unexplored challenge (first item in Limitations).

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

-  **Single backbone and features**: All experiments use only one combination of TC-ResNet-8 + 40-dimensional MFCC. The sensitivity of the method to other small models (e.g., DS-CNN, BC-ResNet) or other features is not verified, and evidence for the backbone generalization of the conclusions is lacking.
-  **Base task size not ablated separately**: GSC starts with 10/15 classes, SC-100 starts with 50 classes. The impact of the base scale on the quality of frozen features (and thus on final accuracy) is mixed with factors like AFE, without controlled experiments to isolate them.
-  **"Edge" claims lack edge measurements**: Training times are all measured on NVIDIA RTX 3090. There are no latency, peak memory, or energy consumption data on real edge hardware such as MCUs/DSPs, nor are there reports on inference-side overhead; the assertion that it is "suitable for resource-constrained devices" currently only has indirect support from GPU time.
-  **Incomplete ablation reporting**: Table 3 does not specify the dataset and task settings; variance or significance tests for multiple runs are not reported for any results (not reported in the paper); the value and sensitivity of the regularization coefficient $\gamma$ are also not given.
-  **Minor flaws in the text**: The task split example for T=11 in Section 4.1 contradicts Table 5 (suspected typo); additionally, there is no experimental comparison to position it against few-shot custom wake-word routes (non-CL methods such as prototype networks).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

-  **Re-balancing plasticity and stability**: Introducing a lightweight feature refresh mechanism without breaking recursive equivalence (e.g., periodically merging analytic statistics from multiple tasks to recalibrate the AFE projection, or block update strategies like block-recursive Moore-Penrose inverse) to allow frozen representations to absorb the distribution of later tasks. This is a key direction to simultaneously preserve the "closed-form, exemplar-free" properties and achieve higher new-class accuracy.
-  **Stronger readout functions**: Exploring kernelized analytic learning or hierarchical analytic structures to break through the expressive bottleneck of linear classifiers while maintaining recursiveness. AFE could also study structured random projections (e.g., grouped projections preserving time-frequency structures) to replace pure random initialization.
-  **Multi-language and cross-domain incremental**: Pushing the framework to multi-language KWS and speaker/channel drift scenarios to test the robustness of AFAM under larger distribution differences.
-  **End-to-end edge validation**: Quantifying training time, peak memory, and energy consumption on real MCUs/low-power DSPs, and combining quantization/binarization (such as BiFSMN-type schemes) to truly land the "second-level, backpropagation-free" incremental adaptation on milliwatt-level devices.
-  **Utilizing SC-100 timestamps for streaming early-decision incremental**: SC-100 comes with keyword start/end annotations. AnalyticKWS's single-epoch forward characteristic can be combined with streaming early-decision frameworks to enable "learning while speaking" online keyword registration.
