## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Voice assistant devices (represented in the paper by Amazon Echo and Google Home) operate by sending continuous audio streams to cloud servers for processing, incurring threefold costs: network congestion, response latency, and privacy leakage. The industry-standard remedy is hybrid processing: running a minimal keyword spotting (KWS) component locally on the device, and only waking up the large-scale automatic speech recognition (ASR) model in the cloud upon detecting a trigger word (e.g., "Alexa"). This architecture offloads the vast majority of the burden from the cloud, but shifts all pressure to the edge—the paper explicitly notes that such on-device KWS components typically operate under a model size constraint of less than 2 MB and extremely limited computational budgets (Introduction).

The paper addresses a very real pain point in TinyML deployment that is often glossed over in literature: **on microcontrollers, peak SRAM usage during inference is often the primary bottleneck, rather than the model parameter count itself**. A model with excellent accuracy on the test set may fail with an Out-Of-Memory (OOM) error during deployment simply because the activation buffer exceeds the SRAM limit at a certain layer—"a model that is correct in theory fails in practice." This phenomenon directly corresponds to the OOM record for the OASI 3 model on the STM32F401 in Table III of the paper: it achieves the highest accuracy of 91.0% among the three models, but its peak RAM usage of 135.5 KB exceeds the limit of the low-end board, causing deployment to fail directly.

The third pain point is a mismatch in optimization paradigms. Existing approaches to improve KWS—compact model hand-design, Neural Architecture Search (NAS), and model compression (pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation)—are almost exclusively **single-objective**: they run once and give you a single solution. However, real-world TinyML KWS is a multi-objective problem: it requires maximizing accuracy while minimizing memory footprint, latency, and energy consumption. Single-objective methods either hard-code resource constraints to optimize only accuracy (yielding no trade-off frontier) or weight multiple objectives into a scalar (where weights are arbitrarily chosen, yielding no frontier for deployers to select from based on scenarios). Multi-Objective Bayesian Optimization (MOBO) naturally fits this problem by building surrogate models for the accuracy-efficiency trade-off and searching for the Pareto optimal solution set; however, MOBO has a well-known weakness—extreme sensitivity to initialization—which is drastically amplified in TinyML scenarios where the evaluation budget is already minimal.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper’s critique of existing methods focuses specifically on the initialization stage, structured in layers:

- **Space-filling and objective-agnostic initialization**: Structured sampling methods such as Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS), Sobol sequences, and factorial designs, as well as meta-learning warm-start methods like MI-SMBO, provide early exploration capabilities at low overhead. However, their sampling criteria only consider the coverage of the design space, completely ignoring the structure of the objective space. The paper cites reference [5] (Greif et al. 2025) stating that objective-agnostic initialization typically leads to lower-quality surrogate models and slower convergence rates. In terms of the GP-MOBO mechanism, the initial dataset $D_0$ determines the posterior mean and variance of the Gaussian Process early on, thereby shaping the entire exploration trajectory—if initial points are dominated or clustered in the objective space, the Expected Hypervolume Improvement (EHVI) acquisition function’s estimation of hypervolume improvement becomes distorted, and early decisions mislead subsequent convergence.
- **Cost and blind spots of evolutionary multi-objective algorithms**: Represented by NSGA-II, it approximates the frontier via non-dominated sorting, but the sorting complexity grows quadratically with population size ($O(n^2)$). This is reflected in Table I of the paper, showing 1389.5 seconds per iteration, making it the most expensive among all compared methods; simultaneously, the randomness of its crossover and mutation operators means that while it can "fill" the frontier, it often fails to converge to boundary points—especially the corner that is "compact and reasonably accurate," which is precisely the area of greatest concern for TinyML.
- **Insufficient capability of single heuristic optimizers**: When Multi-Objective Simulated Annealing (MOSA) is used alone as an optimizer (Table I), the final accuracy is only 87.9%, the lowest among the four methods, indicating that this model-free search method of random perturbation plus annealing acceptance has sufficient exploration but insufficient refinement.
- **Lack of systematic comparison in TinyML literature**: The paper points out that these initialization methods are used ad hoc in TinyML literature, lacking systematic comparison and statistical analysis—this sentence is also the motivation for the paper’s experimental design (controlled comparison with fixed MOBO, swapping only initialization + Kruskal–Wallis test).

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The core challenge can be condensed into one sentence: **Without expanding the evaluation budget, let the initial dataset of MOBO itself carry the structure of the objective space (Pareto) and the coverage of the design space, thereby avoiding early misleading decisions and SRAM violation configurations caused by poor surrogate model conditioning.** Broken down, there are four sub-challenges: (1) Each objective evaluation equals a complete network training and validation, which is extremely expensive; the overhead of any initialization strategy must be strictly limited; (2) Under low budgets, the early calibration quality of the GP surrogate determines success or failure; initial points must be both close to the frontier and dispersed in the design space, two requirements that constrain each other; (3) SRAM constraints are hard; objective-agnostic uniform sampling will produce many configurations that are found to be infeasible only at deployment time, wasting precious budget; (4) Deployment feasibility (SRAM, Flash, latency) is multi-dimensional, and latency must be measured on real hardware; how to handle these three types of resources in the optimization loop requires careful design rather than simply stacking them into the objective function.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall architecture of OASI is a bi-level black-box constrained multi-objective optimization framework (Paper Fig. 1, Fig. 2): the lower level is the training problem—given an architecture configuration $h$, solve for training parameters $\theta^*(h) = \arg\min L(D_{tr}; \theta, h)$ (Eq. 1); the upper level is the search problem—searching for $h$ in the architecture hyperparameter space $H$ that simultaneously optimizes accuracy and resources. Evaluating each $h$ at the upper level triggers a complete lower-level training, which is the root cause of the expensive evaluation and the motivation for introducing surrogate models.

The KWS pipeline itself (Section II.A) is a standard streaming structure: continuous audio $x(t)$ is mapped to a log-Mel feature sequence $E = \{e_0, \dots, e_{T-1}\} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times K}$ via Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) ($T$ frames, $K$ dimensions per frame); for the $i$-th frame, a context slice $E[i] = [e_{is-P}, \dots, e_{is+F}] \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times (P+F+1)}$ is taken (stride $s$, $P$ frames forward, $F$ frames backward); the acoustic model $g_\theta$ outputs softmax posteriors $g_\theta(E[i]) = [p(C_1|E[i]), \dots, p(C_N|E[i])]^T$, trained with cross-entropy; for streaming inference, time smoothing $\bar{y}_n(t) = (1/W) \cdot \sum_{i=t-W+1}^{t} y_n(i)$ is performed on a window of length $W$, and detection is declared when the smoothed keyword posterior $\bar{y}_{kw}(t) \geq \tau$. This pipeline definition indicates that the object of optimization for OASI is the architecture hyperparameters of the acoustic model $g_\theta$, not the feature pipeline or decoding strategy.

The design vector $h = \{L, F, K, S, D, B, U\}$ encodes seven types of architecture hyperparameters: convolutional depth ($L$), number of filters per layer ($F$), kernel size ($K$), stride ($S$), dropout ratio ($D$), batch normalization switch ($B$), and fully connected units ($U$). This vector is the coordinate of the upper-level search space.

The definitions of the three objective quantities and constraints (Eqs. 2–5) reflect strong engineering rigor, with clear motivation for each:

- **Accuracy**: $Acc(h) = E_{(x,y) \sim D_{val}}[\delta(\hat{y}(x; h), y)]$ (Eq. 2), measured on the validation set—note that it is not measured on the test set, which is reserved for the final report, demonstrating rigor.
- **Flash Usage**: $Flash(h) = \text{bytes}(\theta^*(h), h)$ (Eq. 3), explicitly stating that it "includes quantized weights and compiled inference code"—meaning this objective quantity is not the theoretical parameter count, but a metric close to the deployment image.
- **Peak SRAM**: $RAM(h) = \max_t RAM_{act}(h, t)$ (Eq. 4), covering activation buffers, operator workspaces, and memory arenas managed by the framework, defined as a hard feasibility constraint: a configuration is deployable only if $RAM(h) \leq B_{ram}$.
- **Final Constrained Bi-Objective Problem**: $\min_{h \in H} (f_1(h), f_2(h))$, s.t. $RAM(h) \leq B_{ram}$ (Eq. 5), where $f_1 = -Acc$ (negating to convert maximization to minimization), $f_2 = Flash$.

**Why latency is not included in the optimization objectives** is a key architectural decision in this paper, for which the paper provides a complete chain of reasoning: inference latency and cycle counts are essentially hardware-dependent quantities, and accurate measurement requires compilation and execution on the board; if latency is directly placed into the MOBO objective, hardware-in-the-loop evaluation is required for every iteration, causing the optimization cost to skyrocket, and coupling the entire search process to the configuration of a specific MCU, thereby losing cross-platform portability of the search results. Therefore, the paper adopts a three-resource separation: **peak SRAM is used as a hard constraint during the optimization phase, Flash is used as the objective to be minimized, and latency and cycle-level efficiency are evaluated separately in the hardware-in-the-loop verification stage after optimization**. This split makes the optimization process hardware-agnostic (except for a scalar SRAM upper limit), leaving deployment feasibility to post-hoc verification. Why is SRAM a constraint while Flash is an objective? The paper does not explicitly state this, but mechanistically it can be understood: SRAM overflow is a hard runtime failure (OOM, a veto with no room for trade-off), while Flash usage is a gradual trade-off dimension (larger Flash allows larger models in exchange for higher accuracy), making it suitable as an objective axis.

OASI’s position within this framework is to replace the initialization stage of MOBO: using MOSA to generate a Pareto-biased initial archive, then selecting the initial dataset $D_0$ from it to condition the GP surrogate, after which everything proceeds normally through the GP-EHVI MOBO loop (Fig. 2).

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The MOBO part adopts a standard configuration: fitting a Gaussian Process surrogate $f_k(h) \sim GP(\mu_k(h), k_k(h, h'))$ for each objective, and proposing new candidates using the Expected Hypervolume Improvement (EHVI) acquisition function: $h_{next} = \arg\max_h \alpha_{EHVI}(h)$. EHVI depends on the Pareto frontier estimated from current data and the predictive distribution of the GP; this is the mathematical channel through which initialization quality propagates to search behavior—the initial data determines the early form of $\mu_k$ and $k_k$, thereby determining the value landscape of $\alpha_{EHVI}$.

The core of OASI is Algorithm 1 (Algorithm 1), a MOSA-style multi-chain short-range annealing sampler. Inputs include: search space $H$, objective vector $f(h)$, number of chains $N_{chains}$, iterations per chain $N_{iter}$, dual initial temperatures $T_{acc}^0$ and $T_{size}^0$, cooling rates $\alpha_{acc}$ and $\alpha_{size}$, and initial dataset size $n$. The process is:

1. Initialize the archive $IA$ as empty;
2. For each chain: reset dual temperatures to initial values; randomly sample a starting point $h_{curr}$ from $H$ and evaluate $f(h_{curr})$, storing $(h_{curr}, f_{curr})$ into $IA$;
3. Each iteration: perturb the current solution to obtain a neighbor $h_{next}$ and evaluate; similarly store $(h_{next}, f_{next})$ into $IA$—**note that rejected evaluations are not discarded, all enter the archive**;
4. Calculate dual acceptance probabilities and sample two uniform random numbers $u_1, u_2 \sim U(0,1)$: for the accuracy objective (maximizing $f_1$), $p_{acc} = 1$ if $f_1(h_{next}) > f_1(h_{curr})$, otherwise $p_{acc} = \exp(-(f_1(h_{curr}) - f_1(h_{next}))/T_{acc})$; for the size objective (minimizing $f_2$), $p_{size} = 1$ if $f_2(h_{next}) < f_2(h_{curr})$, otherwise $p_{size} = \exp(-(f_2(h_{next}) - f_2(h_{curr}))/T_{size})$;
5. Accept the move (update $h_{curr}$) if and only if both $u_1 < p_{acc}$ and $u_2 < p_{size}$ hold simultaneously;
6. Geometric cooling: $T_{acc} \leftarrow \alpha_{acc} \cdot T_{acc}$, $T_{size} \leftarrow \alpha_{size} \cdot T_{size}$;
7. After all chains end, select a diverse subset of size $n$ from $IA$ using the maximin rule as $D_0$.

There are three points worth digging into mathematically. **First, the product-form acceptance criterion for independent dual objectives.** Standard single-objective simulated annealing uses a Metropolis criterion; here, a separate annealing chain is built for each of the two objectives, and acceptance requires both to pass simultaneously, equivalent to a joint criterion with acceptance probability $p_{acc} \times p_{size}$. A solution that improves in accuracy but worsens in size is still accepted with probability $\exp(-\Delta_{size}/T_{size})$—when the temperature is high (early in the chain), such cross-objective trade-off moves are tolerated; as the temperature decays, the acceptance criterion tightens, and the chain naturally shrinks toward the non-dominated region. This biases the sampling distribution toward the Pareto frontier without explicitly calculating dominance relations. **Second, the archive accumulates all evaluations (including rejected solutions).** Rejected samples mark the dominated regions in the objective space, and they also have informational value for the GP surrogate—the GP is a regression model, and knowing "where it is bad" is as valuable as knowing "where it is good" for reducing uncertainty. **Third, maximin subset selection.** Maximin is a classic diversity criterion (maximizing the minimum distance between selected points), applied here to the design space $H$, ensuring that $D_0$ has broad coverage in the hyperparameter space.

For evaluation metrics, Tchebycheff scalarization is also used: $J(h) = \max(w_1 \cdot |f_1(h) - f_1^*|, w_2 \cdot |f_2(h) - f_2^*|)$, with equal weights $w_1 = w_2 = 0.5$ and reference ideal point $(f_1^*, f_2^*)$. The significance of the Tchebycheff norm is that it is a scalarization form capable of reaching all points on the Pareto frontier (including non-convex parts), having no dead angles unlike weighted sums, making it reasonable for selecting the "best compromise solution" among the four methods.

On the hardware side, the Deployability Index (DI, Eq. 6) is proposed:

$$ DI_{i,h} = \prod_{u \in \{R,F,T\}} (1 - U_{i,h}^{(u)}/B_h^{(u)}) \cdot \mathbb{I}(U_{i,h}^{(u)} \leq B_h^{(u)} \forall u) $$

where $U^{(R)}$, $U^{(F)}$, $U^{(T)}$ are the peak SRAM, Flash usage, and latency of model $i$ on board $h$, respectively, $B^{(R)}$, $B^{(F)}$, $B^{(T)}$ are the corresponding hardware or application upper limits, and $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. If any resource dimension exceeds the limit, DI becomes zero directly; otherwise, DI equals the product of the "remaining slack ratio" of the three dimensions. **Why use multiplication instead of addition or minimum**: The paper’s reason is that the multiplicative form penalizes the saturation of any single dimension—if one item uses 95% of the budget, $(1 - 0.95) = 0.05$, this small factor drags down the entire product, which accurately reflects the bottleneck behavior of MCU deployment "if any resource hits the limit, the whole thing is undeployable." In an additive form, saturation in one item can be masked by surplus in others; in a minimum form, information from other dimensions is completely ignored. Multiplication is a compromise between the two. A larger DI indicates more sufficient deployment slack.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Pareto-Biased Initialization Sampling (OASI Core)

The innovation does not lie in simulated annealing itself (MOSA is a method from 2008, cited as [8] AMOSA), but in **demoting a complete optimizer to a seeder**. Three mutually supporting design decisions and their "whys":

- **Short-chain design (40–50 iterations per chain)**: Controls initialization overhead. Short chains mean each chain can only perform local annealing convergence, but multiple chains starting from different random points collectively cover different segments of the frontier. This aligns better with the positioning of "seeding" rather than "solving" than a single long chain—OASI does not need MOSA to find the optimal solution, only to pull the sampling quality close to the frontier.
- **Full archive accumulation**: As mentioned, rejected samples provide supervision on dominated regions for the GP, allowing the surrogate model to have calibrated uncertainty estimates for the entire objective space (not just near the frontier), which directly improves the quality of early EHVI decisions.
- **Objective-aware does not mean frontier stacking**: If seeds are filtered only by Pareto dominance, initial points will all crowd near the frontier, causing design space coverage to collapse, and the GP variance will go out of control in regions far from the frontier, rendering the exploration component of the acquisition function ineffective. Maximin subset selection is an intentional compromise—first use objective information to raise archive quality, then use diversity criteria to ensure coverage, preserving the structure of both spaces (objective space and design space).

The paper summarizes the essential difference between OASI and previous initialization methods through the perspective of "surrogate conditioning": LHS/Sobol determines "where the initial points are," while OASI determines "what the initial GP looks like." In the TinyML setting where SRAM violations are common and budgets are extremely tight, poor early surrogate conditioning causes the acquisition function to make misleading proposals and hinders convergence; OASI corrects this without expanding the budget.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Resource Tri-Separation and Deployability Index

The second innovation is the resource handling system贯穿 optimization and deployment: SRAM as a hard constraint during optimization (Eq. 5), Flash as an objective (sister term to Eq. 4), latency left for hardware-in-the-loop verification (Section IV), and finally using DI to compress 3D resource usage into a deployment readiness scalar that can be compared across boards (Eq. 6). The value of this system is fully demonstrated in Table III: OASI 3 has the highest accuracy (91.0%) but DI is zero on F469 (0.000) and directly OOMs on F401, while OASI 2 achieves the most uniform deployment slack across the three boards with 88.7% accuracy (DI 0.865/0.541/0.132). DI gives the deployment decision of "which point on the frontier to choose" an explicit quantitative basis for the first time, rather than relying on naked-eye inspection of accuracy.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Hardware-in-the-Loop Verification Loop and Breaking the MACC Illusion

The third innovation is more experimental but equally important: compiling int8 quantized models using STM32Cube.AI and executing inference binaries on real STM32 MCUs via the ST Edge AI Developer Cloud, capturing "memory access behaviors and kernel scheduling effects that theoretical FLOPs or MACC cannot reflect" (citing [10]). Key evidence from the experiments (analysis near Table III and Fig. 7): OASI 1 and OASI 2 have identical MACC (6.8 M), but the measured cycle counts and latency on the STM32H7 differ significantly (22.5 ms vs. 14.3 ms)—**two models with the same computational cost run nearly 60% faster/slower, indicating that memory reuse strategies and kernel scheduling are the dominant factors in effective execution cost on MCUs**. Meanwhile, latency and cycle counts show a near-linear relationship, indicating that execution is compute-bound when memory is sufficient. This finding itself has independent value: it proves that NAS or compression methods using MACC/FLOPs as efficiency proxies may systematically select the wrong models in MCU scenarios.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

- **Vs. LHS/Sobol/Random**: The three only guarantee uniform coverage of the design space, turning a blind eye to the structure of the objective space; OASI’s sampling distribution is explicitly shaped by the dual-objective annealing criterion. The GD comparison in Table II (0.000000 vs. 0.003757/0.005563/0.462394) is the direct quantification of this difference.
- **Vs. Meta-learning warm-starts like MI-SMBO**: Meta-learning methods rely on external task priors or historical metadata for transfer; OASI generates seeds entirely within the current problem, requiring no cross-task data, making it applicable to a wider range (at the cost of requiring true training every time, see Limitations).
- **Vs. NSGA-II**: Evolutionary methods operate directly on populations and require no surrogate models, but the non-dominated sorting overhead of 1389.5 s per iteration (Table I) makes them uneconomical in TinyML scenarios where training is expensive; moreover, their random operators struggle to converge to the boundary region that is "compact and reasonably accurate." OASI-MOBO takes 164.0 s per iteration, almost identical to random-initialized MOBO’s 164.2 s.
- **Vs. MOSA used alone as an optimizer**: MOSA alone achieves only 87.9% accuracy (lowest in Table I), but takes only 147.5 s per iteration and 150.0 s for initialization—cheap and with strong exploration capabilities. The combination logic of OASI is to leverage strengths and avoid weaknesses: let MOSA provide seeds (using its exploration), let GP-EHVI perform refinement (using its sample efficiency), resulting in the best accuracy of 90.1% and Tchebycheff value of 0.0040 among the four.
- **Vs. Conventional warm-start**: General warm-starts only provide a starting point for the optimizer; OASI acts directly on the conditioning of the surrogate model, changing the statistical foundation of the entire subsequent search.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Google Speech Commands v2 (GSC), taking 10 balanced classes, with training/validation/test splits of 8k/1k/1k audio clips. Audio is uniformly normalized to 1.0 second, 16 kHz sampling rate, mapped to 40-dimensional log-Mel features (25 ms window, 10 ms frame shift). The backbone network is a Depthwise Separable CNN (DS-CNN, Fig. 3): standard convolutions are decomposed into depthwise spatial filtering plus pointwise projection, reducing parameter count and MACs while maintaining accuracy, supporting low-latency TinyML inference—the motivation for choosing this backbone is that it is the de facto standard compact architecture for KWS edge deployment. The search space is $h \in \{d \in \{1,2,3\}, w \in [16,64], k \in \{3\times3, 5\times5\}, f \in \{1,2,3\}, BN, Dropout\}$, with the paper emphasizing that these value ranges "reflect embedded constraints." Training configuration: Adam optimizer (learning rate 1e-3, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$), batch size 64, up to 100 epochs, early stopping patience 10. Optimization is performed on an RTX A4000 GPU, and all deployment metrics are measured only on the target MCU. Code and implementation are open source (github.com/sumansamui/OASI-MOBO-TinyML_KWS).

### Definitions and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Top-1 Validation Accuracy and Model Size**: Basic metrics for the task.
- **Tchebycheff Scalarization Value $J(h)$** (equal weights $w_1 = w_2 = 0.5$): Compresses the bi-objective trade-off into a single scalar to rank a unique "best compromise solution" among methods. Tchebycheff is chosen over weighted sum because it can reach all points on the Pareto frontier (including non-convex segments); smaller $J$ is better.
- **Hypervolume (HV)**: The volume of the objective space dominated by the current solution set relative to a reference point, measuring both convergence and distribution; larger is better—the standard comprehensive metric for multi-objective optimization.
- **Generational Distance (GD)**: The average distance from the solution set to the reference Pareto frontier, a pure convergence metric; smaller is better. OASI achieves GD = 0.000000, meaning the final solutions lie completely on the reference frontier.
- **Kruskal–Wallis Test** (non-parametric, multi-group comparison): A test for differences between methods suitable for small samples without assuming normal distribution, reporting H statistic, p-value, and effect size $\eta^2$.
- **Hardware-in-the-Loop Metrics**: Measured latency on three MCUs (STM32H7 600 MHz, STM32F469 180 MHz, STM32F401 84 MHz) (Table III), measured cycle counts on STM32H7S78-DK (Fig. 7), peak SRAM, Flash, MACC, parameter count, and DI for each board calculated by Eq. 6.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Optimizer-Level Comparison (Table I, same total budget T, Top-1 selected via Tchebycheff, smaller J is better)**: OASI-MOBO achieves 90.1% accuracy, 0.103 MB size, J = 0.0040, comprehensively optimal; NSGA-II is 88.9%, 0.210 MB, J = 0.0127 (second highest accuracy but double the size); MOSA used alone is 87.9%, 0.114 MB, J = 0.0144; Random-initialized MOBO is 88.5%, 0.120 MB, J = 0.0145. In terms of computational overhead: NSGA-II is the most expensive at 1389.5 s per iteration (non-dominated sorting grows quadratically with population), MOSA is the cheapest at 147.5 s, and MOBO at 164.2 s is almost identical to OASI-MOBO at 164.0 s—indicating that OASI’s gains do not come from overhead in the search phase, but purely from higher-quality initial conditioning. In the initialization overhead column: OASI-MOBO is 1934.9 s, higher than MOBO’s 1501.8 s (about 29% higher, directly calculable from Table I numbers); the paper’s defense is that "the extra cost is fully justified by significantly better accuracy-size trade-offs." The behavioral diagnosis of NSGA-II is also worth recording: it can fill the frontier, but the randomness of crossover and mutation makes it difficult to converge to boundary points that are compact and reasonably accurate.

**Initialization-Level Controlled Comparison (Table II, fixed MOBO, only swapping initialization)**: In terms of HV, OASI is highest at 0.062748, followed by Sobol 0.059255, Random 0.056773, LHS 0.056336; in terms of GD, OASI is lowest at 0.000000, followed by Sobol 0.003757, Random 0.005563, while LHS is as high as 0.462394—two orders of magnitude worse than random initialization. The paper does not provide a specific explanation for this anomaly; the author cautiously speculates that LHS’s stratified uniform coverage causes initial points to be far from the frontier in the objective space and regularly distributed, and the GP posterior’s mean reversion effect may cause the acquisition function to linger in low-quality regions longer; however, this is interpretation, not a paper conclusion. In the time column: LHS/Random 1501.79 s, Sobol 1562.11 s, OASI 1934.88 s; OASI’s initialization overhead is about 29% higher, in exchange for an HV improvement of about 8–11% (relative to LHS/Random) and GD dropping to zero. Qualitative observation in Fig. 4: LHS, Random, and Sobol spread points across the space but ignore objectives, with the surrogate model starting from uninformative data; OASI’s seeds are guided by accuracy and size, giving the surrogate a stronger starting point. Fig. 5 shows the progression curves of best validation accuracy and hypervolume over time for each strategy. On the comprehensive objective score J curve in Fig. 6a, OASI achieves and maintains the lowest value, Sobol shows progressive improvement but remains higher than OASI, LHS flattens at a high level, and Random is worst throughout; the distribution plot in Fig. 6b shows that OASI’s J distribution is tighter (smaller variance between runs), contrasting with the large variance of LHS and Random—this corresponds to the paper’s claim of "convergence stability."

**Statistical Tests**: Kruskal–Wallis test H = 5.40, p = 0.144, $\eta^2$ = 0.0007, not reaching conventional significance levels. The paper’s handling of this is honest: acknowledging modest statistical separation, but emphasizing that OASI is the only method to achieve GD = 0 and consistently lower dispersion. In other words, the claim retreats from "significantly better mean" to "deterministic advantage in stability and frontier hit rate."

### Findings from Ablation Studies

The paper does not perform architecture-level ablations (e.g., removing a component of DS-CNN); its corresponding ablation design is the initialization controlled experiment in Section IV: **fixing the MOBO surrogate model, acquisition function, and budget, only switching initialization $\in \{LHS, Sobol, Random, OASI\}$** (Table II and Fig. 4/5/6). This is a clean ablation of the single variable "initialization," and the findings can be summarized in four points: (1) The advantage of objective-aware initialization over objective-agnostic initialization is consistent in direction across HV, GD, and J curves, and manifests as stability in the form of reduced variance between runs (tighter distribution in Fig. 6b); (2) Sobol is competitive early on but then enters a plateau ("Sobol plateaus despite competitive early behavior"), indicating that the uniformity dividend of low-discrepancy sequences saturates as the budget is exhausted; (3) LHS flattens at a high level in J and is anomalously poor in GD (0.462394), being the worst in convergence among the four (the paper attributes this to uninformative initial data); (4) Random is worst overall. Additionally, the single row for MOSA in the optimizer-level comparison (Table I) actually constitutes another layer of ablation: the same annealing sampler, used alone as an optimizer (87.9%) vs. used as a seeder (OASI-MOBO 90.1%) differs by 2.2 percentage points, directly proving the value of the "annealing exploration + surrogate refinement" division of labor. As for the sensitivity ablation of OASI’s own hyperparameters (number of chains, temperature, cooling rate, seed set size n), the paper does not report it.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed OASI**: The first MOSA-based MOBO initialization strategy for TinyML KWS, explicitly considering the accuracy-model size trade-off. The core methodological contribution is repositioning multi-objective simulated annealing from an optimizer to a seeder, directly improving surrogate model conditioning through Pareto-biased sampling.
2. **Initialization Comparison Conclusions under Low Budget**: Proven via controlled experiments that objective-aware initialization leads to more stable convergence and higher-quality Pareto frontiers, outperforming objective-agnostic strategies like LHS, Sobol sequences, and random sampling—under the same extremely small evaluation budget, OASI’s GD is constantly zero (Table II), and its Tchebycheff value of 0.0040 is only one-third of the sub-optimal method (Table I).
3. **Hardware-in-the-Loop Verification**: HIL experiments on STM32 microcontrollers prove that models selected by OASI can converge stably and deploy successfully (e.g., OASI 2 is feasible on NUCLEO-F401RE with 51.0 KB RAM / 62.8 KB Flash), while objective-agnostic initialization leads to undeployable (OOM) models.
4. **Deployability Index DI** (Eq. 6): A multiplicative quantitative deployment readiness metric that penalizes the saturation of any single resource dimension, making the deployment decision of "which point on the frontier to choose" explicit.
5. **Empirical Insights into MCU Execution Costs**: Two models with the same MACC have a measured latency difference of nearly 60% (OASI 1 and OASI 2, Table III), proving that memory reuse and kernel scheduling dominate effective execution costs—this serves as a warning to all automatic design methods that use FLOPs/MACC as efficiency proxies.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Dual-objective cropping removes the third dimension**: Optimization only explicitly handles accuracy and Flash, with SRAM as a constraint and latency excluded from objectives. This means only a 2D frontier is obtained, and points on the frontier may have poor latency (OASI 3 on F469 is 548.3 ms, OOM on F401 is an instance); MOBO itself has no mechanism to avoid selecting it, and it ultimately relies on post-hoc filtering by DI. For latency-sensitive applications (e.g., wake-up scenarios requiring low response), the 2D frontier may be systematically biased.
- **Initialization overhead is not truly zero**: The abstract claims "no extra optimization cost," which is only true in the sense of evaluation budget (number of trainings); in wall-clock time, OASI’s initialization overhead is 1934.88 s, about 29% higher than LHS/Random’s 1501.79 s (directly calculable from Table II), because every accept/reject evaluation on the MOSA chain requires a true model training. Furthermore, the exact definition of "budget" (whether all evaluations on the chain count toward the total budget T) is not fully clarified in the paper; from Table I numbers, initialization overhead is listed separately, so readers can only infer that budget refers to the number of evaluations rather than time.
- **New hyperparameters introduce new sensitivities**: Specific values for the number of chains $N_{chains}$, iterations per chain (paper only gives a range of 40–50), dual initial temperatures, cooling rates, and seed set size $n$ are not reported; reproducibility relies on open-source code; these quantities themselves may need tuning per task, carrying the risk of "swapping one layer of hyperparameters for another," and the paper does not perform this sensitivity analysis.
- **Missing GP details**: The search space is discrete-hybrid (BN is a boolean switch, kernel size is discrete levels, width is a continuous interval); how the GP kernel function is chosen and handles categorical variables is not explained; under TinyML’s small sample conditions, kernel selection has a non-negligible impact on results.
- **DI depends on budget values**: How the upper limits $B^{(R)}$, $B^{(F)}$, $B^{(T)}$ for each board in Eq. 6 are set (hardware nominal values or application requirement values) is not reported in the paper; DI is a relative quantity, and the absolute values of conclusions will change with a different set of budget numbers.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Insufficient statistical power is the biggest weakness**: Kruskal–Wallis test p = 0.144, effect size $\eta^2$ = 0.0007, differences between methods are not significant, and the effect size is almost zero; the paper does not report the number of repeated runs and random seeds, casting doubt on the test power under small samples. The paper itself can only retreat to the qualitative claim of "GD = 0 and lower dispersion."
- **Single dataset, single backbone**: All conclusions are built on the 10-class subset of GSC v2 plus a single DS-CNN backbone. It is unknown whether this holds for other KWS architectures (TC-ResNet, BC-ResNet, FSMN series) or larger vocabulary tasks; the accuracy range of 10 classes, Top-1 88–91% is also relatively narrow, with limited discriminative power.
- **Construction of the reference Pareto frontier is not explained**: The interpretability of GD = 0 completely depends on how the reference frontier was generated (merge of solutions from all methods? independent dense sampling?), which is not disclosed in the paper; the reference point value for HV is also not reported, making the absolute values of these two core metrics unreproducible independently.
- **Inconsistent口径 across tables**: The Size (MB) in Table I (OASI-MOBO 0.103 MB) does not match the Flash (KB) in Table III (OASI 1 with the same 90.1% accuracy is 73.2 KB); the paper does not explain the conversion relationship (speculation: the former is the model file, the latter is the image including compiled code, or one is float32 and the other int8, but this is purely reader speculation).
- **Missing key ablations**: Maximin diversity selection vs. directly taking the Pareto optimal subset of the archive, full archive (including rejected solutions) vs. using only accepted solutions, and scanning the seed set size $n$—these ablations that directly test the "why" of design decisions were not performed.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Incorporate latency into optimization without relying on per-step HIL**: Train a latency/cycle prediction surrogate using already measured hardware data (e.g., a cost model with architecture features and memory footprint as inputs), achieving 3D or 4D objective MOBO; or use a GP classifier to learn the SRAM feasible region, converting the hard constraint into a feasibility probability incorporated into the acquisition function (feasibility-aware EHVI), moving deployment awareness from "post-hoc DI filtering" to "endogenous optimization."
- **Compress initialization overhead**: Replace complete training on MOSA chains with low-fidelity evaluations (fewer epoch training, weight-sharing supernetwork evaluation, early stopping based on learning curve extrapolation), compressing the initialization overhead back to the level of space-filling methods while retaining Pareto bias.
- **OASI’s own hyperparameter adaptation and meta-learning**: Adaptively set the number of chains, temperature scheduling, and $n$ based on budget and problem dimension; or perform meta-learning transfer of seeds produced by OASI on historical tasks (combining with the MI-SMBO approach) to further reduce initialization costs on new tasks.
- **Expand empirical basis**: Cross-validation on multiple datasets (full 35 classes of GSC, multilingual command words), multiple backbones, and multiple boards, reporting seed counts, confidence intervals, and test power analysis, upgrading the "stability advantage" from a qualitative claim to a statistical conclusion.
- **Application of DI**: Use DI directly as an automatic selection criterion in the deployment phase (given a target board, select the frontier point with the largest DI), and study the correlation between DI and actual deployment success rates under different budget settings—the paper provides the index definition but has not yet validated its predictive efficacy.
