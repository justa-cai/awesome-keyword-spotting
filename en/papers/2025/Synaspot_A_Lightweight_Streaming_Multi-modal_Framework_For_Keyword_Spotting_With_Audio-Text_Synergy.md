## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is responsible for continuously detecting the occurrence of preset keywords in a continuous audio stream, serving as a critical entry point for human-computer interaction systems. Since KWS is typically deployed on resource-constrained devices, models must be compact and low-latency, driving the continuous evolution of small-sized, streaming-capable KWS architectures.

Based on how keywords are specified, KWS systems are divided into two main categories. The first is customized KWS, such as factory-fused device wake-up words like "Ok Google." In these systems, the target word is locked during the training phase, allowing for extreme accuracy and efficiency, but users cannot change them. The second is open-vocabulary KWS, which allows users to specify keywords on demand (also known as user-defined keyword spotting). This approach is much more flexible and is the research object of this paper.

There are three technical routes for enrollment (i.e., "telling the system what word to detect") in open-vocabulary KWS: audio-based enrollment (users read an example once, matched using DTW or metric learning), text-based enrollment (inputting text, matched using phoneme-level text representations), and multi-modal enrollment combining both. Existing work has shown that multi-modal enrollment features can simultaneously enhance the expressive power and accuracy of KWS models; cross-modal alignment features provide overall gains; using audio alignment as auxiliary supervision can further improve performance (citation [4]); and combining multiple alignment modalities brings additional representation benefits (citation [6]). In other words, "multi-modal is useful" has been repeatedly verified in academia.

However, the pain point lies precisely after "usefulness": **the increase in parameter count brought by multi-modal approaches, along with the constraints of end-to-end deployment, limits the practical usability of such models.** This is the core contradiction explicitly pointed out in the paper's abstract—academia has demonstrated the advantages of multi-modal approaches, but industry cannot fit them into a truly streaming, truly lightweight edge-side system. The stance of this Alibaba team is very engineering-oriented: they do not want another offline benchmark-brushing multi-modal KWS, but a product-level solution that can run with millisecond-level latency on real speech streams.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper梳理 (reviews) the structural deficiencies of existing methods from two dimensions: the "enrollment side" and the "discrimination side."

**Deficiencies on the Enrollment Side:**
- Pure audio enrollment (DTW [1,2], metric learning [3]) relies on users recording example audio. The enrollment threshold is high, the effect directly depends on the quality of the examples, and acoustic condition changes (near/far field, noise, accents) can cause matching to fail.
- Pure text enrollment ([4,5,6]) has a low threshold but loses the user's personal pronunciation features. In some scenarios (e.g., Chinese-English mixed, polyphonic characters), the mapping from text to pronunciation itself is ambiguous.
- While multi-modal enrollment is beneficial, existing implementations have high parameter costs: InfoNCE [5] and CLAD [5] in Table 1 both have 2.2M parameters, and MM-KWS [4] in Table 2 reaches 3.9M parameters—for an entry-level model that resides on a device and listens continuously, this cost is unacceptable.

**Deficiencies on the Discrimination Side (Decision Side):**
- Many existing decision methods rely on **non-streaming end-to-end models**, such as using GRU plus fully connected layers for discrimination ([7,8,9]). These models require the input audio segment to have **precisely aligned word boundaries**—you must first cut out the keyword exactly before feeding it to the model. In real streaming scenarios, the system does not know where the keyword starts and ends frame-by-frame, making this premise invalid.
- On the other hand, many lightweight streaming models ([10,11]) **only support customized KWS and lack open-vocabulary capabilities**—lightweight and flexible attributes are mutually exclusive in existing work.
- To bypass the boundary issue, some work proposes **fixed-window streaming schemes** ([12]), performing end-to-end decisions on fixed-length windows. However, this design leads to significant performance fluctuations when keyword lengths vary: if the window is set too short, long words are truncated; if set too long, short words' context is diluted. Moreover, fixed windows mean processing the entire audio segment each time, resulting in higher computational load and memory usage (Section 3.6 experiments confirm this: MM-KWS using a 2-second window sees EER on WenetPhrase-Hard degrade from 26.23% with exact boundaries to 39.15%, Table 2).

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Compressing the above contradictions into one sentence, the key challenge this paper aims to solve is: **How to make an open-vocabulary KWS system simultaneously satisfy three previously mutually exclusive attributes—(1) flexible enrollment supporting audio, text, and audio-text hybrid modalities; (2) true streaming inference, not relying on word boundaries or fixed windows, capable of directly processing variable-length keywords; (3) parameter count controlled within 1M (ultimately achieving 0.9M), significantly smaller than existing open-vocabulary solutions (2.2M～3.9M).** The essence of the challenge is: the benefits of multi-modal approaches are usually obtained by stacking encoders and discrimination networks, while streaming lightweight constraints require moving most of the computation of "multi-modal" and "discrimination" off the online path, incurring costs only during the offline enrollment phase.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

As shown in Figure 1 of the paper, Synaspot consists of two stages: **the training stage** and **the inference and decoding stage**. This two-stage split is itself the most important design decision in the entire paper, with the motivation being **to move computation from online to offline**:

- In the training stage, three things must be learned: a speaker-independent audio encoder, a text encoder, and a mixed-modal embedding generator, using contrastive learning to align the three modalities into a shared embedding space.
- In the inference stage, the system first calculates three enrollment embeddings (audio embedding, text embedding, mixed embedding) using the enrollment audio and text, and **caches** them. The paper explicitly states that these embeddings are "fixed once verified" and do not need to be recalculated. Thus, the **only model that needs to run on the online path is the audio encoder**—waveforms are fed in continuously in small chunks, frame-level audio embeddings are calculated frame-by-frame, and a **parameter-free mathematical decoder** produces keyword scores for each frame (the decoding part in Figure 1 explicitly marks the "No Params" module).

Why split it this way? Because the overhead of multi-modal approaches is concentrated entirely in the enrollment phase, which is one-time, offline, and can be manually verified; while on the streaming path, there is only a DFSMN encoder plus pure mathematical operations, which is both lightweight and naturally supports keywords of any length—decoding is window aggregation based on frame-level similarity, without needing to know where the word boundaries are. This simultaneously solves the two deadlocks of "multi-modal parameter inflation" and "dependency on boundary alignment."

The audio encoder uses an L-layer DFSMN (Deep Feedforward Sequence Memory Network, citation [13]), taking FBank frames as input and outputting frame-level audio embeddings. The paper does not specifically argue why DFSMN was chosen, but it can be inferred from its attributes (Note: this is a note inference, not from the paper text): DFSMN models temporal dependencies using feedforward memory blocks, has no recurrent structure, has controllable latency and simple state management when inferring chunk-by-chunk, and has high parameter efficiency, making it a mature choice for KWS edge deployment (consistent with the industrial KWS deployment tradition of the cFSMN series). The specific configuration is L=7 layers, hidden dimension 256 (Section 3.2).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**(1) AAM Loss for Phoneme Classifier (Function 1).** A phoneme classifier is attached to the frame-level embeddings output by the audio encoder to predict labels frame-by-frame. To reduce phoneme confusion and false positives, the paper adopts the Additive Angular Margin (AAM) loss (citation [14]):

$$L_{ph} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{e^{s\cos(\theta_{y_i}+m)}}{e^{s\cos(\theta_{y_i}+m)}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq y_i}^{l}e^{s\cos(\theta_j)}}$$

where $\theta_j$ is the angle between the model weight vector $w_j$ and the input $x_i$, $s$ is the scaling parameter, and $m$ is the angular margin penalty. Translated into action: standard softmax only requires the logit of the correct phoneme to be the largest, while AAM additionally requires the angle of the correct class to "rotate" by a fixed angle $m$ more, forcing the encoder to pull the embeddings of different phonemes apart in the angle space with a hard margin. Why do this? Because false positives in KWS mostly come from phoneme substitutions with similar pronunciations (the LPH difficult negative sample set is precisely composed of phonetically/visually similar words selected by Levenshtein distance). Pulling apart the phoneme spacing at the source in the embedding space is equivalent to cutting off a large batch of false positive sources before downstream detection. Note that the values of $s$ and $m$ are not reported in the paper.

**(2) Gradient Reversal for Speaker Disentanglement (Function 2).** To strip speaker features (voiceprint information) from speech, the paper attaches a speaker classifier to the audio encoder and **reverses its gradient** (the approach of Domain Adversarial Neural Network, DANN, citation [15]): the speaker classifier consists of an attentive pooling layer (citation [14]) plus a linear classifier, optimized using cross-entropy $L_{vp}$. The two objectives are combined:

$$L_{audio} = \alpha_A \cdot L_{ph} + \beta_A \cdot L_{vp}$$

The mechanism of gradient reversal is: during the forward pass, the speaker classifier tries to identify the speaker from the embeddings as much as possible; during the backward pass, the gradient is negated and passed back to the encoder, so the encoder is trained to "make the speaker classifier guess as poorly as possible." The effect is that sentences with the same text content but spoken by different speakers produce similar embeddings. Why is this step necessary? Because enrollment audio is read by a specific user. If the embedding retains voiceprint information, the enrollment embedding becomes bound to "this person," and if another speaker reads the same word, it will not match—for open-vocabulary KWS, the enrollment embedding must capture "what the word is" rather than "who is speaking." Attentive pooling aggregates frame-level embeddings into an utterance-level vector before judging the speaker (Note inference: this is the standard practice of compressing frame-level representations into utterance-level discrimination, the paper does not explain the motivation separately).

**(3) Text and Mixed Embedding Modeling (Functions 3-5).** The text embedding $E_T$ is obtained using an embedding layer plus LSTM (citation [16])—LSTM processes variable-length phoneme/character sequences (Motivation inferred from notes: enrollment text length varies, requiring sequence encoding). The mixed embedding $E_M$ is constructed using a cross-attention layer: **$E_T$ acts as query, $E_A$ acts as key and value** (marked as Cross-Domain Attention + AddNorm + Linear + Biased Module in Figure 1). Intuitively: use text to "query" audio, fusing the semantic skeleton of the text with the acoustic details of the audio into a mixed representation. Why let text be the query? Text is a compact semantic sequence, while audio frames are numerous and redundant. Using text as the query allows extracting acoustic information related to each text unit from the audio on demand (the paper does not explicitly argue the direction choice; this is an interpretation based on structure).

The three modalities are aligned in a shared embedding space using contrastive learning (InfoNCE form, sim is cosine similarity on L2-normalized embeddings, $\tau$ is temperature):

$$L_{cl}^{at} = -\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp(sim(E_T^i, E_A^i)/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(sim(E_T^j, E_A^j)/\tau)}, \quad L_{cl}^{am} = -\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp(sim(E_M^i, E_A^i)/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(sim(E_M^j, E_A^j)/\tau)}$$

That is, text-audio alignment uses one loss, and mixed-audio alignment uses another loss (note that both contrastive terms use $E_A$ as the anchor; the audio embedding is the "public coordinate system" of the shared space, and the audio encoder trained in Section 2.1.1 is reused and jointly fine-tuned here). The total objective:

$$L_{mixed} = \alpha_M \cdot L_{ph} + \beta_M \cdot L_{cl}^{at} + \gamma_M \cdot L_{cl}^{am}$$

Why use contrastive learning instead of direct regression alignment? The paper cites the conclusion from [5]: cross-modal alignment features can overall enhance performance; the in-batch negative sample mechanism of InfoNCE恰好 (just happens to) provide the "discriminative power" most needed by KWS—not only aligning positive pairs, but also pushing apart embeddings of different words, which directly corresponds to negative sample suppression in detection tasks.

**(4) Streaming Mathematical Decoding (Functions 6-7).** After calculating the streaming audio embedding $E_W$ in the online phase, first calculate the frame-level similarity $p_{ij}$ between it and each enrollment embedding $E_{Enroll}$ ($Enroll \in \{E_A, E_T, E_M\}$). Since the raw scores are noisy, perform **causal smoothing** (sliding average, citation [17]):

$$p'_{ij} = \frac{1}{j - h_{smooth} + 1}\sum_{k=h_{smooth}}^{j}p_{ik}, \quad h_{smooth} = \max\{1, j - w_{smooth} + 1\}$$

Then, within the causal scoring window, aggregate the "optimal similarity of each enrollment unit" to obtain the confidence score for the $j$-th frame:

$$Score = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\max_{h_{scoring}\leq k\leq j}p'_{ik}, \quad h_{scoring} = \max\{1, j - w_{scoring} + 1\}$$

where $n$ is the number of frames of the enrollment embedding (the enrollment keyword is cut into $n$ units). Translated into action: each unit of the enrollment word "finds its optimal matching frame" in the recent scoring window, and then the average is taken over all units. Why use max instead of frame-by-frame alignment summation? Because in streaming audio, enrollment units and actual pronunciation frames do not need to correspond one-to-one (different speaking speeds, presence or absence of pauses). Max tolerates elastic offsets on the time axis, equivalent to a soft sliding alignment (the paper does not detail the argument; this is a note interpretation); this also explains why this scheme naturally supports **variable-length keywords**—$n$ is the length of each enrollment word itself, and the decoding formula is independent of word length. The values of the two window sizes $w_{smooth}$ and $w_{scoring}$ are not reported in the paper.

Finally, depending on the enrollment modality, obtain $Score_A$ (pure audio), $Score_T$ (pure text), or the score-level fusion $Score_{All} = \alpha_S \cdot Score_A + \beta_S \cdot Score_T + \gamma_S \cdot Score_M$.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Speaker-Independent Audio Encoder (AAM Margin + Gradient Reversal Voiceprint Adversarial)

This innovation turns the premise that "enrollment audio can be used" from an engineering assumption into a training objective. It solves problems at two levels simultaneously: AAM loss pulls apart the boundaries of easily confused phonemes from the perspective of **phoneme discriminability** (directly striking LPH-type phonetically similar negative samples), while gradient reversal strips voiceprint from the perspective of **speaker invariance** (directly striking "enrollment-person dependency"). Ablation experiments (Table 3) confirm the weight of this design: after removing the speaker classifier, the EER on LibriPhrase-Easy degraded from 5.77% to 8.85% (+3.08 percentage points), and on LibriPhrase-Hard from 27.29% to 32.04% (+4.75 percentage points), and AUC dropped from 79.15% to 72.90%—it was the harshest drop among all ablation items.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Three-Modal Shared Embedding Space Anchored on Audio and Mixed-Modal Enrollment

Text embeddings, mixed embeddings, and audio embeddings are pinned into the same shared space with $E_A$ as the public coordinate system by two InfoNCE losses. The mixed embedding (cross-attention querying audio with text) is the most information-rich of the three enrollment modalities: text provides the semantic skeleton, and enrollment audio provides personalized acoustic details. The benefits are shown in Table 1: the full version of Synaspot (TA three-modal) reduced EER on LPE from 7.07% to 5.77% and on LPH from 28.69% to 27.29% compared to Synaspot-AT which only used text enrollment; Table 3 ablation shows that removing the mixed embedding caused LPE EER to rebound from 5.77% to 7.07%. Another layer of value is **enrollment flexibility**: users read once if they have audio, type if they don't have audio, and use mixed mode for highest precision if they have both—all three modes share the same 0.9M online model.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Parameter-Free Streaming Mathematical Decoder

This is the most "counter-trend" design in the paper. Mainstream approaches train another discrimination network (GRU+FC) or use fixed-window end-to-end decisions, but Synaspot degrades decoding entirely into **similarity calculation + causal smoothing + window max aggregation + score weighted fusion**, these four pure mathematical operations, with zero parameters at the decoding end (Figure 1 "No Params"). Why dare to do this? Because the alignment work has already been completed by contrastive learning in the training phase—the shared embedding space ensures that embeddings of correct words and corresponding audio embeddings are frame-by-frame similar, while embeddings of incorrect words are not. The decoder only needs to "measure distance" without needing to "learn how to measure." The benefits are triple: (1) Parameters are saved (compared to MM-KWS's 3.9M, Synaspot only needs 0.9M, Table 2); (2) No word boundaries or fixed windows are needed, directly supporting variable-length keywords; (3) Decoding is causal and frame-by-frame, scores can be output as soon as a chunk arrives, and latency is determined only by window size.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Differences with several representative methods can be listed in a comparison table:

- **Comparison with non-streaming end-to-end discrimination (CMCD [9], MM-KWS [4], GRU+FC types [7,8])**: These methods require exact word boundaries or large windows. Although CMCD has only 0.7M parameters (smaller than Synaspot), it only supports text enrollment, and under streaming conditions (the paper simulates streaming by randomly splicing speech segments before and after each sample of LibriPhrase, Section 3.3), the EER is LPE 8.42% / LPH 32.90%, while Synaspot wins comprehensively with 5.77% / 27.29% (Table 1). MM-KWS can achieve WPE EER of 4.24% under exact boundaries (better than Synaspot's 14.56%), but degrades to 18.38% with a 2-second fixed window, and 11.51% with a 1.5-second window (Table 2), showing extreme sensitivity to window configuration; Synaspot has no window concept, and its 14.56% score was achieved under true streaming conditions, with only 23% of the parameters of MM-KWS.
- **Comparison with multi-modal contrastive learning schemes (InfoNCE [5], CLAD [5])**: Both are contrastive learning routes, but both have 2.2M parameters and do not support streaming. Synaspot uses 0.9M (-59% parameters) and improves EER on LPE by 2.88 percentage points compared to CLAD (8.65% → 5.77%, Table 1), indicating that the route of cutting off the discrimination network and compressing all alignment into the shared space wins in the parameter-performance trade-off.
- **Comparison with lightweight streaming KWS ([10,11])**: These models are streaming and lightweight but lock onto a single wake-up word, lacking open-vocabulary capabilities. Synaspot proves that open-vocabulary does not necessarily sacrifice streaming and lightweight attributes.
- **Comparison with fixed-window streaming open-vocabulary (w-CTC alignment scheme [12])**: Both are streaming open-vocabulary, but [12] relies on fixed-length windows for end-to-end decisions, leading to large performance fluctuations when word lengths change; Synaspot's frame-by-frame mathematical decoding is insensitive to word length.
- **Enrollment Modality Dimension**: Most of the above methods only support single-modal enrollment (the EM column in baselines in Table 1 and Table 2 is almost all T), while Synaspot is one of the few to simultaneously provide A/T/TA three enrollment modes and report results separately (Synaspot-AA / Synaspot-AT / Synaspot full version).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

- **English Training Data**: Two subsets of LibriSpeech (citation [20]), train-clean-100 and train-clean-360 (Section 3.2). Totaling approximately 460 hours (this hour count is common knowledge for LibriSpeech corpus, not annotated in the paper text).
- **English Evaluation Data**: LibriPhrase (citation [9]), a phrase dataset extracted from LibriSpeech, with the evaluation part derived from the train-others-500 subset. Negative samples are divided into easy set (LPE) and hard set (LPH) based on Levenshtein distance (citation [21]), with the hard set containing many phonetically or visually similar words. The specific number of positive and negative samples is not reported in the paper (need to check the original LibriPhrase paper [9]).
- **Chinese Evaluation Data**: WenetPhrase (citation [4]), containing two subsets WenetPhrase-Easy (WPE) and WenetPhrase-Hard (WPH), used to verify Mandarin generalization. The scale is not reported in the paper.
- **Streaming Simulation**: To simulate real streaming scenarios, speech segments are randomly spliced before and after each audio of WPE/WPH as acoustic buffers (Section 3.1); LibriPhrase evaluation also randomly supplements audio segments before and after each sample (Section 3.3). That is, all reported scores are measured under the condition of being "buried in a continuous speech stream," not cut isolated words.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Two metrics: **EER** (Equal Error Rate, the error rate when false alarm rate and miss rate are equal) and **AUC** (Area Under the ROC Curve). Rationale for selection (note analysis): Open-vocabulary KWS is essentially a similarity detection task of "enrollment embedding vs. streaming audio frames." The decision threshold needs to be adjusted according to product false alarm/miss preferences. EER and AUC are both **threshold-independent** global metrics, capable of fairly comparing the overall discriminative power of different scorers,不受 (unaffected by) bias at a specific operating point. This also aligns with the evaluation protocols of baseline works (CMCD, CLAD, etc.), ensuring comparability. The paper does not report operating point metrics (e.g., recall at fixed false alarm rate), nor does it report latency or real-time factor.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**English LibriPhrase (Table 1, streaming conditions)**: CMCD [9] (T enrollment, 0.7M): EER 8.42/32.90, AUC 96.70/73.58; Triplet [18] (T, 0.6M): EER 32.75/44.36; SoftTriplet [19] (T, parameters not reported): EER 28.74/41.95; InfoNCE [5] (T, 2.2M): EER 8.99/32.51, AUC 96.85/74.87; CLAD [5] (T, 2.2M): EER 8.65/30.30, AUC 97.03/76.15. Synaspot three variants: Synaspot-AA (A enrollment, 0.9M) EER 8.85/32.14; Synaspot-AT (T enrollment, 0.9M) EER 7.07/28.69, AUC 97.17/77.35; Synaspot full version (TA, 0.9M) **EER 5.77/27.29, AUC 97.34/79.15**. Key points: (1) Compared to the strongest baseline CLAD, with 59% fewer parameters (0.9M vs 2.2M), LPE EER improved relatively by 33% (8.65→5.77) and LPH AUC was 3.00 percentage points higher; (2) The gain of three-modal fusion is clearly visible (AT→TA on LPE 7.07→5.77); (3) Text enrollment is better than audio enrollment (AA 8.85 vs AT 7.07). Additionally, the streaming scores reported in the main text of Section 3.3 are LPE 5.97% EER / 97.19% AUC, LPH 27.71% EER / 78.70% AUC, which has a slight discrepancy with Table 1 (5.77/97.34, 27.29/79.15), and the paper does not explain this (speculation: two batches of tests under different random splicing seeds, note remains skeptical).

**Chinese WenetPhrase (Table 2)**: MM-KWS [4] (T, 3.9M) achieves EER 4.24/26.23, AUC 99.19/79.24 under **exact word boundaries**; the same model degrades to EER 11.51/31.80 with a 1.5-second window, and 18.38/39.15 with a 2-second window—the longer the window, the worse the performance (a 2-second window floods too much context into the decision, diluting short words), and the sensitivity to window configuration is quantitatively confirmed. Synaspot-AA (A, 0.9M) EER 18.46/36.68; Synaspot-AT (T, 0.9M) EER 19.76/39.95; Synaspot (TA, 0.9M) **EER 14.56/34.50, AUC 92.87/70.35**. Key points: (1) Compared to any window-limited version of MM-KWS, Synaspot dominates (vs 2.0s version WPE 18.38→14.56), with only 23% of the parameters; (2) But compared to MM-KWS with **exact boundaries**, there is still a significant gap (WPE 4.24 vs 14.56), indicating that streaming without boundaries comes at the cost of precision, which the paper presents frankly; (3) A detail not discussed by the paper: on Chinese, **audio enrollment surpasses text enrollment** (AA 18.46 vs AT 19.76), opposite to the English ranking, notes speculate this is related to the additional ambiguity of Chinese text-to-pronunciation mapping (polyphonic characters, tones).

**Section 3.6 Summary of Streaming vs. Non-Streaming**: Traditional offline frameworks are sensitive to window length, require processing 2-second audio segments, and have higher computational load and memory usage; streaming architectures input frame-by-frame, have faster inference speed, and gain operational flexibility through dynamic context adaptation. This section is a qualitative discussion, without specific numbers for memory/speed (not reported in the paper).

**Figure 2 Qualitative Analysis**: The similarity matrices between enrollment embeddings of the three modalities and the output of the online encoder are drawn as heatmaps. Positive sample pairs show clear bright areas on the heatmap, while negative samples do not, intuitively verifying the feasibility of the shared embedding space and streaming decoding; Section 3.4 also explains that the final wake-up score is obtained by collaborative integration of the pre-registered x-coordinates of the three modalities.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Table 3 (LibriPhrase, streaming conditions) only performed two ablation cuts, but both hit vital points:

| Configuration | LPE EER | LPH EER | LPE AUC | LPH AUC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Synaspot Full Version | 5.77 | 27.29 | 97.34 | 79.15 |
| Remove Mixed Embedding | 7.07 | 29.09 | 97.04 | 76.85 |
| Remove Speaker Classifier | 8.85 | 32.04 | 95.87 | 72.90 |

Finding 1: **The speaker classifier (gradient reversal) is the largest single contributor**. After removal, LPE EER +3.08, LPH EER +4.75, LPH AUC dropped by 6.25 percentage points. This verifies the judgment that "enrollment embeddings must be speaker-independent"—the harm of residual voiceprint to open-vocabulary KWS is greater than missing a modality. Finding 2: **Mixed embedding contribution is second** (+1.30/+1.80 EER), and after removing it, the system degrades to the level of Synaspot-AT (7.07/29.09 vs 7.07/28.69), indicating that the gain of the full version indeed comes from the mixed modality rather than training randomness. Finding 3: The paper text summarizes that this architecture "achieves speaker-discriminative separation while maintaining robustness to enrollment data variations," meaning the two components one manages discrimination and the other manages robustness, with orthogonal functions. Regret: No ablation on AAM loss (vs standard softmax), causal smoothing window, scoring window size, or score fusion weights. Loss weights $(\alpha_A,\beta_A)=(0.5,0.5)$, $(\alpha_M,\beta_M,\gamma_M)=(0.4,0.3,0.3)$, $(\alpha_S,\beta_S,\gamma_S)=(0.5,0.25,0.25)$ (Section 3.2) were given directly, with no sensitivity analysis reported.

## Main Contributions

The paper self-describes three contributions, summarized below with the author's overall evaluation:

1. **Proposed Synaspot**—a lightweight, streaming, multi-modal KWS framework, fusing audio, text, and audio-text hybrid three enrollment modalities, with a total system parameter count of 0.9M (Tables 1, 2), outperforming baselines on both English and Chinese tasks.
2. **Developed a flexible multi-modal enrollment system**—users can choose audio enrollment (read once), text enrollment (type), or mixed enrollment (provide both) according to the scenario. The three modes share the same online encoder, enrollment embeddings are cached offline, and verified once, used for life.
3. **Verified the feasibility of the "offline multi-modal + online single encoder + zero-parameter mathematical decoding" architecture route**—series experiments in Tables 1/2/3 prove that compressing multi-modal overhead into the offline enrollment phase and simplifying the online path to only similarity operations can reduce parameters from 2.2M～3.9M to 0.9M without sacrificing (and even improving) precision, while breaking free from word boundary and fixed window dependencies.

The author believes its true industry significance lies in demonstrating that "multi-modal" and "edge-side streaming" can be decoupled: the value of multi-modal (expressiveness, discriminative power) does not need to be realized by additional networks on the online path, but can be transferred to a pure mathematical decoder through the design of a shared embedding space. This思路 (approach) has direct transferability to other edge-side multi-modal tasks (e.g., joint edge-side voiceprint + semantic decision).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Gap between streaming and precision**: Under Chinese exact boundary conditions, MM-KWS achieved WPE EER 4.24% (Table 2), while Synaspot streaming achieved 14.56%, a gap of about 10 percentage points; on the English side, although Synaspot streaming leads, that is a relative advantage after baselines were forced into streaming conditions. The flexibility of being boundary-free and window-free is exchanged for discriminative precision, which the paper acknowledges with data.
- **Key hyperparameters not public**: The scaling factor $s$ and angular margin $m$ of AAM loss, smoothing window $w_{smooth}$, scoring window $w_{scoring}$, and specific chunk size (how many frames/milliseconds) are not reported (not reported in the paper), requiring grid search for reproduction.
- **Structural risk of max aggregation** (note analysis, not discussed in the paper): The scoring formula takes the maximum similarity of each enrollment unit within the window and then averages. This "each unit seeks its own optimal match" soft alignment has not been specially analyzed for robustness to omitted sounds, inserted sounds, or obvious pauses in pronunciation. If other phonetically similar words exist within the window, the max operation might pick the wrong frame.
- **The "verification" step of enrollment embeddings is a black box**: The paper says enrollment embeddings are fixed "once verified," but what verification is (manual listening? automatic filtering of poor-quality enrollment audio?) is not elaborated; whether mixed embeddings are dragged down when enrollment audio quality is poor is also not analyzed.
- **Text enrollment depends on text normalization quality**: On Chinese, text enrollment is worse than audio enrollment (Table 2: AT 19.76 vs AA 18.46), exposing the bottleneck of the text-to-pronunciation frontend (polyphonic characters, tones, Chinese-English mixed reading), which the paper does not discuss.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **No quantitative measurement of latency/efficiency**: The entire paper emphasizes lightweight, streaming, and low latency, but does not report any runtime numbers such as RTF, single chunk latency, memory usage, or power consumption (not reported in the paper). 0.9M parameters are only a static proxy metric; the assertion in Section 3.6 that non-streaming schemes have "higher computational load" remains at the qualitative level.
- **Insufficient ablation coverage**: Table 3 only has two ablations (mixed embedding, speaker classifier), with no ablation on AAM, contrastive learning temperature $\tau$, two decoding windows, or the three sets of loss/fusion weights; it is impossible to judge whether weights like 0.4/0.3/0.3 are saturated.
- **Single data domain**: Training and evaluation are all on LibriSpeech-derived corpora (read audiobooks, relatively clean close-talk) and WenetPhrase, with no testing on real device scenarios such as far-field, added noise, reverberation, or multi-speaker overlap; while the selling point of this scheme is exactly edge-side deployment.
- **No statistical significance**: All comparisons are single-run results, with no repetition of multiple random seeds, no confidence intervals; the inconsistency between the numbers in the main text of Section 3.3 and Table 1 (LPH EER 27.71% vs 27.29%) exactly hints at the variance brought by random splicing, which the paper does not handle.
- **Limitations in the definition of difficult negative samples**: LPH is constructed based on Levenshtein distance, covering "phonetically/visually similar" but not covering semantic deception (synonyms with different pronunciations) and actual product false trigger sounds (children, accents, TV broadcasts).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Complete edge-side measurements**: Report chunk latency, RTF, memory, and power consumption on real embedded platforms, and provide quantitative curves of $w_{smooth}/w_{scoring}$ vs. latency-precision, turning the "streaming lightweight" narrative from parameter count to latency narrative.
- **Stronger Chinese text frontend**: Introduce G2P with tone modeling or directly replace the LSTM text encoder with pre-trained phoneme representations, narrowing the gap between text enrollment and audio enrollment on Chinese (Table 2).
- **Enrollment quality adaptation**: Automatically evaluate the quality of enrollment audio and dynamically adjust the fusion weights of the three paths of $Score_{All}$ (currently fixed at 0.5/0.25/0.25), automatically biasing to the text path when enrollment audio is poor.
- **Decoding robustness upgrade**: Replace pure max aggregation with elastic alignment with penalties (causal approximation similar to DTW), handling omitted sounds/pauses while maintaining zero or near-zero parameters.
- **Distillation with self-supervised/foundation models**: Distill phoneme boundary knowledge from large-scale pre-trained speech models into the audio encoder, improving discriminative power for difficult negative samples without increasing online parameters (EER of 27.29% on LPH is still relatively high).
- **Expand negative sample system**: Add false trigger samples from real product logs and cross-speaker evaluation (systematic reports where the enroller and tester are different speakers) to test the effectiveness of gradient reversal for voiceprint disentanglement across channels (close-talk enrollment, far-field testing).
