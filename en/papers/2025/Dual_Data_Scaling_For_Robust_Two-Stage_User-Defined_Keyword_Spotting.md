## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is a resident module in voice entry points such as smart speakers, wireless earbuds, in-car assistants, and wearable devices. The paper begins by highlighting an emerging industrial context: the large-scale deployment of smart devices and conversational terminals (e.g., "XiaoZhi AI" as cited in the paper) creates numerous application scenarios for User-Defined Keyword Spotting (UDKWS) to meet personalized user needs (Introduction, Paragraph 1). Instead of being limited to a few vendor-presets, users can register their preferred wake-up words on the device on-site. This is the source of demand for the entire paper.

There is a fundamental difference in training conditions between predefined keywords and user-defined keywords. Predefined schemes (e.g., Hey Snips) can perform full-shot training on large-scale target word corpora, with positive and negative samples carefully constructed around the target word; this is a mature pipeline in the industry. User-defined schemes lack this condition—when the model goes online, it cannot predict which word the user will register. It must rely on zero-shot capabilities, treating registration information (text transcription or a few audio examples) as query conditions to detect the word in continuous speech. The paper explicitly states that UDKWS suffers from a systematic performance gap relative to predefined schemes precisely because it relies primarily on zero-shot capabilities (Introduction, Paragraph 1).

This gap can be decomposed into three sub-problems with different natures. First is localization: the wake-up word is embedded in continuous speech, and the system must provide its temporal boundaries. Second is discrimination: real speech is full of confusable words that differ from the target word by only one or two phonemes. For example, the paper cites "Hi Snips" and "Hey Snips," which differ by only one vowel (AY1/EY1). Third is generalization: the system must work on-site for words never seen during training, relying solely on registration information. The requirements for model capabilities in these three sub-problems are inconsistent. Satisfying all three simultaneously with a single end-to-end model is the core difficulty in this field and the reason this paper adopts a two-stage architecture.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing approaches into three groups and points out their shortcomings one by one.

The first group consists of traditional ASR solutions: decoding the complete word sequence first and then matching keywords. The paper notes that this approach is computationally expensive and performs poorly on out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words (Introduction, Paragraph 2)—and user-defined words are inherently OOV, making this path nearly unusable for UDKWS.

The second group comprises recent lightweight phoneme-level methods. TDT-KWS [8] extends the standard RNN-T architecture, skipping input frames based on predicted duration to significantly accelerate inference; MFA-KWS [7,9] proposes a CTC streaming decoding algorithm with higher recall than ASR and graph-search baselines; U2-KWS [11] and CaTT-KWS [12] adopt multi-stage frameworks for more precise detection. The paper acknowledges that these methods perform strongly after training on large-scale ASR corpora but points out a common shortcoming: they still require substantial fine-tuning data to achieve optimal performance in actual deployment (Introduction, end of Paragraph 2)—"fine-tuning data" is precisely a scarce resource in user-defined scenarios, as users will not collect a batch of training data for a new word.

The third group includes registration-query methods, categorized by registration modality into QbyE (audio example query), QbyA (audio template coarse matching), QbyT (text registration), and QbyAT (text plus audio multi-modal registration). Lightweight QbyE supports low power and low latency, but QbyT [1,2,16-19] is more stable and performs better with text registration. QbyAT [3,20] has achieved SOTA on LibriPhrase, and [2,3,17,20] indicate that such methods are particularly effective against confusable words. The criticism of this entire family falls on the mismatch between training and inference: these methods are trained on pre-segmented (audio, word) pairs, but inference relies on sliding window detection. The boundaries provided by the sliding window are inaccurate and lead to more false alarms (Introduction, Paragraph 3). Additionally, there is an implicit cost on the registration side: methods like MM-KWS and PLCL rely on large pre-encoders such as DistilBERT, G2P embeddings, or audio-text joint registration modules for anchor registration, incurring significant offline computational overhead (Section 2.2).

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Synthesizing the above pain points, the paper aims to simultaneously satisfy four mutually constraining constraints: (1) Streaming and low overhead—KWS is a resident process and cannot bear a heavy decoder for user-defined words; (2) Accurate localization—candidate boundaries come from acoustic alignment rather than sliding window grids, suppressing false alarms at the source; (3) Discrimination of confusable words—word pairs differing by only one phoneme must be distinguishable; (4) Zero-shot generalization—usable for wake-up words never seen before. The answer provided is "architecture decoupling plus data scaling": using a two-stage structure to separate high-recall streaming detection from high-precision fine-grained verification, and then feeding both stages with dual data scaling. The method design and experimental organization of the entire paper revolve around this main thread.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

DS-KWS is a two-stage serial structure overall (Figure 1). The first stage is the CTC branch: audio is passed through a Conformer audio encoder to obtain an embedding sequence, which is connected to a CTC decoder to output frame-by-frame phoneme token probability distributions. The phoneme search module—a retriever using the streaming CTC decoding algorithm proposed in [7,9]—retrieves candidate paths corresponding to the target phoneme sequence from the CTC output, outputting the first-stage score S1 and candidate segment timestamps. The second stage is the QbyT branch: candidate segments are extracted according to timestamps and fed into the phoneme matcher along with features from the text registration side. Verification is performed at both phoneme-level and sentence-level granularities, outputting the second-stage score S2 (Figure 1, Figure 2).

Why split detection and verification into two stages? Although the paper does not use the term "decoupling," the motivation can be directly read from the design: the metric orientation of the detection stage is high recall—better to have false positives than false negatives, as missed detections cannot be corrected at the second stage. The metric orientation of the verification stage is high precision—suppressing false positives that slipped through after the first stage was relaxed. The root cause of sliding window methods is the lack of an explicit localization step, where boundaries are rigidly defined by the window grid. In contrast, the alignment nature of CTC naturally provides timestamps, with boundaries derived from acoustic alignment. The first stage deliberately uses fuzzy phoneme aggregation to relax matching (see below), effectively designing "relaxation" explicitly into the retrieval end and leaving "tightening" to the second stage: wide entry, strict exit.

There is another layer of data-level motivation for the two stages: the capability bottlenecks of the two stages are different, and the types of data fed to them are also different. The essence of the first stage is an acoustic model, consuming ASR corpora (without requiring word-pair annotations). The essence of the second stage is a cross-modal discriminator, consuming word pairs, where the key variable is the class diversity of anchor words. This division of labor is the premise for the validity of the dual data scaling described later—if both stages consume the same type of data, "dual" scaling would be impossible.

Regarding deployment modes, the paper provides three configurations (Figure 1): M0 is a pure CTC single stage with 3.6M parameters; M1 adds a lightweight branch of approximately 0.5M parameters on top of M0, totaling 4.1M; M2 adds approximately 4.1M on top of M0, totaling 7.7M. The difference between M1 and M2 lies in how candidate segment features are obtained: M1 directly crops frame-level features from the first-stage encoder output according to timestamps and passes them through a lightweight projection layer for dimension mapping, adding only about 0.5M parameters; M2 cuts the candidate segments into raw speech segments according to timestamps and re-encodes them using a trainable encoder in the second stage. This provides stronger discriminative representations but adds about 3.6M more parameters than M1 (Section 2.2). Why have these two tiers? The rationale for M1 is saving compute—candidate segments do not need to pass through the encoder again, and once the first-stage encoder is frozen, its acoustic capabilities will not be skewed by the matching gradients of the second stage, making it suitable for extremely compute-constrained scenarios. The rationale for M2 is representation adaptation—the features output by the first-stage encoder are representations optimized for CTC phoneme recognition and may not be the optimal representations for matching discrimination. Re-encoding gives the second stage full freedom. Table 2 data supports this trade-off: under the same LP-GP-1460 data, M1’s LPH EER is 10.02%, while M2’s is 6.13%, with re-encoding bringing a 3.89 percentage point gain.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The formalization of the entire pipeline is quite concise, consisting of six equations.

On the audio side, input speech $F_a$ is sent to the audio encoder to obtain an embedding sequence (Equation 1): $E_a = (e_1, e_2, ..., e_T)$, where $T$ is the length of the encoder output. The encoder adopts Conformer [21], motivated by its convolution-enhanced Transformer structure, which possesses both local spectral detail modeling and long-range dependency modeling capabilities, making it suitable for phoneme-level streaming tasks.

On the text registration side, the target word text is first converted into a phoneme sequence via G2P—the vocabulary consists of 71 phoneme symbols (including the CTC blank placeholder), which are then converted into token indices via a character-level tokenizer (Equation 2): $X = \text{Tokenizer}(\text{G2P}(\text{text})) \in \mathbb{Z}^{71}$. Note that the text side stops at indices here and does not pass through any deep encoder, which is key to lightweight registration (see Innovation 2).

The audio embedding $E_a$ is trained using the CTC loss, which maps it to a frame-by-frame token probability distribution (Equation 3): $c = (c_1, ..., c_T)$, where $c_t$ is the predicted distribution over the 71 phoneme tokens at frame $t$. The alignment nature of CTC (phoneme-frame alignment can be回溯 after blank insertion and repetition folding) is the mathematical foundation for the phoneme search module to output timestamps.

The phoneme search module retrieves candidate paths corresponding to the target phoneme sequence from the output distribution. For fuzzy targets, the paper defines phoneme aggregation (Equation 4): for the fuzzy set $F(x_t)$ of target phoneme $x_t$, $S_{agg}(x_t) = \sum_{f \in F(x_t)} S(f)$, where $S(f)$ is the CTC probability of phoneme $f$. Taking "Hi Snips" vs. "Hey Snips" as an example, they differ only in AY1/EY1. Summing the probabilities of these two phonemes before retrieval allows pronunciation variants starting with "Hi" to hit the target sequence for "Hey Snips." The paper explicitly states that this aggregation improves recall by accepting variants of the target word (Section 2.1)—the mathematical implementation of the first stage's active relaxation.

The phoneme matcher in the second stage (Figure 2) uses a lightweight attention mechanism to align audio features $E_a$ with the phoneme sequence $E_t$. The aligned features are fed into a discriminator, which outputs True/False judgments at both phoneme-level and sentence-level granularities. The sentence-level score $P_{utt}$ serves as the output score S2 (Figure 2, Section 2.2). The matcher is trained using a joint loss (Equation 5): $L_{matcher} = L_{utt} + L_{phon}$, representing sentence-level and phoneme-level matching losses, respectively. The overall framework objective (Equation 6) is: $L_{total} = L_{CTC} + L_{matcher}$, i.e., the superposition of acoustic supervision and matching supervision (in M2 mode, the shared encoder accepts gradients from both types simultaneously).

Why dual supervision at phoneme-level and sentence-level? The phoneme-level loss provides direct supervision at each aligned position, offering dense gradient signals, specifically helping the model see "where the phoneme differences lie"—which is exactly what is needed to distinguish confusable pairs with single-phoneme differences. The sentence-level loss aligns with the final usage mode (whole-word judgment), preventing the phoneme-level supervision from optimizing representations that are disconnected from the overall judgment. The two layers stacked together mean $L_{phon}$ determines how finely the model sees, and $L_{utt}$ determines how accurately it judges.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Streaming CTC Retrieval with Fuzzy Phoneme Aggregation for Candidate Localization

This design simultaneously addresses two pain points: "inaccurate sliding window boundaries" and "cannot lose recall." Compared to sliding windows, the candidate boundaries from CTC retrieval come from alignment paths, naturally adhering to acoustic events. Compared to hard matching, fuzzy aggregation merges probabilities of neighboring confusable phonemes, avoiding missed detections of pronunciation variants. The cost is that the retrieval end becomes wider, mixing in more plausible-sounding but incorrect candidates—the paper explicitly offloads this cost to the second stage for digestion, where the division of labor between the two stages is most clearly manifested. From the score distribution in Figure 3, the scores of positive samples in the first stage are scattered in a wide interval from 0.04 to 1, indicating that the first stage is indeed "wide"; after filtering by the first stage, the second stage's scores clearly separate positive and negative samples (Section 4.3), indicating that the second stage is indeed "strict."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Lightweight Text Registration via nn.Embedding

Existing QbyT/QbyAT solutions generally have heavy registration sides: the MM-KWS [3,16] series uses DistilBERT to encode registered text, the CED [3,17] series uses G2P embeddings, and PLCL [3,20] uses an audio-text joint registration module. The paper points out that these components have large offline computational overheads (Section 2.2). DS-KWS takes a radically simple approach: a single `nn.Embedding` lookup table maps phoneme indices directly into the feature space to obtain $E_t$, significantly reducing the computational and memory requirements for registration. Why dare to do this: the information content of registered text is inherently small—a wake-up word is only a sequence of a few phonemes, with no long text requiring deep semantic encoding. The matching task requires the "alignment relationship between phonemes and acoustic frames," not the "semantic representation of words." Reducing registration from "running a large model" to "a single lookup table" is a substantive improvement for the product requirement that "user on-site registration must take effect in seconds." The paper does not report exactly how many milliseconds or how much memory is saved.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Dual-Granularity Matcher with Phoneme-Level and Sentence-Level Supervision

The matcher itself is extremely lightweight: 2 layers of Transformer, one GRU, and several fully connected layers, with `nn.Embedding` on the text side. The entire second-stage lightweight branch has approximately 0.5M parameters (Section 3.2, Figure 2). Structurally, it uses attention for audio-phoneme alignment, followed by a discrimination head outputting at two granularities. Why is it so light yet effective: the second stage does not need to re-understand speech (acoustic understanding is already completed by the first stage); it only needs to perform fine-grained comparison on already-aligned candidates, a task with lower difficulty than encoding from scratch. The lightness also ensures that the combination of "freezing the large encoder plus small matcher" in M1 mode is controllable in engineering.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Dual Data Scaling Strategy

This is the "Dual Data Scaling" in the title and the core claim of the paper. The two scaling axes correspond to the capability bottlenecks of the two stages respectively.

The first axis expands ASR training corpora to serve the first-stage acoustic model: expanding from LibriSpeech-460 (460 hours) to LS-GS-1460 (1460 hours, LibriSpeech-460 combined with GS-1000 from the Middle part of GigaSpeech) (Abstract, Section 3.1). Why expand this axis: the retrieval quality of the first stage depends on the accuracy of the acoustic model's modeling of phoneme distributions. Especially on data like LibriSpeech-other with accent noise, if the acoustic model is poor, fuzzy aggregation is meaningless. Table 2 shows that expanding the corpus reduced P-WER on LSother from 18.79% to 11.80%.

The second axis expands the number of anchor word classes to serve the second-stage matcher: the training word-pair set expands from LP-460 (approximately 78k anchor classes) to LP-GP-1460 (approximately 155k classes) (Abstract, Section 3.1). Why expand this axis: the essence of the matcher is cross-modal alignment plus discrimination. Its discrimination power comes from how many "similar but different" word-pair combinations it has seen. The number of anchor classes directly determines the coverage of confusable words. Table 3 shows that as anchors expanded from 12k to 155k, LPH EER monotonically decreased from 13.38% to 10.65%.

Why must it be "dual" rather than single-axis expansion: The cross-comparison of Table 2 and Table 3 provides evidence (see Ablation section)—fixing the anchor count while swapping for a stronger encoder, or fixing the encoder while swapping for more anchors, each yields independent benefits. This indicates that the two axes complement two different shortcomings, and only their superposition delivers the final 6.13%.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to sliding-window-based QbyE/QbyT/QbyAT methods (CMCD [1], EMKWS [16], CED [17], SLiCK [19], AdaKWS [2], MM-KWS [3], PLCL [20]): DS-KWS’s detection end is not sliding-window scoring but CTC streaming alignment retrieval. Candidate generation and boundaries are determined by acoustic alignment, directly addressing the mismatch of "pre-segmented training plus sliding-window inference." Compared to multi-stage ASR methods (U2-KWS [11], CaTT-KWS [12]): DS-KWS’s second stage is not running decoding again, but an explicit cross-modal matcher with dual phoneme-level and sentence-level supervision. Compared to W-CTC [6] (also a single-stage scheme with ASR pre-training and CTC alignment): DS-KWS adds a second-stage verification, reducing LPH EER from 10.21% to 6.13% (Table 1). This difference can be roughly viewed as the net contribution of the second stage. Compared to the authors' previous work MM-KWS [3] (from the same lineage of the first author): the registration end retreats from multi-modal heavy registration to `nn.Embedding` lookup. The detection end switches from sliding windows to CTC streaming retrieval. The思路 shifts from "stronger registration representations" to "more accurate candidate localization plus more anchor coverage plus two-stage division of labor."

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

ASR training corpora, three tiers (Section 3.1): LibriSpeech-100 (LS-100, 100 hours, clean training subset); LibriSpeech-460 (LS-460, 460 hours, clean training subset); GigaSpeech-1000 (GS-1000, taking the Middle part of GigaSpeech); LS-460 combined with GS-1000 forms LS-GS-1460 (1460 hours).

Word-pair training sets (Section 3.1): Following the construction method of LibriPhrase, only anchors with duration between 0.5 and 2 seconds and at least 10 instances are retained. LP-100 has approximately 12k anchor classes; LP-460 has approximately 78k classes. Two subsets, R2W (20k classes) and R4W (40k classes), are randomly sampled from LP-460 to form a scale gradient. LS-460 combined with GigaPhrase-1000 (GP-1000) forms LP-GP-1460, with approximately 155k classes. The paper does not detail the construction of GigaPhrase itself (how word pairs are generated from GigaSpeech), but it is inferred to be the same method as LibriPhrase.

Three evaluation sets (Section 3.1): LibriSpeech’s clean and other test sets (LSclean, LSother) evaluate intermediate ASR quality; LibriPhrase is the main benchmark, derived from LibriSpeech-other-500, divided into Easy (LPE) and Hard (LPH) subsets—LPH is the confusable word pair subset and the most critical battlefield in the paper; Hey-Snips dataset uses only the test set (containing 2,529 positive and 20,543 negative examples) to evaluate zero-shot performance.

Model and training configurations (Section 3.2): Input is 80-channel mel-spectrogram, 25ms window, 10ms frame shift; the audio encoder is a 6-layer Conformer, encoding dimension 144, linear dimension 576, kernel size 3, 4-head attention, with approximately 3.6M trainable parameters; the first stage is trained using WeNet with CTC loss, with a phoneme vocabulary of 71 symbols (including blank), training one model each for 100h/460h/1460h. The second-stage lightweight QbyT branch consists of 2 layers of Transformer, one GRU, and several fully connected layers. Text features are encoded via `nn.Embedding`, with a total of approximately 0.5M parameters. Details such as optimizer, learning rate, and number of training epochs are not reported in the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper uses three sets of metrics, each corresponding to a layer of validation purpose. P-WER (reported on LSclean/LSother, Table 2): The paper does not provide a formal definition, but inferred from context, it is phoneme-level word error rate. The reason for selecting it is that the essence of the first stage's capability is an acoustic model. P-WER directly quantifies its changes with corpus expansion, serving as intermediate evidence for "whether the first scaling axis is useful." EER and AUC (LibriPhrase, Tables 1, 2, 3): Equal Error Rate is the error rate when the false alarm rate equals the miss rate. AUC is the area under the ROC curve. Both are conventional metrics for the LibriPhrase word-pair discrimination benchmark. Reporting both兼顾s single-operating-point performance and full-threshold ranking capability. Recall@FAR (Hey-Snips, Table 4): Recall rate at a fixed false alarm rate, reporting two tiers: FAR=0.5 and FAR=1. The reason for selecting it is that wake-up products have a clear false alarm budget—the number of false alarms per hour that users can tolerate is a product constraint (corresponding to "one false alarm per hour" in the Abstract). Comparing recall under this constraint has engineering significance, whereas the operating point of EER usually falls at a false alarm rate unacceptable to products, not reflecting real usage conditions.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The main comparison is in Table 1 (LibriPhrase). Baselines are arranged by LPH EER: CMCD [1] 0.7M parameters, LPH EER 32.90%, AUC 73.58%; EMKWS [16] 3.7M, EER 23.36%, AUC 84.21%; CED [17] 3.6M, EER 14.40%, AUC 92.70%; SLiCK [19] 0.6M, EER 11.10%, AUC 94.90%; AdaKWS-Small [2] 109M, EER 11.48%, AUC 95.09%; MM-KWS-T [3] 3.9M, EER 10.41%, AUC 95.36%; W-CTC [6] 3.6M, EER 10.21%, AUC 95.93%; PLCL-T [20] 40M, EER 9.96%, AUC 95.56%; Multi-modal registered MM-KWS-AT [3] 3.9M, EER 9.30%, AUC 96.25%; PLCL-AT [20] 40M, EER 8.47%, AUC 96.59%. DS-KWS-M1 (4.1M) achieves LPH EER 10.02%, AUC 95.77%, LPE EER 0.52%, LPE AUC 99.98%; DS-KWS-M2 achieves LPH EER 6.13%, AUC 97.85%, LPE EER 0.45%, LPE AUC 99.98% (Table 1).

Several comparison dimensions are worth highlighting. First, against the strongest baseline PLCL-AT (40M, EER 8.47%), DS-KWS-M2 reduces EER by another 2.34 percentage points with approximately one-fifth of the parameters, indicating that the gains come mainly from architecture and data strategies rather than stacking parameters. Second, against W-CTC (3.6M, EER 10.21%), which follows the same ASR pre-training route, DS-KWS-M1 has similar parameters (4.1M) and slightly better EER (10.02%), while M2 pulls significantly ahead to 6.13%, making the net contribution of the second-stage verification clearly visible. Third, on the LPE subset, performance is saturated across the board (EER generally around 1%). DS-KWS-M2’s 0.45% is the best in the table, but the true differentiation lies in LPH—this aligns with the design intent of this benchmark: anyone can get the Easy subset right; the Hard confusable subset is where rankings are decided. The paper also emphasizes two points (Section 4.1): even without expanding data with GS-1000 and GP-1000, LP-460-M2 still achieves 7.97% LPH EER (Table 2), indicating that the two-stage architecture itself is effective, and data expansion acts as an amplifier rather than a prerequisite. The result of 10.02% under M1 with frozen encoder shows that the framework remains robust even when the encoder is completely immovable.

Zero-shot comparison is in Table 4 (Hey-Snips test set). On official Snips data, RIL-KWS [22] trained with full-shot achieves two recall tiers (FAR=0.5/1) of 96.47%/97.18%, and MDTC [5] achieves 99.88%/99.92%. DS-KWS in complete zero-shot mode has three configurations: M0 (3.6M) 98.89%/98.97%, M1 (4.1M) 98.58%/98.93%, M2 (7.7M) 98.97%/99.13% (Table 4). Interpretation: M2’s zero-shot recall exceeds full-shot RIL-KWS and approaches full-shot MDTC. The abstract’s claim that "zero-shot performance is comparable to full-shot trained models, with 99.13% recall under one false alarm per hour" stems from this. A noteworthy detail of honesty: M1’s recall at FAR=0.5 (98.58%) is actually lower than pure CTC’s M0 (98.89%). The paper explains this itself: the 0.5M parameter phoneme matcher has limited generalization capability under frozen encoder, performing slightly worse than using CTC alone (Section 4.3)—there is a real trade-off between freezing to save compute and the zero-shot generalization of the matcher, which the paper does not shy away from.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The ablation unfolds along three axes, with a relatively complete chain of evidence.

First axis: ASR corpus expansion (Table 2). The first-stage encoder is trained separately with LS-100, LS-460, and LS-GS-1460. P-WER decreases monotonically with corpus size: on LSclean, from 6.98% to 4.44% to 4.45%; on LSother, from 18.79% to 13.39% to 11.80% (Table 2)—the largest drop occurs on the most difficult other set, indicating that expanding data mainly supplements accent and noise robustness. Corresponding to KWS metrics, in M1 mode, LPH EER goes from 15.34% (LP-100-M1) to 10.78% (LP-460-M1) to 10.02% (LP-GP-1460-M1); in M2 mode, from 13.71% to 7.97% to 6.13%. P-WER is not reported for M2 rows in Table 2 ("-"), because the encoder in M2 is fine-tuned in the second stage and is no longer a pure ASR model.

Second axis: Anchor class expansion (Table 3). Fixing the first-stage encoder to LS-460 pre-trained and frozen, varying only the anchor count: 12k (LPH EER 13.38%) → 20k (12.50%) → 40k (11.62%) → 78k (10.78%) → 155k (10.65%), monotonically improving; AUC increases from 93.22% to 95.45%, LPE EER decreases from 1.19% to 0.64% (Table 3). Two observations: First, diminishing returns are obvious. The first few tiers drop by about 0.8 to 0.9 percentage points per step, while the last step (78k to 155k) drops only 0.13 percentage points, indicating that the marginal value of anchor diversity is decaying. Second, cross-referencing Table 3 with Table 2 allows separating the contributions of the two axes—for the same 12k anchors, the LS-100 encoder gives 15.34% (Table 2 LP-100-M1), while the LS-460 encoder gives 13.38% (Table 3 LP-100), a difference of 1.96 percentage points coming from the encoder corpus. For the same 155k anchors, the LS-460 encoder gives 10.65% (Table 3), while the LS-GS-1460 encoder gives 10.02% (Table 2 LP-GP-1460-M1), a difference of 0.63 percentage points. These two sets of controls prove that encoder corpus and anchor classes each contribute independently, living up to the name "dual expansion."

Third axis: Direct evidence of two-stage division of labor (Figure 3). Although the first-stage CTC scores can separate positive and negative samples, the positive sample scores are scattered in a wide interval from 0.04 to 1. After filtering by the first stage, the second-stage scores clearly separate positive and negative samples (Section 4.3). This figure is intuitive support for "the first stage manages recall, the second stage manages precision": wide entry, strict exit. The final judgment quality is determined by the second stage, which also explains why M2 has the strongest zero-shot generalization.

Synthesizing the three axes, there is another fact not at the center of the paper's narrative but obvious in the data: the single largest gain comes from the mode upgrade from M1 to M2 (LPH EER drops from 10.02% to 6.13% under LP-GP-1460, a drop of 3.89 percentage points), which is greater than any single tier of pure data expansion. The paper attributes this to "simultaneous expansion of pre-training and fine-tuning data" (Section 4.2), meaning the gain of M2 itself is the realization of dual expansion on a fine-tunable architecture—data, architecture, and mode are coupled, not three independent knobs.

## Main Contributions

Summarized into four points. First, proposing a two-stage UDKWS framework combining CTC streaming retrieval localization with QbyT dual-granularity verification: using acoustic alignment to replace sliding windows to solve inaccurate boundaries and false alarm issues. The first stage explicitly uses fuzzy phoneme aggregation to preserve recall. The second stage verifies under dual phoneme-level and sentence-level supervision, with a clear division of labor: wide entry, strict exit. Second, proposing and systematically studying a dual data scaling strategy: ASR corpus expanded from 460 to 1460 hours to serve the acoustic model (LSother P-WER dropped from 18.79% to 11.80%); anchor classes expanded from 12k to 155k to serve the matcher's discrimination power (LPH EER dropped from 13.38% to 10.65%). Cross-ablation proves both axes are independently effective. Third, lightweight registration design: `nn.Embedding` lookup table replaces heavy registration encoders like DistilBERT, reducing registration overhead from model inference level to lookup table level. The second-stage lightweight branch totals only about 0.5M parameters. Fourth, in terms of results: 6.13% EER and 97.85% AUC on LibriPhrase-Hard, 0.45% EER on LPE (Table 1), significantly exceeding SOTA methods like PLCL-AT and MM-KWS-AT. On Hey-Snips, zero-shot reaches 99.13% recall at FAR=1 (Table 4), comparable to full-shot trained models.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

The absence of efficiency numbers is the biggest gap. KWS is a task for resident low-power scenarios, but the paper never reports inference latency, RTF, FLOPS, or memory usage. Especially in M2 mode, the parameter count is more than double that of M0 (3.6M to 7.7M), and every candidate segment must pass through a trainable encoder again. How the streaming tail latency behaves and whether computational load accumulates when candidates are dense are not reported. The trade-off between M1 and M2 can therefore only be viewed in terms of precision, not cost, creating a substantive information gap for deployment decisions—the authors themselves admit in the conclusion that deployment verification on low-resource platforms is needed.

The joint decision mechanism for the two-stage scores is not explained. How are the S1 threshold and S2 threshold determined? Is S1 and S2 a serial filter or score fusion? The text only states that the matcher outputs the sentence-level score $P_{utt}$ as the output score S2 (Figure 1, Section 2.2). The operation point used for "filtering" in the first stage is not detailed. This directly affects reproducibility and the space for tuning false alarm rates.

The construction of the fuzzy phoneme set $F(x_t)$ is a black box: which phoneme pairs are considered fuzzy, whether it is manually defined or data-driven, and whether multiple fuzzy sets taking effect simultaneously could cascade and amplify false alarms—the paper does not explain. This is the core knob for the recall-precision trade-off at the first stage. Without it, Equation 4 cannot be reproduced independently.

The absolute level of 6.13% LPH EER is still not low—approximately one error in every sixteen judgments at the equal error rate operating point, while LPE is already at 0.45%. Whether the remaining errors in the confusable subset have systematic patterns (long vowels, nasal codas, stress position differences) is not analyzed by the paper, making it impossible to judge how far the ceiling is.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First, the parameter count for DS-KWS-M2 in Table 1 is annotated as 4.1M, which contradicts the 7.7M in Table 4 and the annotation in Figure 1 (M0 3.6M, M2 increment 4.1M). This should be a typo, and 7.7M should be used for horizontal comparison. Second, the data expansion ablation only has three tiers: LS-100, LS-460, and LS-GS-1460 (Table 2). The absence of a GS-1000-only tier makes it impossible to distinguish whether the gain from 1460 hours comes from the absolute volume of data or the domain diversity of GigaSpeech. On the anchor axis, there is no larger tier beyond 155k to verify if gains have saturated (the last step in Table 3 drops only 0.13 percentage points, suggesting approaching saturation). Third, training details (optimizer, learning rate schedule, number of training epochs, positive/negative sampling ratio for word pairs) are not reported, making independent reproduction difficult. Fourth, language and domain are singular: all experiments are in the English audiobook domain (derived from LibriSpeech, GigaSpeech). The gap with real device scenarios (near-field, far-field, TV noise, multi-speaker) is not discussed, nor is there verification in Chinese or other languages—considering the Introduction opens with the motivation of Chinese products like "XiaoZhi AI," this gap is particularly conspicuous. Fifth, in the Hey-Snips zero-shot comparison (Table 4), the two references (RIL-KWS, MDTC) are both full-shot models. There is a lack of horizontal comparison with other zero-shot UDKWS methods on the same test set. The conclusion that "zero-shot is comparable to full-shot" lacks an anchor from a similar opponent. Sixth, all ablations use EER/AUC as metrics, with no evaluation under any latency or power constraints, which mismatches the product attributes of KWS.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper self-reports two directions in the conclusion: fine-tuning strategies for a wider set of wake-up words (upgrading zero-shot to few-shot adaptability), and deployment verification on low-resource platforms. Following the gaps in the paper, extensions could include: joint threshold learning or learnable fusion weights for the two-stage scores, replacing manual threshold setting; constructing fuzzy phoneme sets from confusable statistics data rather than manual definition, or even automatically expanding them with anchor data; after the diminishing returns of anchor expansion, shifting to active mining of hard word pairs (hard negative mining, curriculum sampling) may be more cost-effective than uniform class expansion; distilling the second-stage matcher back into a single-stage model to recover the latency overhead of M2 mode; verification on cross-language and real far-field data. Among these directions, deployment verification and cross-language verification are key to determining whether this framework can step out of the audiobook benchmark and land on real products like "XiaoZhi AI."
