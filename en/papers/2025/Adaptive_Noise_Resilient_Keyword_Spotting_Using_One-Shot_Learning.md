- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09304 (Code: https://github.com/lucianomrau/NoiseRobustKeywordSpottingMCU)

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the entry point for voice interaction in smart devices. Unlike large-scale speech recognition, KWS does not require transcribing entire sentences; instead, it detects predefined words or phrases within a continuous audio stream to trigger specific actions—such as waking up a virtual assistant, controlling smartphones and wearables, operating smart home devices, initiating in-car voice commands, and monitoring health or security. This makes KWS a core component of modern consumer electronics.

However, mainstream KWS research has focused primarily on accuracy, relying on computationally intensive deep learning models that are infeasible for low-power devices. Deploying KWS to resource-constrained embedded systems (microcontrollers, low-power edge devices) requires satisfying three hard constraints: computational efficiency, memory footprint, and energy consumption. More critically, the paper emphasizes "standalone" and "autonomous" KWS. To protect user privacy, the system cannot rely on internet connectivity; all robustness designs must reside in the device's local firmware and hardware. This transforms "robustness" from a cloud-backed problem into a firmware-level issue that the edge device must handle independently.

The primary adversary in real-world conditions is background noise. Models that achieve high accuracy on clean test sets in the laboratory suffer significant performance degradation when deployed in kitchens, streets, or vehicles. The paper uses Fig. 1 to present the standard block diagram of a standalone KWS system to position its work: the analog front-end captures and digitizes the signal; the data acquisition module picks up sound via a microphone, aided by Automatic Gain Control (AGC) to improve far-field coverage; Voice Activity Detection (VAD) triggers downstream processing only when speech is detected to save compute power; optional noise reduction modules (beamforming, adaptive noise cancellation) combat reverberation, interference, and noise; the feature extractor converts audio into discriminative representations (log-Mel spectrograms or MFCCs); the KWS model is typically implemented using efficient neural networks and compressed via quantization, pruning, and shrinking to reduce computational overhead; finally, two often-overlooked modules are confidence monitoring (assessing the reliability of model predictions under uncertain conditions) and domain adaptation (allowing the system to adjust in real-time to changing conditions and permitting external user intervention). The proposed continuous noise adaptation method essentially provides an extremely lightweight concrete implementation for the "domain adaptation module" block.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing work into four types and points out their shortcomings one by one; this梳理 itself constitutes the problem definition of the entire paper:

- **Accuracy-oriented SOTA is not deployable**. Most recent literature pursues accuracy using computationally intensive deep learning models, which cannot run on low-power devices, let alone perform noise adaptation on the edge.
- **"One-time" noise robustness training, unadjustable after deployment**. Huang et al. used speech enhancement techniques to improve "Ok Google" recognition in noisy environments; Jung et al. proposed a multi-task network combining KWS and speaker verification, relying on the synergy of speech enhancement and feature extraction to improve performance under noise; Park et al. enhanced robustness using noise student-teacher knowledge distillation, but at the cost of increased inference latency and energy consumption; Wu et al. used domain-aware training to improve far-field small-size KWS, addressing environmental noise and room reverberation. The common limitation of these methods is that they are fixed after training; the model cannot be adjusted to new conditions after deployment. Once the noise environment changes, the robustness guarantee becomes invalid.
- **Excessive overhead for on-device domain adaptation**. Cioflan et al. proposed a domain adaptation method that mixes clean speech samples stored on the device with real-time collected noise signals to generate noisy samples, adapting a lightweight depthwise separable convolutional network (DS-CNN). While the idea is correct and effective, the energy consumption for a single adaptation is as high as 5.81 J, and the reported minimum latency is 14 seconds, with no demonstration on an MCU. This cost is impractical for low-power devices.
- **MCU version is limited by memory and evaluation has blind spots**. Subsequent work by Cioflan et al. deployed a similar method on an MCU: the device stores noise-free speech, mixes it with real-time collected noise to generate noisy speech for model updates. This is effective when sufficient speech data is available, but performance is limited by memory. More critically, the experimental flaw is that evaluation was conducted only under a single noise condition (SNR=0 dB), completely ignoring varying noise levels and SNR mismatch between training and testing phases. Since real-world noise is non-stationary, this blind spot is precisely the norm during deployment.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

The goal is to compress all overhead of "adapting a deployed KWS model to specific noise" to a level affordable by resource-constrained devices. This is broken down into four sub-problems: First, data overhead—the number of adaptation samples to be stored and processed must be minimized to the extreme (the paper's answer is 1 sample per class, trained for only 1 epoch). Second, computational overhead—the number of parameters to be updated via backpropagation must be minimized to the extreme (the paper's answer is to fine-tune only the last fully connected layer, freezing all convolutional layers). Third, evaluation must be close to reality—adaptation is performed under a specific noise source and SNR, but the adapted model must be robust across the entire SNR range because real-world noise is non-stationary (this directly addresses the blind spot of Cioflan's MCU work, which only tested at 0 dB). Fourth, it must answer "when is adaptation worth it"—if the accuracy exceeds 90% before adaptation, wasting compute power on adaptation is meaningless. The adaptation trigger condition itself is a decision problem that needs research.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire solution follows a clear two-stage design.

**Stage 1: Pre-train two models.** The first is called the `baseline-model`, trained only on clean speech command data, serving as a reference for ideal quiet conditions. The second is called the `noise-aware-model`, trained on a mixture of clean and noisy data, attempting to inject noise robustness during the pre-training phase. Why train two? Because this is an experimental hypothesis to be tested: whether multi-style noisy pre-training provides a better starting point for subsequent dynamic noise adaptation. If the answer is negative (as later experiments prove), only a model trained on pure clean data needs to be stored during deployment, and the pre-training process is simpler.

**Stage 2: On-site noise adaptation.** Use few-shot transfer learning (TL) to adapt the pre-trained model to specific on-site noise conditions (noise source + SNR). The key constraint is to fine-tune only the last fully connected layer, freezing all convolutional layers, thereby minimizing memory and computational requirements.

**Feature Front-end** follows the process in reference [4]: 16 kHz sampled audio is converted into a log-Mel time-frequency representation using a 25 ms window length, 10 ms frame shift, and 64 Mel filters (covering 50–7500 Hz). This step compresses the signal length to 1/2.48 of the original (data length reduction factor of 2.48). The motivation for choosing this is not "everyone uses it," but rather the fact demonstrated in reference [4]: this log-Mel configuration can be computed directly by an external low-power analog front-end, significantly reducing the energy consumption of the end-to-end KWS system on resource-constrained devices. That is, the feature extraction step can be nearly free in the target deployment form.

**Model Structure** (Fig. 2) is a CNN inspired by [4]: described in the text as "five convolutional layers with Batch Normalization (BN) inserted between layers, followed by an average pooling layer that takes the average over the channels of the entire feature map; the resulting single-channel value is then fed into a fully connected layer." From the annotations in Fig. 2, the convolution configuration from left to right is 64@3×3 (stride 2), 128@3×3 (stride 1), 128@3×3 (stride 2), 192@3×3 (stride 1), 192@1×1 (stride 1), 12@1×1 (stride 1), all followed by ReLU. The end consists of channel average pooling plus a 12-neuron fully connected softmax layer (there is a discrepancy between the text's "five convolutional layers" and the six groups of convolution annotations visible in the figure; 12@1×1 is closer to a class-projection convolution, and reproducers should refer to the original figure). The "why" behind several structural choices is worth expanding: First, choosing the CNN family over RNN inherits the lineage of DS-CNN, which has been validated on MCUs—Zhang et al. achieved 95.4% accuracy using DS-CNN on Cortex-M7 STM32F746G, and Sørensen et al. have also deployed quantized DS-CNN on embedded platforms. The storage/computation/accuracy trade-off of this architecture route is known for MCUs. Second, channel average pooling compresses the entire feature map of each channel into a single mean value. The benefits are threefold—the input to the fully connected layer is extremely small (one value per channel), the number of parameters and computational cost drop sharply, and it is naturally robust to positional shifts of keywords on the time axis (taking the mean is insensitive to time translation). Third, this model itself is not the contribution of this paper; it is merely a standard carrier. The true contribution lies in "how to adapt it to new noise at the lowest cost."

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The mathematical framework is a standard transfer learning setting. Let the log-Mel spectrogram of the input audio be $x$. The network consists of a frozen feature extractor $\varphi(\cdot)$ (all convolutional layers + BN + channel average pooling) and a trainable last-layer fully connected layer $W \in \mathbb{R}^{12 \times d}$ ($d$ is the number of channels after pooling). The output probability is:

$$p = \mathrm{softmax}(W\,\varphi(x) + b)$$

The training objective is the cross-entropy loss:

$$L_{CE} = -\sum_{k=1}^{12} y_k \log p_k$$

where $y$ is the one-hot label. The optimizer configurations for the two stages are different, which is an intentional layered design:

**Pre-training stage**: Uses the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of $10^{-4}$. If the validation set shows no improvement for 10 consecutive epochs, the learning rate is decayed by a factor of 10. The batch size is 16, for a total of 50 epochs, with other parameters taking default values. **Adaptation stage**: Uses Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with the same learning rate of $10^{-4}$, and the loss remains cross-entropy. The update rule for each adaptation is:

$$W \leftarrow W - \eta \nabla_W L_{CE}, \quad \eta = 10^{-4}$$

The decisive difference brought by freezing is that backpropagation is truncated at the input of $W$. The calculation of $\nabla_W L_{CE} = \frac{\partial L_{CE}}{\partial p}\cdot\frac{\partial p}{\partial (W\varphi(x))}\cdot\varphi(x)^{\top}$ only requires the cached $\varphi(x)$ from the forward pass. There is no need to save gradients for any convolutional layer, nor to maintain optimizer states for backbone parameters (Adam's first and second moment estimates each occupy memory equal to the number of parameters, which is fatal on an MCU). The specific meaning of "one-shot learning" here is: during adaptation, only 1 noisy speech sample per class (12 keywords, totaling 12 samples) is used, and the data is passed through only once (1 epoch).

**Noisy data generation** adopts an additive noise model: clean 1-second speech from GSC is mixed with noise source signals according to the target SNR, $x_{noisy} = x_{clean} + \alpha \cdot n$. The selection of $\alpha$ ensures that $10\log_{10}(P_{x_{clean}}/P_{\alpha n})$ equals the target SNR. The values range from -3 dB to 24 dB in steps of 3 dB, totaling 10 levels (the paper text describes "pollution with additive noise, SNR from -3 to 24 dB"; the formula is the standard form of this process). The noisy training, validation, and test sets are all generated independently by the same process.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Extremely Lightweight Continuous Noise Adaptation by Fine-tuning Only the Last Fully Connected Layer

This is the core design of the entire paper. Why dare to tune only the last layer? The decision logic has three layers: First, **memory cost**—the adaptation samples to be saved are only 12 one-second speeches, the parameters to be updated are only $W$ (size 12×d plus 12 biases), and the optimizer state covers only this layer. Second, **computational cost**—the backpropagation chain is truncated at the FC layer input. The forward pass still needs to pass through the backbone (this is the necessary cost to obtain $\varphi(x)$), but the backward pass involves only one layer of matrix operations. Third, **feature protection**—the general acoustic features learned by the convolutional backbone remain completely unchanged, naturally avoiding catastrophic forgetting and overfitting to the 12 samples, which are the most common issues when fine-tuning deep networks with small samples. The implicit bet of this design is: after the frozen backbone, most of the input distribution drift caused by noise can still be absorbed by re-weighting in the final linear decision layer. Experiments provide strong backing: adaptation with only 1-shot and 1 epoch allows the model to consistently exceed the pre-adaptation model across all test scenarios, achieving accuracy improvements of 4.9% to 46.0% at SNR≤18 dB (Abstract). Compared to benchmark methods: Cioflan et al.'s on-device domain adaptation requires updating more parameters of the DS-CNN, relies on a large number of mixed samples, and incurs 5.81 J of energy consumption and at least 14 seconds of latency. This paper compresses the same goal of "adapting to new noise" to one sample per class, one pass of data, and one layer of parameters.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Systematic Comparison of Noise-Aware Pre-training and the Conclusion that "Pre-training is Not the Answer"

The second innovation is actually at the level of experimental design. The paper trained five `noise-aware` model instances, adding 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100% balanced noisy training sets to the clean training set, respectively (the noisy part is balanced equally for each noise source and each SNR), and compared them with the `baseline-model` trained purely on clean data. Three layers of findings emerged: First, on clean test sets, noisy training caused accuracy to monotonically decrease from 96.27% to 95.73%, but the drop was less than 0.54% (all comparisons p<0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test)—the cost is controllable. Second, on noisy tests with colored noise and indoor noise (Fig. 3, full scan of SNR -3 to 24 dB), no single noise addition ratio was universally optimal across all noise sources. However, the 100% instance was optimal in 3 out of 6 noise sources and had the highest overall average accuracy. Friedman tests confirmed significant performance differences between models (χ²=1126.856, p<0.001), and Holm-corrected Wilcoxon post-hoc tests showed that the 100% variant was significantly better than all other ratios (all p<0.001)—thus, the 100% instance was selected for subsequent adaptation experiments. Third, and most counter-intuitively: on the three on-site noise sources (car horn, dog bark, street music), in the pre-adaptation comparison, the `baseline-model` was actually more robust than the `noise-aware-model` in 2 out of 3 noise sources. The gap between the two models was small only for street music, and both models were significantly dragged down by street music. The conclusion is that multi-style pre-training with colored and indoor noise **does not** transfer robustness to unrelated on-site noise. `noise-aware` pre-training offers no significant advantage for dynamic noise adaptation—the simplest clean pre-training is sufficient for deployment, leaving robustness to the runtime adaptation mechanism. This "negative conclusion" is half the value of the entire paper: it prevents engineers from taking the detour of "one-and-done noisy pre-training."

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Adaptation Evaluation Protocol Anchored at Extreme SNRs with Full SNR Scanning

The third innovation addresses the evaluation methodology. Adaptation occurs under a specific noise source and specific SNR, but real-world noise is non-stationary, so the adapted model must be tested across the entire SNR range. The paper's protocol is: only show results adapted at the two extreme SNRs of -3 dB and 24 dB (orange and green lines in Fig. 5), then test the adapted model across the full SNR range (-3 to 24 dB). The experiment observed that adaptations performed at intermediate SNRs (-3<SNR<24 dB) consistently produced accuracy curves falling within the interval bounded by the -3 dB and 24 dB boundary curves. This means that testing only two extremes can cheaply estimate the performance envelope of all adaptation conditions. The protocol yielded two findings with engineering guidance significance: First, models adapted at -3 dB outperform those adapted at 24 dB in the low-to-mid SNR range (SNR≲18 dB)—adaptation samples under heavy noise conditions contain more information. Second, at SNR≳18 dB, the adapted `noise-aware-model` achieves the highest accuracy, regardless of whether the adaptation SNR was -3 or 24 dB. Third, in the high SNR range, the pre-trained model's accuracy before adaptation already exceeds 90%, making the marginal value of adaptation questionable—"the adaptation conditions for continuous learning models should be carefully selected," meaning when to trigger adaptation is an independent decision problem. This protocol directly fills the blind spot of Cioflan's MCU work, which only evaluated at a single point of 0 dB and ignored training/testing SNR mismatch.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Placing this paper in the coordinate system of related work makes the differences clearest: Compared to speech enhancement methods (Huang et al.), this paper requires no additional microphone arrays or dedicated DSPs, solving the problem purely on the algorithm side. Compared to multi-task methods (Jung et al.), it requires no labels or compute power for auxiliary tasks like speaker verification. Compared to knowledge distillation methods (Park et al.), it requires no teacher model, and shifts the cost from "every inference becomes more expensive" to "spend a small amount once during adaptation"—this difference is fundamental for always-on KWS, because KWS inference runs all day long; even a slight increase in latency and energy consumption during inference would be magnified by several orders of magnitude, whereas adaptation is an occasional event. Compared to domain-aware training (Wu et al.), this paper does not rely on prior assumptions about the target noise field and can continue to adjust after deployment. Compared to the most direct competitors, Cioflan et al. ([14] and MCU version [23]), the differences focus on four points—the adaptation target (this paper only tunes the last FC layer, the other updates the main body of DS-CNN), data requirements (this paper uses 1 sample per class, totaling 12, for 1 epoch, the other requires large amounts of stored speech for continuous mixing and updates), known overhead (the other has 5.81 J energy consumption and minimum 14 seconds latency, this paper has not been practically tested on an MCU but the design goal is minimization, belonging to the proof-of-concept stage), and evaluation completeness (the other only tests at a single point of 0 dB, this paper performs full SNR scanning and studies the selection of adaptation SNR). It must be honestly pointed out that this paper did not perform head-to-head comparisons with these methods under the same experimental framework; the above differences are qualitative comparisons based on the conditions reported in their respective papers.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Speech data uses Google Speech Commands (GSC) version 2: a total of 105,000 one-second speeches, 35 keyword classes, plus 6 recordings of background noise without human voice (Warden). Following KWS community conventions, a 12-class benchmark is constructed: 10 keywords ("Yes", "No", "Up", "Down", "Left", "Right", "On", "Off", "Stop", "Go"), the remaining 25 words are grouped into an "Unknown" class, and 1-second segments are randomly截取 from the noise-free recordings to form the "Silence" class. Clean data is split into training/validation/test sets in an 80:10:10 ratio, with each set internally balanced by class. Noise sources are divided into three levels totaling 8 types, forming a progression from "synthetic" to "real": The first level consists of colored noise "White" and "Pink" (stationary, synthetic); the second level consists of general indoor space noise "Babble" (from NOISEX-92), "Office", "Kitchen", "Living room" (from DEMAND database), used for noisy mixing in the pre-training phase and noisy evaluation; the third level consists of specific on-site noise "Car horn", "Dog bark", "Street music" (from urban sound research datasets), specifically used as target noise for adaptation experiments, simulating sudden acoustic events truly encountered after device deployment. The SNR of the noisy sets ranges from -3 to 24 dB in 3 dB steps, totaling 10 levels. Experimental environment: Intel Core i9-12900K CPU, 32 GB RAM, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 (10 GB VRAM); software stack Python 3.12.8, Torchaudio 2.5.1, PyTorch 2.5.1, code is open source.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric is classification accuracy (accuracy). The rationale holds in the context of the paper: the 12-class benchmark is a standard task in the GSC community, each set is sampled balanced by class, so accuracy is not distorted by class imbalance, and it can be directly compared horizontally with previous works such as Zhang et al. (95.4% on MCU). More worth learning than the metric itself is the paper's rigorous statistical approach: all pre-training experiments were repeated 25 times with different weight initializations, and the average accuracy of the 25 runs was reported to avoid luck from a single initialization. Model comparisons used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired significance analysis, and full multi-model comparisons used the Friedman test with Holm-corrected post-hoc tests to control the error rate of multiple comparisons. This process is not common in KWS edge papers. The limitation is that the paper does not report per-class accuracy, KWS-specific metrics like false alarm/miss rates, nor AUC or F1—these are equally critical for actual KWS products.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Experiment 1 (Pre-trained Model Comparison)**: The `baseline-model` achieved an average accuracy of 96.27% on the clean test set. The five `noise-aware` instances (clean data mixed with 20%/40%/60%/80%/100% balanced noisy data) achieved accuracies of 96.04%, 95.98%, 95.95%, 95.81%, and 95.73% on the clean test set, respectively. This is a monotonic decrease, and the drop in all cases is less than 0.54% (all comparisons p<0.001). Noisy testing (Fig. 3, average of six noise sources × ten SNR levels): The average accuracy for each row roughly falls within the range of 88.27% to 95.17%. The hardest noise source row ranges from 88.27%–89.75%, and the easiest row ranges from 94.93%–95.17%. As the noisy training ratio increases from 20% to 100%, the overall average accuracy increases from approximately 89.9% to approximately 91.0%. Statistical tests confirm that the 100% instance is optimal (χ²=1126.856, p<0.001; post-hoc tests all p<0.001). Fig. 4 further compares the performance of `baseline` and 100% `noise-aware` across noise sources and SNRs: both models degrade as SNR decreases, but `noise-aware` consistently outperforms `baseline` in all scenarios, with the most obvious advantage in colored noise—proving that it indeed learned the noise patterns added during training. However, in the pre-adaptation comparison for the three on-site noise sources (blue line in Fig. 5), `baseline` was actually more robust in 2 out of 3 noise sources, indicating that the benefits of noisy pre-training cannot be transferred to unseen noise types.

**Experiment 2 (On-site Noise Adaptation)**: Both pre-trained models were adapted to specific noise conditions using 1-shot, 1 epoch. The core result of Fig. 5: The adapted models exceed the pre-adaptation models across all noise sources and all SNRs. The model adapted at -3 dB dominates in the SNR≲18 dB range, while in the SNR≳18 dB range, the adapted `noise-aware-model` is highest (independent of adaptation SNR). The pre-adaptation model already exceeds 90% in the high SNR range, raising the question of adaptation trigger conditions. The quantitative conclusion given in the abstract is: the adapted model consistently outperforms the pre-trained model in all scenarios, with accuracy improvements of 4.9% to 46.0% at SNR≤18 dB. Comparison with SOTA on-device adaptation methods can only be qualitative: Cioflan et al.'s MCU method requires large amounts of stored speech, is limited by memory, is evaluated only at a single point of 0 dB, and its predecessor incurred costs of 5.81 J and 14 seconds. This paper achieves the goal with 1 sample per class, 1 epoch, and 1 layer of parameters, but has not yet practically tested energy consumption and latency on an MCU, belonging to the proof-of-concept stage—this is the gap that must be marked when comparing with SOTA.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

The paper's ablation studies are very complete, covering four axes:

**Ablation of Noise Addition Training Ratio (20%–100%)**: As mentioned above, the higher the ratio, the better the overall noisy accuracy, and 100% is significantly optimal. However, clean accuracy is slightly and monotonically damaged, and no ratio wins across all noise sources—the benefit of the noise addition ratio is noise-source-dependent.

**Ablation of Adaptation Epochs (1–5 epochs, Fig. 6)**: The model can significantly improve performance using only 1 epoch for adaptation. Continuing to add epochs still yields benefits in heavy noise (SNR<3 dB) and some 3–9 dB scenarios, but performance basically stabilizes when SNR is above 9 dB. The conclusion is "start with 1 epoch, train more rounds if noise is heavier," and the cost of adding epochs is a linear increase in computation and system latency.

**Ablation of Single-sample vs. Multi-sample (1-shot vs. 5-shot, Fig. 6)**: When trained for only 1 epoch, their accuracies are close. The gap opens up only when the number of epochs increases, and the difference is concentrated in SNR<3 dB. Looking at the differences by noise source is interesting: adding data does not significantly improve performance under Car horn; under Street music, 1-shot actually achieves higher accuracy; under Dog bark, 5-shot is better at SNR≤6 dB, and the heavier the noise, the greater the improvement. The conclusion is that adding samples does not guarantee improvement, but it definitely requires five times the storage and processing volume—for resource-constrained devices, defaulting to 1-shot is a reasonable starting point.

**Ablation of Adaptation SNR and Pre-trained Model Selection**: The choice of adaptation SNR (-3 vs. 24 dB) determines performance in the low-to-mid SNR range (-3 dB adaptation is better); the pre-trained model that is better before adaptation (depending on the noise source, `baseline` or `noise-aware`), after the same 1-shot adaptation, which one is the best model depends on the noise source—adaptation partially smooths out the gap in pre-training quality, further strengthening the conclusion that "pre-training need not be deliberately noisy."

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed the currently known lightest KWS continuous noise adaptation mechanism**: Only fine-tuning the last fully connected layer, 1 sample per class, trained for only 1 epoch. Across three real noise sources and the full SNR range of -3 to 24 dB, the adapted model consistently outperforms the pre-adaptation model (improvement of 4.9%–46.0% at SNR≤18 dB), compressing the data and computational overhead of on-device noise adaptation to an engineering-acceptable level.
2. **Falsified the intuition that "noisy pre-training helps dynamic adaptation" using a strict statistical process**: Controlled experiments under 25 repetitions and the full suite of Wilcoxon/Friedman/Holm tests show that multi-style pre-training with colored and indoor noise cannot transfer to on-site noise, offers no clear advantage for dynamic adaptation, and slightly damages clean accuracy (<0.54%).
3. **Established an adaptation evaluation protocol anchored at extreme SNRs with full-range scanning**: Proved that results of adaptation at intermediate SNRs are enveloped by the two boundary curves of -3/24 dB, allowing the performance interval of all conditions to be estimated with two experiments. Also identified that "when to adapt" (when pre-adaptation accuracy already exceeds 90%, the benefit is questionable) is a prerequisite decision problem for the implementation of continuous learning.
4. **Systematically characterized the quantitative relationship between the three adjustment knobs of adaptation overhead** (number of epochs, number of shots, adaptation SNR) **and benefits**, providing a default configuration for resource-constrained deployment (1-shot, 1 epoch, adaptation under heavy noise conditions).
5. **Methodology and code are fully open source** (GSC v2 standard benchmark, 8 three-level noise sources, Python/PyTorch full stack), providing a reproducible baseline for subsequent deployment research on MCUs.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, the capacity ceiling of only tuning the last fully connected layer. This method bets that "noise drift can be absorbed by the linear decision layer." In heavy noise scenarios where the spectral structure is severely destroyed (e.g., Street music drags both pre-trained models to very low levels), re-weighting in the last layer is difficult to fully compensate—this explains why more epochs and samples are still needed to approach usable levels under heavy noise. When the spectral overlap between noise and speech is severe enough, the frozen backbone itself becomes a bottleneck. Second, the source of adaptation samples is not closed-loop: the 1-shot noisy samples in the experiments were prepared offline. On real devices, either clean keyword speech is pre-stored and mixed with real-time collected noise (following Cioflan's approach, still requiring storage occupation), or real noisy keywords are waited for and triggered for collection (how to label and how to ensure coverage of all 12 classes are not discussed). Third, "when to adapt" remains an unresolved trigger problem, which the paper itself lists as a core future work; the current protocol relies on manual selection of the adaptation timing. Fourth, the impact of adaptation on performance under clean conditions (the cost of catastrophic forgetting) is not reported by the paper—after adaptation pulls the model towards specific noise, whether performance drops in quiet environments or under other noise sources, there is no data.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First and foremost: **No practical tests were performed on the target hardware**. All experiments were completed on an i9-12900K plus RTX 3080. The paper is positioned as a proof-of-concept, and energy consumption, latency, and memory usage on the MCU are not reported—yet the paper's argument is precisely "lightweight enough to be deployable." This key claim currently has only design-level arguments, without measurement-level evidence (in contrast, the competitor Cioflan series has measurable numbers of 5.81 J and 14 seconds available for reference). Second, the paper does not report the number of parameters and storage occupation of the model itself, so readers cannot independently calculate the degree of "lightweight." Third, the adaptation ablation in Fig. 6 uses the same noise source and same SNR as the adaptation training during evaluation (matched-condition evaluation), posing an optimistic risk of adaptation samples being too close to the test distribution. Although Fig. 5 performs cross-SNR scanning, the noise source is still the same, and generalization across noise sources is not tested. Fourth, noise types only cover additive, single-source noise, not involving reverberation, superposition of multiple noise sources, and the coupling of noise with speaker changes. Fifth, the only metric is overall accuracy, lacking per-class accuracy, false alarm/miss rates, and inference latency reports required by KWS engineering. Sixth, the paper does not report the number of repetitions and significance tests for the adaptation stage experiments (25 repetitions are only used for the pre-training stage), and the 4.9%–46.0% improvement interval is not broken down by noise source and SNR conditions. Seventh, there are no head-to-head experiments with competitors like Cioflan et al. under the same framework; the overhead comparison is a qualitative statement across papers.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper explicitly lists two future works: First, truly deploy the pre-trained model and continuous noise adaptation on resource-constrained devices for real-time operation, verifying the "lightweight" claim with measured energy consumption, latency, and memory. Second, research mechanisms for automatically detecting when to perform adaptation—this is precisely the engineering implementation of the linkage between the confidence monitoring module and the domain adaptation module in Fig. 1. For example, using model confidence drops or VAD statistical features as adaptation trigger signals, turning "when to adapt" from a manual decision into a system-autonomous behavior. Combining experimental findings, there are several natural extensions: extending adaptation evaluation to cross-noise-source generalization and measuring catastrophic forgetting after adaptation; exploring minimal-ratio low-rank or bias-only parameterization beyond last-layer fine-tuning, breaking the capacity limit of the linear layer while keeping memory increase controllable; linking adaptation triggers with SNR monitoring to form an event-driven strategy of "adapt only under heavy noise, do not waste compute in quiet environments"; and repeating the validity conclusion of 1-shot adaptation on real product-level KWS metrics (false alarm rate, miss rate). Overall, the value of this paper lies not in the model structure, but in how cleanly it cuts the engineering problem of "the minimum sufficient conditions for on-device noise adaptation": 12 samples, 1 epoch, 1 layer of parameters, evaluation anchored at both ends—and an honest negative conclusion: noisy pre-training cannot replace runtime adaptation.
