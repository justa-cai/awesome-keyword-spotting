- **Authors/Affiliations**: George Igwegbe, Martins Awojide, Mboh Bless, Nirel Kadzo (Carnegie Mellon University, Africa)
- **Date**: June 2025
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01024
- **Keywords**: speech commands, keyword spotting, Kinyarwanda, low-resource language, wake word, data collection, edge device deployment

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Speech commands constitute a special category within spoken language corpora: unlike standard speech recognition corpora that involve reading full sentences, they consist of a small control vocabulary composed of single words or phrases. Typical components include general instructions (e.g., "Start", "Stop", "Left", "Right", "Play", "Record" in English corpora), confirmation words ("Yes", "No"), digits 0 to 9, and brand-customized wake words ("Hey Alexa", "Hey Siri", "Ok Google"). The purpose of such vocabularies is not transcription, but device control: when a user speaks a keyword, the Keyword Spotting (KWS) model on the device recognizes it and triggers an action or wakes up a larger AI system. The paper emphasizes that these models are typically stored and run locally on the device, leading to four expectations: smaller model size, less computation, lower energy consumption, and sufficiently high accuracy to avoid false alarms triggering downstream systems. These four points represent the fundamental difference in engineering constraints between edge-side KWS and server-level speech recognition.

The paper’s entry point for pain points is the inequality of language resources. Speakers of mainstream languages such as English, French, and German can enjoy voice features on smart devices because well-curated speech command datasets and fine-tuned models exist; however, speakers of African languages have no equivalent functionality. The authors specifically point out an asymmetry: while there is no shortage of text-side work for African languages (the paper cites morphologically aware language models like KinyaBERT and text classification benchmarks like KINNEWS/KIRNEWS), the author team "has not found any work on speech commands for African languages" in their literature search—the existence of text resources instead highlights the void in speech command resources.

The choice of Kinyarwanda (the lingua franca of Rwanda) for the first phase is justified by clear reasons: Rwanda has already demonstrated a willingness to develop speech recognition technology and has accumulated voice data through crowdsourcing, making it one of the largest dataset languages on Mozilla Common Voice; there are also reports of local speech recognition products being deployed locally. In other words, this language is already in the "best data condition" tier among African languages. Using it for the first proof-of-concept offers the highest success rate and the strongest demonstration effect.

There is also a social motivation running through the entire paper: speech command devices are particularly valuable for people with disabilities. The paper cites cases where Alexa helps users with mobility impairments, visual impairments, or cognitive disabilities improve their lives. The primary vision of the project is to allow native Kinyarwanda speakers (especially users with disabilities) to control and wake up devices in their native language, rather than being forced to use the mainstream languages pre-installed in products.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper reviews three existing routes for speech command modeling through a literature review and points out the gaps in each for low-resource African language scenarios:

- **Prerequisites for the Howl route are not met.** The Howl library, built by Mozilla for the Firefox Voice project, is a representative open-source wake word detection work. It starts from open datasets like Common Voice, uses forced alignment tools such as the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) along with transcribed text to perform alignment, and then uses the TextGrid files produced by the aligner and the language's pronunciation dictionary to cut word-level audio from pre-aligned sentence-level recordings. It subsequently covers data augmentation, concatenation, training, and evaluation. The paper demonstrates its capabilities with two corpora: "Hey Firefox" and "Hey Snips". However, this route requires four things to be complete: sentence-level recordings, transcribed text, a language pronunciation dictionary, and a suitable forced aligner. For Kinyarwanda, the pronunciation dictionary and available aligners are precisely the most scarce links—this is the "why it doesn't work" at the toolchain level.
- **Knowledge distillation compression route lacks a source large model.** Another route involves compressing a large ASR model of a major language containing a large number of positive samples of target words into a small, fast command word detection model using knowledge distillation, and then further improving it using teacher-student training with positive and negative samples, producing models with lower latency and error rates in some studies. However, the prerequisite for this route is "having an available large ASR model for the target language," which low-resource languages恰恰 (precisely) lack, making the entire route unfeasible.
- **Existing word-level data coverage is incomplete.** The Multilingual Spoken Word Corpus (MSWC) provides word-level data for multiple languages, including Kinyarwanda, by segmenting sentence-level recordings from Common Voice. However, the paper's word frequency statistics (Fig. 1 for digits, Fig. 2 for instructions) show uneven coverage of the selected 23 command words, with a significantly insufficient number of samples for the digit "Zero" (Kinyarwanda: Zeru); more importantly, the custom wake word "Muraho Afrika" is self-created by the project, so no existing corpus could possibly have it. Furthermore, MSWC mainly provides positive samples and random voice samples, lacking adversarial negative samples (confusion words with similar pronunciations to the keyword), which are crucial for controlling the false alarm rate.
- **Bias issues have no ready-made solutions.** The paper also cites Hutiri's work, pointing out that KWS is the entry point for users to access larger AI services. A biased KWS system will directly destroy users' perception of product effectiveness and cause marginalized groups to feel excluded when product behavior is abnormal—this provides the motivation for "local speakers collecting their own corpora."

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

Under the triple constraints of "incomplete coverage of existing word-level data, no dictionary or aligner, and limited local engineering manpower and expertise," how to assemble a Kinyarwanda training corpus covering 23 command words (including a custom wake word), pair it with a baseline model iteration route from scratch (CNN to LSTM, spectrogram to MFCC, TensorFlow to PyTorch), and finally deploy the model to PCs, mobile phones, and edge devices to complete feasibility verification. This is a "dataset + end-to-end process" paper, not an architecture innovation paper—its unit of contribution is corpus, toolchain, and experience, not new network structures.

## Methodology

(This section is organized according to the structure of dataset papers: corpus design → data collection process → cleaning and quality control protocol → baseline model → deployment path.)

### Corpus Design: Logic of Selecting the 23-Word Vocabulary

The vocabulary is the core of corpus design. The paper follows the design principles established by the Warden speech commands dataset: the vocabulary must balance "manageability of the collection process" with "sufficient diversity to support practical applications," composed of common command words, digits, and words specifically designed to test the model's discrimination ability, making the trained model more practical for applications like IoT and robotics.

The final vocabulary (Table I, total 23 keywords) is divided into four layers:

- **Digit Layer (10 words)**: Zero/Zeru, One/Rimwe, Two/Kabiri, Three/Gatatu, Four/Kane, Five/Gatanu, Six/Gatandatu, Seven/Karindwi, Eight/Umunani, Nine/Icyenda. The range is limited to 0 to 9. The paper explains this is benchmarked against the T9 keyboard setting on remote controls and is consistent with similar work in other languages; a larger digit range can be expanded according to the digit requirements of the target device—the example given is the channel switching function of standard televisions, where two-digit channels require a two-digit vocabulary. This idea of "trimming the vocabulary according to digit count" directly links corpus scale to product requirements, a very pragmatic cut in vocabulary design.
- **Device Instruction Layer (8 words)**: On/Gucana, Off/Kuzimya, Ok/Sawa, Go/Genda, Left/Ibumoso, Right/Iburyo, Up/Hejuru, Down/Hasi. This set covers switches, confirmation, and four-way directions, forming the minimally usable device remote control vocabulary.
- **Confirmation Layer (2 words)**: No/Oya, Yes/Yego, corresponding to binary confirmation in voice interfaces.
- **Action and Wake Word Layer (3 words)**: Start/Tangira, Stop/Hagarara, and the wake word Hello Afrika/Muraho Afrika. The wake word directly borrows the project name, forming a brand-consistent "Hello Afrika" wake phrase.

Why this ratio? Digits account for 10/23 because digits are a high-frequency rigid demand in remote control scenarios, have short word forms, and are easy to collect; the 8 instruction words cover four directions plus switches, ensuring "the ability to truly control a device"; confirmation words are indispensable for closing the interaction loop; and the wake word is the anchor of the entire KWS semantics. Author's note: From a linguistic perspective, Zeru (zero) and Sawa (good/confirmation) are suspected loanwords (approximating English "zero" and Swahili "sawa"). The mixing of loanwords and native vocabulary is both a benefit and a difficulty for the model's acoustic coverage—their pronunciation variants in real user speech may be larger, a point the paper does not analyze in depth.

### Data Sources and Collection Process: Three-Source Assembly

The paper's data comes from three sources, each solving a piece of the puzzle (Fig. 4 provides the overall process flow).

**Source 1: Multilingual Spoken Word Corpus (MSWC).** The paper describes it as a layer above the Mozilla Common Voice plan, derived from segmenting sentence-level audio from Common Voice into spoken words, containing word-level databases for multiple languages including Kinyarwanda and Swahili (Reference [12] points to the MLCommons data page). The reason for choosing it is straightforward: it has already done the heaviest dirty work of "word-level segmentation," bypassing the dependency on forced alignment and dictionaries in the Howl route. The team extracted and organized available data for the 23 command words from MSWC (mainly positive samples and random voice samples). Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 provide word count statistics for digit and instruction keywords in MSWC, respectively—showing significant differences in sample sizes across categories. Specific values are not given in the main text (the paper does not report per-category sample counts).

**Source 2: Google Speech Commands (GSC).** The reason for introducing it is very specific: the MSWC word frequency statistics (Fig. 1) show insufficient data for the "Zero" (Zeru) category, creating a linguistic gap that cannot cover all 23 words. Introducing resources like GSC from external sources is to make the dataset more complete and balanced. This reflects a data strategy of "not pursuing single-source purity, but pursuing complete category coverage"—for low-resource languages, coverage takes priority over source singularity.

**Source 3: Local Native Speaker Collection.** MSWC lacks some keywords needed to build a robust wake word system (the wake word "Muraho Afrika" is inevitably missing), so self-collection is required. The choice of the collection toolchain is a representative engineering decision in this paper: the team built a data collection application using Microsoft's Power Apps, SharePoint, and Power Automate application suite. Why choose no-code/low-code platforms instead of developing a custom mobile recording app? The paper does not argue point by point, but the motivation can be reconstructed from the context: the team is small (four authors covering literature, corpus, collection, cleaning, training, and deployment across the full chain), and the cycle is limited. The Power Apps suite solves form interfaces (Fig. 3 word list menu), cloud storage (SharePoint), and process orchestration (Power Automate) in one stop, and facilitates handing the application directly to local native speakers for use. The collection process is: hand the application to locals who speak the local language, with the interface prompting them to start recording. This application also undertakes the collection of positive and adversarial voice samples—the purpose of adversarial samples is to reserve negative examples for false alarm control, echoing the false alarm issues emphasized by Saidutta et al. in the literature review.

Scale of the local dataset (Section V-A): 23 keywords, approximately 140 speakers, approximately 130MB of audio samples and non-identifiable metadata. The qualifier "non-identifiable metadata" is noteworthy—the team explicitly does not retain speaker identity, balancing privacy and subsequent open release (the dataset is publicly available via two Google Drive links).

### Data Cleaning and Quality Control Protocol

The cleaning protocol consists of two steps: manual listening for each item and format repair.

**Manual Listening Exclusion**: Every audio sample from community collection and MSWC is reviewed, excluding five types of problematic samples—wrong words (saying something other than the target word), incomplete words, empty samples, overlapping voices, and excessive silence before speech. These samples are excluded during the training phase. Why these five? They correspond to label errors (wrong words), truncation (incomplete), invalid data (empty), multi-speaker contamination (overlap), and insufficient effective duration (long silence squeezing effective speech), which are the five most common types of dirty data in word-level classification. The paper states this is a quality control measure to ensure the integrity and reliability of speech models.

**Format Repair**: All collected samples underwent a round-trip conversion from OPUS compression back to WAV. The reason is written very specifically: the collection application used OPUS encoding during development to reduce audio file size, and the encoding process stripped the RIFF tags from the WAV header. Converting back to WAV was to restore the RIFF tags, allowing the files to be read normally by standard audio toolchains. This is a typical case of "mobile-side design decisions to save traffic/storage digging a pit for the training pipeline," explaining why the team plans to modify the encoding process of the collection application in future work.

It is worth pointing out that the entire cleaning protocol is purely manual listening for each item, without introducing procedural quality checks such as SNR thresholds, duration filtering, or automatic acoustic anomaly detection—this is feasible at the 130MB scale, but the paper itself lists "further data cleaning" as future work, indicating that the current protocol does not solve data quality issues in one go (the experimental results later will confirm this).

### Baseline Model and Training Route: Four-Step Iteration

The model's iteration route is a clear "from tutorial to self-developed" climbing path, with each step driven by specific problems.

**Step 1: CNN Baseline.** The starting point is the baseline model corresponding to the TensorFlow official "Simple Audio Recognition: Recognize Keywords" tutorial (Reference [16]): a two-layer convolutional neural network with MaxPool and Dropout. The input feature is converting WAV to spectrograms, trained on three datasets: "mswc_dataset", "local_collected_dataset", and "combined_mswc_local_collected_dataset" (the merged set of MSWC and local collection). Trained for approximately 150 epochs, using early stopping for regularization to mitigate overfitting. Why start with this tutorial model? Because it verifies the correctness of the entire data pipeline—the output of this step is "the data can run" rather than "the model is good enough," and 150 epochs plus early stopping is also a prudent extension of the tutorial-level configuration.

**Step 2: Switch to LSTM.** The paper observed that the baseline model "performed reasonably well initially on smaller datasets, but performance dropped significantly on larger datasets," so it turned to an LSTM-based model, which surpassed the CNN version (specific accuracy values for CNN and LSTM are not reported in the paper). Why did CNN fail when data increased? The paper does not dig deep into the mechanism, but from the feature side, the problem may lie not in capacity but in representation: the next step's feature change provides clues.

**Step 3: Switch Spectrogram to MFCC.** During LSTM training, the input feature switched from spectrograms to Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC). The reason given by the paper is that MFCC is "good at extracting features from datasets specifically designed for human voice classification." Interestingly, the supporting Reference [17] is a paper on automatic bird sound classification—the authors borrowed the feature engineering conclusion that "MFCC is superior to raw Mel spectrograms" from cross-domain (bird sound recognition). From speech processing common sense, MFCC's decorrelation and cepstral compression are indeed more suitable for the fully connected layers of small models; this choice has correct engineering intuition, even if the source of the argument is slightly unexpected.

**Step 4: Data Augmentation and PyTorch Rewrite.** To augment the dataset (Reference [18] is SpecAugment), four types of augmentation were applied to 80% of the dataset: shift, zero-padding, playback speed modification, and amplitude amplification. The augmented data was used to train a PyTorch-implemented LSTM model, and "the PyTorch model showed significant improvement compared to its TensorFlow predecessor" (the magnitude of improvement is not reported in the paper). Training was also extended to all three dataset categories. Why 80% instead of 100%? The paper does not explain; leaving 20% of the original data un-augmented is usually to maintain the anchor of the original distribution and prevent augmentation artifacts from dominating training, but this can only be noted as the author's speculation. Why switch to PyTorch? The paper does not give a framework-level attribution; combined with the context, it is speculated to be related to the flexibility of the subsequent Edge Impulse toolchain and custom training loops.

**Side Branch: Pre-training and Fine-tuning Attempt.** The team also explored pre-training a Kinyarwanda pre-trained model using the most frequent keywords in the MSWC corpus (identified by the confusion matrix as the most commonly used words), expecting the model to first absorb language-specific features and then fine-tune using locally collected data. This design motivation is the standard play in low-resource NLP (general first, downstream later), but the result "did not meet expectations"—the validation accuracy after fine-tuning was only 36.7% (Section VI-B). The failure of this side branch is itself informative: the distribution of high-frequency words in MSWC is misaligned with the target distribution of the local 23-word command set, and what the pre-trained model learned did not transfer well.

### Deployment Path: From Linux PC to Wio Terminal

The approach on the deployment side is the "shortest path to landing." The LSTM model was first deployed on a Linux PC, evaluating performance by inputting locally saved WAV files. Subsequently, the team used the Edge Impulse platform to deploy the model to local machines and edge devices, selecting Seeed's Wio Terminal for the edge device. Why use Edge Impulse? The paper states directly: the platform provides TensorFlow Lite models with quantization, simplifying the deployment stage; the team originally planned to do quantization themselves using the TensorFlow Lite API in the future, but "current time constraints and limited professional knowledge" led them to choose the ready-made tools of Edge Impulse. This is a rare frank expression in the paper—admitting the manpower gap in quantization engineering and using platform tools to run the deployment verification first. The abstract claims deployment targets of three types of devices: PC, mobile phone, and edge device; the main text details Linux PC and Wio Terminal (via Edge Impulse), while deployment details and test data for mobile phones are not reported in the paper.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Compared to the three existing routes, the difference in this paper lies in "bypassing" and "supplementing": relative to the Howl route, it does not perform forced alignment and dictionary construction, but directly consumes the already segmented word-level data from MSWC, filling the gap with self-collection; relative to the knowledge distillation route, it does not rely on a large ASR teacher model for the target language, but trains a small model from scratch; relative to Warden's Google Speech Commands, it targets a language that previously had no command word corpus, includes a custom wake word in the vocabulary, and plans the position of adversarial negative samples during the collection stage (although the actual addition is postponed to future work). Essentially, this paper reconstructs the problem of "doing speech commands for low-resource languages" from a model problem to a data engineering problem.

## Main Contributions

- **The first (according to the author team's literature search) speech command corpus and model work for African languages**, using Kinyarwanda as the first phase, directly responding to the asymmetric void of "many text language models, zero speech command resources."
- **A 23-word Kinyarwanda command vocabulary** (Table I): 10 digits, 8 device instructions, 2 confirmation words, 2 action words, and 1 custom wake word "Muraho Afrika," with English translations, which can serve as a template for subsequent African language command vocabulary design.
- **A no-code/low-code local collection toolchain** (Power Apps + SharePoint + Power Automate) and its accompanying manual quality control protocol, producing a local dataset of approximately 140 speakers, approximately 130MB, covering 23 keywords, while avoiding privacy risks with non-identifiable metadata.
- **A complete baseline iteration route**: CNN spectrogram baseline (approx. 150 epochs early stopping) → LSTM → MFCC features → four types of data augmentation (80% data) → PyTorch rewrite, plus a failed side branch of pre-training and fine-tuning, providing a "run first, optimize later" path reference for similar low-resource projects.
- **Multi-device deployment verification**: Linux PC direct inference, outputting quantized TFLite models via Edge Impulse for deployment to Wio Terminal edge devices.
- **Open Source**: Code and guides are hosted on GitHub (gigwegbe/hello_afrika_speech_commands), and the dataset is publicly available via two Google Drive links.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The experiments involve three training sets: MSWC extracted set, local collection set, and their merged set. The per-category sample counts on the MSWC side are presented in bar charts in Fig. 1 (digit class) and Fig. 2 (instruction class), with specific values not given in the main text (not reported by the paper); the known conclusion is that the Zero category is insufficient, prompting the introduction of GSC for supplementation. The local collection set consists of 23 keywords, approximately 140 speakers, and approximately 130MB of audio. The split ratio of training/validation/test sets, whether speakers are isolated across sets, and the final number of samples per category are not reported by the paper—this is the main gap in the experimental description of this paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The only quantitative metric adopted by the paper is validation accuracy. This choice is weak for the KWS task: in engineering, the false alarm rate (False Alarm, e.g., number of false wake-ups per hour) and false rejection rate (False Rejection) that truly determine usability are not measured, nor are F1, per-category accuracy, or confusion matrix values reported. The paper is not unaware of this—the literature review specifically discusses the method by Saidutta et al. using a three-class classification of "keyword speech/non-keyword speech/non-speech" to progressively refine and reduce false alarms to one-eighth without compromising baseline accuracy, calling it a ready-made method usable for corpus design or any deep KWS model; but in the experimental stage of this paper, neither this three-class scheme was implemented nor were false alarm metrics reported, with the addition of negative samples and adversarial words explicitly postponed to future work. As a reference baseline: the random accuracy for uniform guessing among 23 classes is approximately 4.3% (1/23, calculated by the author), which provides a floor line for interpreting the following numbers.

### Detailed Results and Comparisons (Section VI-B)

- **Pre-training and Fine-tuning Route**: Pre-trained using high-frequency words identified by the confusion matrix, then fine-tuned using local data, resulting in a validation accuracy of **36.7%** (Fig. 5)—the paper self-evaluates this as "not meeting expectations."
- **LSTM Main Route (Augmented PyTorch Version) Validation Accuracy on Three Datasets**: MSWC set **78.1%** (highest of the three, Fig. 6); Local collection set **36.8%** (Fig. 7); MSWC plus local merged set **71.8%** (Fig. 8).
- Specific values for the CNN baseline on the three datasets, the improvement magnitude of LSTM relative to CNN, and the "significant improvement" magnitude of the PyTorch version relative to the TensorFlow version are not reported by the paper.
- The deployment side (Linux PC, Edge Impulse/Wio Terminal) only performed functional verification (inference by inputting local WAV files), without reporting edge-side metrics such as latency, memory usage, model size, or accuracy difference before and after quantization (not reported by the paper).

### Attribution Analysis of Result Differences

The paper provides two attributions for the phenomenon that "the local set 36.8% is far lower than the MSWC set 78.1%, and the merged set 71.8% is actually lower than MSWC training alone" (Section VI-B): First, the distribution inconsistency between the MSWC and local collection sets may affect model performance; Second, the inherent diversity of the dataset—the local collection comes from over 140 speakers, a speaker queue significantly larger than the corresponding MSWC scale, with higher diversity also meaning larger variance.

On this basis, several layers of engineering interpretation can be added (author's analysis, not from the original paper): The merged set being lower than MSWC training alone indicates that under the current cleaning protocol, local data is a "negative asset"—the distribution expansion it brings exceeds the information increment it brings, which corroborates the failure of the pre-training and fine-tuning side branch with 36.7%, the two numbers being almost identical, pointing to the same conclusion: the domain gap between MSWC (and pre-training based on it) and local App collection is too large. Possible sources of domain difference include collection devices (mobile phone microphones vs. diverse devices in Common Voice), channels (OPUS lossy round-trip transcoding only occurs on local data, the paper does not evaluate its impact on audio quality), environmental noise, and residual label noise not removed by manual listening cleaning. Class imbalance among the 23 classes (word count differences shown in Fig. 1/Fig. 2) will also drag down the recall of minority classes, thereby lowering overall accuracy. Additionally, although 36.8% is far from the 4.3% random floor, it is still far from practical use—commercial wake word systems are usually measured by false alarm/rejection trade-off curves, and simple accuracy cannot determine how far the model is from being usable.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Thin evaluation metrics**: Only validation accuracy, no false alarm rate, false rejection rate, latency, or model size; for a system aiming to "wake up devices," the lack of false alarm metrics means the core risk is not quantified.
- **Non-scalable cleaning protocol**: Exclusion of the five types of problems relies entirely on manual listening for each item, without procedural SNR/duration/energy threshold filtering; this protocol is not sustainable as data scale increases, and the paper also lists further cleaning as future work.
- **Distortion introduced by lossy transcoding is not evaluated**: The round-trip conversion from OPUS to WAV restores RIFF tags but cannot restore acoustic details discarded by lossy encoding; the impact of this distortion on training is not measured by the paper.
- **Pre-training and fine-tuning route failed and was not deeply investigated**: The 36.7% result has only the phenomenon without mechanistic analysis; domain adaptation techniques (such as adversarial domain adaptation, channel normalization) were not attempted.
- **Quantization completely outsourced**: Quantization was handled by the Edge Impulse platform; quantization-aware training and post-quantization accuracy loss are not involved, with the team admitting limitations due to time and professional knowledge.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- The dataset split method (training/validation/test ratio, whether speakers are isolated) is not reported by the paper; if speakers overlap between training and validation, validation accuracy will be overestimated, requiring reservation in the interpretation of 78.1%.
- Per-category sample counts, class balance processing, and specific parameters of augmentation operations (shift magnitude, speed change range, amplification gain) are not reported by the paper, making the experiment not precisely reproducible.
- The abstract claims deployment on three types of devices: PC, mobile phone, and edge device; the main text only covers PC and Wio Terminal, with mobile phone deployment and test details missing.
- The three-class progressive refinement false alarm control method recognized in the literature review was not included in the experiment, leaving a gap between theory and experiment.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The directions planned by the paper themselves form a clear closing route: First, use existing frameworks to identify audio samples that lower validation accuracy as the basis for further data cleaning (upgrading cleaning from manual blind review to error-driven); Second, continue to expand the scale and speaker diversity of local collection, and modify the encoding process of the collection application to eradicate the OPUS transcoding problem; Third, supplement negative samples (especially adversarial words) before training to address false alarms in real-world use—this is a step to truly implement the false alarm methodology from the literature review; Fourth, evaluate the model on more edge devices; Fifth, add personalized interaction: calibration on first use, detection of user voiceprints, and providing feedback only to approved users, which aligns with the common practices of commercial voice assistants and responds to the bias concerns raised by Hutiri in the review. The author adds two more directions not mentioned in the paper but logically following: Domain mixing training or channel normalization for MSWC and local data to save the current dilemma of "merging leads to point drop"; and introducing false alarm/rejection curves as primary metrics to align the evaluation of "speech command system usability" with KWS industry standards.

One-sentence summary: The value of this paper lies not in model accuracy—accuracies of 36.8% to 78.1% are still far from practical use—but in the fact that it walked the complete path of "building a corpus from scratch for a language with almost no speech command resources, building collection tools, and running through edge-side deployment" and open-sourced it. Among them, the three-layer ratio of vocabulary design, the no-code collection toolchain, and the lesson of low-resource data domain difference where "merging data leads to point drop" are engineering judgments that can be transferred to other African languages and even all low-resource languages.
