## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) refers to the task of detecting specific words or phrases from a continuous audio stream and triggering a response. It serves as the voice interface for voice assistants, security systems, in-vehicle systems, medical aids, industrial control, and various edge devices. Unlike general Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), KWS does not require transcribing the entire speech segment into text; it only needs to determine whether the spoken input belongs to a predefined set of keywords. Consequently, KWS models can be significantly smaller than ASR systems, making them naturally suitable for local execution on devices.

The core background highlighted at the beginning of the paper is the divergence between cloud computing and on-device computing. Currently, the majority of speech recognition computation occurs in the cloud, creating a rigid dependency on constant internet connectivity. However, in scenarios where internet access is unavailable, latency is unacceptable, or data privacy is sensitive, recognition computation must be moved to the device itself, which is known as on-device computation. The paper illustrates this need with a home automation example: when a user says "open the door," the door should open immediately. If the recognition of this command still requires a round-trip to the cloud, the user experience and reliability are unacceptable.

The second background is the need for contactless interaction for special populations and scenarios. The paper mentions that hearing-impaired individuals can interact with others and devices via voice commands, and disabled individuals can operate machines using voice. During the COVID-19 pandemic, people were isolated and advised not to touch anything; in such contactless scenarios, all operations can be completed via voice commands, with KWS serving as the enabling technology.

The third, and the true foundation of this paper, is the inequality of language resources. Odia is one of India's classical languages, primarily spoken in Odisha. While English has a de facto standard resource like Google's 40-word Speech Commands dataset, there are no public KWS datasets for Odia, nor are there KWS systems optimized for edge devices. Existing Odia isolated word recognition works either rely on traditional toolchains or their models are not designed for edge constraints. In other words, this is not "another English KWS benchmarking" work, but rather a "from-scratch implementation of on-device KWS on a low-resource classical language" work—building the dataset, selecting the architecture, performing compression, and running it on a real development board.

The fourth pain point is the contradiction between model size and edge device resource budgets. Deep learning models are typically stored in float32, often occupying several megabytes, while edge devices have very limited memory and storage. Taking the three models trained in this paper as examples (Table 4), before quantization, the CNN is 1599 KB, the LSTM is 4040 KB, and the CNN+LSTM hybrid architecture is 3107 KB. Such sizes are a heavy burden for resource-constrained devices and must be compressed.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper systematically reviews existing approaches to on-device KWS through a literature review and exposes their respective shortcomings:

- **Gap in prior work for the same language**: Existing Odia word/number recognition studies (References [2][3][4], including the author's own 2023 IEEE InC4 conference work) are not designed for edge devices. The models can only run on general-purpose computers or the cloud and cannot be migrated to edge hardware with memory budgets in the hundreds of KB. This is the most direct entry point for this paper.
- **High threshold for dedicated hardware routes**: Some research has implemented low-power CNN KWS on the Movidius Myriad 2 Vision Processing Unit (Reference [6], using Google's dataset with ten words, 80:10:10 split, improving power consumption, throughput, and latency); other work designed a 65nm CMOS process, only 32 KB memory, 5 microwatt power consumption programmable LSTM accelerator chip Laika (Reference [8], trained on TIMIT, compressing 32-bit floating-point operations to 8-bit fixed-point). While these dedicated chip/accelerator routes yield good results, their high hardware costs and development thresholds make them unsuitable for low-cost general deployment.
- **Small vocabulary size in TinyML-based development board solutions**: Some research used CNN for a contactless elevator operating system deployed on an Arduino 33 BLE Sense board (Reference [11]), but it only had six labels with an accuracy of 83.5%. Both vocabulary scale and accuracy have obvious ceilings, far from practical command sets.
- **High deployment costs for large model pre-training routes**: An Italian 79-command voice interface for people with speech disorders (Reference [10]) fine-tuned three variants of Whisper (tiny/base/small), trained on 65,000 data points collected from 208 users with speech disorders, with whisper-small achieving a 95.9% word recognition rate; WAV2KWS (Reference [13]) performed transfer learning based on WAV2VEC 2.0 self-supervised representations, achieving 97.9% on Google's dataset. While these solutions have impressive accuracy, the parameter scale and inference overhead of pre-trained large models are not on the same order of magnitude as the resource budgets of edge devices.
- **Bias in dataset languages**: Available command word datasets in the literature are almost entirely concentrated in languages such as English (Google 40-word dataset [1]), Italian (13 labels [7], 79 commands [10]), and French (robotic arm control [12]); construction site voice command recognition (Reference [17], 1D CNN, 12 hours of raw audio, 45 volunteers, 13 ethnic language backgrounds) also uses English commands. Odia is completely absent.
- **Lack of comparison in architecture search and novel architectures**: Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has already found efficient DNNs with 184,000 parameters and 97.04% accuracy for KWS (Reference [9], Interspeech 2019), and Swin-Transformer has also been introduced to KWS (Reference [15]). However, for small datasets of low-resource languages, it lacks systematic empirical evidence on whether training these complex architectures from scratch is cost-effective and how traditional trios (CNN, LSTM, CNN+LSTM) perform relatively on this corpus.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the key challenges this paper aims to solve can be summarized into four points: (1) In the absence of any public Odia KWS dataset, build a sufficiently large, cleanly annotated 24-word dataset from scratch, starting from recording device selection, speaker recruitment, and vocabulary design; (2) Through controlled comparison on the same dataset among three candidate architectures (CNN, LSTM, CNN+LSTM), select the one with the optimal trade-off between accuracy and model size, and explain why; (3) Use INT8 quantization to compress the selected model's size to a range acceptable by edge devices, while ensuring accuracy does not collapse; (4) Complete deployment, inference, and memory profiling on real edge hardware (Raspberry Pi 3B) to prove that the entire solution is not just theoretical. Note that these four challenges are not difficult in the sense of algorithmic innovation, but represent a complete chain in the sense of engineering implementation—the value coordinate of this paper is "the first complete landing verification of on-device KWS for a low-resource language," and it should be evaluated using this coordinate.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system is a classic and complete offline keyword recognition pipeline (the workflow is shown in Fig. 2 of the paper): voice commands are collected in .wav format, first preprocessed—using the Pydub library to trim silence from the beginning and end of the audio signal (PRAAT tool was used for silence removal during dataset creation), then the signal is converted to 16-bit and from stereo to mono; then MFCC features are extracted using the Librosa library; features are fed into one of the three candidate models; the model output is given a classification result for 24 words via softmax.

The paper explains the reason for choosing MFCC at the feature layer: MFCC is the most commonly used feature in speech and audio processing. It captures the spectral characteristics of sound, representing the shape of the signal power spectrum. It first transforms the original audio into the frequency domain via Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT), then applies a mel-scale to approximate human auditory perception of sound frequency, and finally performs cepstral calculation on the mel-filtered spectrum to obtain coefficients. MFCC is valuable because it highlights components important for human speech perception and filters out less relevant information, thus being effective in tasks such as speaker identification, emotion detection, and speech-to-text. For keyword spotting, MFCC compresses each approximately one-second word-level audio into a small-sized time-frequency coefficient map, allowing subsequent convolutional networks to extract local patterns on it—this is the answer to "why use MFCC instead of raw waveforms": raw waveforms have too high dimensions and contain a lot of phase information, while MFCC preserves the spectral envelope structure necessary to distinguish words.

The design details of the three candidate models are as follows (the three deliberately keep hyperparameters consistent to form a controlled comparison):

- **CNN Model** (Fig. 6 of the paper): Three two-dimensional convolutional layers, each paired with a max-pooling layer for dimensionality reduction; ReLU activation for input and hidden layers, softmax for the output layer; batch normalization applied to input and hidden layers; kernel size 3×3, stride 2; the first two convolutional layers have 16 feature maps each, and the last convolutional layer has 64 feature maps. Hyperparameters: batch size 32, no dropout, learning rate 0.001, Adam optimizer, trained for 100 epochs.
- **LSTM Model** (Fig. 7 of the paper): Three LSTM layers, each with a dimension of 128 (i.e., all LSTM layers have 128 feature maps), softmax output layer. Hyperparameters are exactly the same as the CNN (batch 32, no dropout, learning rate 0.001, Adam, 100 epochs). The motivation for choosing LSTM is its widespread use in speech recognition and its existing application precedents on low-power devices controlled by FPGAs (Reference [18]'s ESE sparse LSTM engine).
- **CNN+LSTM Hybrid Model** (Fig. 8 of the paper): Two two-dimensional convolutional layers followed by two LSTM layers, each convolutional layer paired with max-pooling; ReLU for input and hidden layers, softmax for output; batch normalization for input and hidden layers; kernel size 4, stride 2; the first convolutional layer has 32 feature maps, the second has 64. Hyperparameters are also kept consistent.

From the perspective of design motivation (the paper's own narrative is brief, here we unfold the implicit "why"): CNN performs local convolution on the MFCC time-frequency map, naturally matching phoneme-level and syllable-level local time-frequency patterns (formants, energy spots of plosives), with high parameter efficiency and low data requirements; LSTM models the temporal dynamics of the entire speech segment through gated recurrent structures, theoretically more suitable for sequential signals like speech, but with a large number of parameters and high sample requirements, posing risks of overfitting and convergence difficulties on small datasets of over 3,000 entries; the hybrid architecture attempts to兼顾 ("balance") both "convolution for local features + recurrence for temporal dependencies." The three models share hyperparameters (batch 32, learning rate 0.001, Adam, 100 epochs, no dropout), so that the final performance differences can be mainly attributed to the architecture itself rather than the training configuration—this is the part of the experimental design in this paper closest to ablation studies. A learning rate of 0.001 is the default value for Adam, a conservative choice; not using dropout, like the lack of data augmentation, is a trade-off worth discussing in small dataset solutions but not elaborated on in the paper.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**MFCC Extraction (Equation 1)**:

$$MFCCs = DCT(\log(MelFilterBank(|FFT(Windowed\ Frame)|^2)))$$

Translated into plain language layer by layer: The windowed and framed signal first undergoes FFT to obtain the spectrum, and the squared modulus is taken to obtain the power spectrum; the power spectrum passes through a set of Mel filters (Mel Filter Bank), compressing the linear frequency axis into a Mel scale that simulates human auditory perception—human ears have high resolution for low frequencies and low resolution for high frequencies, and the Mel scale reproduces this non-linearity; the logarithm is taken for each Mel frequency band, converting multiplicative intensity relationships into additive ones and compressing the dynamic range; finally, a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) is performed, concentrating the energy distribution of the log-Mel spectrum into the first few orders of coefficients to obtain cepstral coefficients. Low-order coefficients characterize the spectral envelope (vocal tract shape information, key to distinguishing phonemes), while high-order coefficients characterize spectral details (often considered noise and discarded).

**INT8 Quantization (Equations 2–5)**: The paper adopts TensorFlow's post-training quantization, mapping float32 weights to 8-bit integers. The quantization formula is:

$$X_{quant} = round(scale \times X + zero\_point) \quad (2)$$

The dequantization formula (used during inference to recover floating-point approximations) is:

$$X_{dequant} = \frac{X_{quant} - zero\_point}{scale} \quad (3)$$

The scale and zero point are determined by the value range of the tensor:

$$scale = \frac{255}{max(X) - min(X)} \quad (4)$$

$$zero\_point = -round(scale \times min(X)) - 128 \quad (5)$$

The principle of this affine quantization mapping is worth expanding: the value interval $[min(X), max(X)]$ of the float32 tensor is linearly mapped to the 256 discrete levels representable by INT8. The denominator is 255 rather than 256 because the value range span covered by 256 integer codes is 255 levels; scale is the floating-point step corresponding to each increase in an integer level; zero_point records the landing point of floating-point 0 on the integer axis. The reason for introducing zero_point is that the distribution of weights and activations is often asymmetric (e.g., activations after ReLU are all non-negative). Pure symmetric quantization (such as int8's -128 to 127 symmetric about 0) would waste half of the code bits. Affine quantization utilizes the entire INT8 value range effectively by shifting the zero point. During inference, convolutions and matrix multiplications can be completed directly in the integer domain (accumulating additions after multiplication using int32), with dequantization only performed at layer boundaries—this is precisely the root cause of why 8-bit integer operations are faster and more efficient on edge hardware than float32, and the route pointed to by both Reference [8]'s chip work and Reference [19] (Google's classic paper on integer arithmetic inference, which is the theoretical source of this paper's quantization method). Ideally, float32 to int8 should have a 4x compression ratio. The paper's actual measurement is about 3x (Table 4: 1599→521, 4040→1358, 3107→1035 KB). The difference comes from unquantized or partially quantized components and format overhead in the model, which the paper does not break down layer by layer to explain the去向 (destination) of this 0.25x loss.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Self-built Odia 24-word Dataset

This is the most irreplaceable contribution of the entire paper. In the absence of any public Odia KWS dataset, the authors collected speech from 15 speakers (10 males, 5 females, aged 20 to 30, Table 1) using a Zoom H1n recorder. Each person read each of the 24 words ten times, resulting in a total of 3600 audio samples (15 × 24 × 10, Table 1). The vocabulary (listed in the paper's vocabulary table, labeled as Table 3 in the text, note that this number conflicts with the results table) covers three categories of high-frequency command semantics: numbers 0 to 9 (Odia pronunciations Suna, Eka, Dui, Tini, Chari, Pancha, Chha, Sata, Aatha, Na), seven colors (Neli Green, Lal Red, Sabuja Blue, Haladia Yellow, Narangi Orange, Baigani Purple, Dhala White), and seven directions (Uttara North, Dakshina South, Purba East, Paschima West, Dahana Right, Aaga Front, Bama Left). The "why" behind the vocabulary design is clear: numbers, colors, and directions are the command categories with the highest frequency of use and cleanest semantic closure in home automation, device control, and assisted interaction. The scale of 24 words is significantly larger than the 6-label scheme in Reference [11], moving closer to a practical command set. Recording specifications are 16-bit, mono, .wav format (Table 2); silence at the beginning and end is removed using PRAAT. It is necessary to point out an internal contradiction in the original text: the abstract and main text repeatedly state a sampling rate of 41,000 Hz, while Table 2 states 44 KHz. The two are inconsistent, and reproducers need to verify this themselves.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Controlled Comparison of Three Architectures on the Same Dataset

A horizontal comparison of three mainstream architectures (CNN, LSTM, CNN+LSTM) on the same dataset, same features, and same hyperparameters, leading to the conclusion that CNN wins with 97% accuracy (Table 3). This conclusion itself is not novel, but it is the first empirical evidence for the specific corpus of Odia, and it has transferable reference value for small dataset scenarios: when training samples are only in the thousands, a pure convolutional architecture with high parameter efficiency and inductive bias matching the local structure of time-frequency maps is more stable than recurrent architectures and hybrid architectures with parameters several times larger. Looking back at Table 4, we can also see the impact of architecture on size: LSTM is the largest at 4040 KB before quantization, CNN is the smallest at 1599 KB, and the hybrid architecture is in the middle (3107 KB)—this directly corresponds to the parameter expansion caused by the four gating matrices of LSTM layers.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: INT8 Quantization Compression and Real Development Board Memory Profiling

The quantization process itself uses TensorFlow's mature post-training quantization capabilities (theoretical source is Reference [19]). The innovation lies not in the algorithm but in the complete closed loop of "quantization + edge deployment + memory profiling": after quantization, the model size is reduced to about one-third (Table 4), accuracy does not drop (Table 5), and it is deployed on a Raspberry Pi 3B for actual testing. Memory profiling is performed on the three quantized models—the peak memory usage for CNN and LSTM is both 12.35 KB, and for CNN+LSTM it is 12.92 KB (Fig. 12 of the paper). The conclusion is that the memory of most edge devices exceeds 100 KB (the conclusion section further states that edge device memory is generally around 256 KB), so these models can be deployed on any edge device for on-device inference. Here, it is necessary to distinguish between "model size" and "peak memory": model size is the size of the quantized weight file (521 to 1358 KB), while peak memory profiling gives the runtime working memory during inference (about 12 KB). The two differ by more than an order of magnitude. The paper does not explain the specific measurement method for peak memory (whether it is the peak of single-layer activation or the entire tensor arena), so reproducers need to pay attention to the口径 (metric).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Comparing one by one with the routes in the literature clarifies the position of this paper. Compared with dedicated chip routes (Laika [8], Movidius solution [6]), this paper takes the route of general hardware plus software quantization, requiring no tape-out or dedicated accelerators, with strong replicability and low thresholds, at the cost of abandoning hardware-level extreme power consumption. Compared with large model transfer routes (Whisper fine-tuning [10], WAV2KWS [13], Swin-Transformer [15]), this paper trains lightweight architectures from scratch, with training costs and deployment occupancy several orders of magnitude smaller, but the accuracy ceiling is limited by data scale (3600 entries compared to 65,000 or Google's full set). Compared with the NAS route (Reference [9], 184,000 parameters, 97.04%), the CNN in this paper still weighs 521 KB after quantization, with parameter efficiency one level lower, but it avoids the expensive search process, making it more friendly for rapid landing on low-resource languages. Compared with the author's own previous Odia isolated number recognition work ([2], only number vocabulary, aimed at general computing), this paper expands the vocabulary to 24 words, introduces quantization compression for the first time, and completes edge development board deployment verification for the first time—advancing from "can recognize" to "can run on the edge." Compared with contemporary edge KWS application works (Arduino elevator solution [11] with 6 words, 83.5%; French robotic arm solution [12] with 94.7%), this paper has advantages in both vocabulary scale and accuracy (24 words, 97%), and is currently the only on-device KWS solution covering Odia in the literature.

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

The dataset is self-built by the authors (explicitly stated in the paper because no public Odia KWS dataset is available): 15 speakers (10 males, 5 females), aged 20 to 30 (Table 1); each person reads each of the 24 words 10 times; a total of 3600 audio entries (Table 1); recording device Zoom H1n, 16-bit quantization, mono, .wav format (Table 2); sampling rate inconsistency between main text and table (41,000 Hz vs. 44 KHz, see above). Silence at the beginning and end is removed using the PRAAT tool. The dataset is split into training, testing, and validation sets at 80%, 10%, and 10% respectively—note that the paper does not specify whether this split is random by sample or by speaker. If it is the former, recordings from the same speaker will appear in both the training and testing sets, and the measured 97% belongs to speaker-dependent accuracy, which cannot be extrapolated to unknown speakers. This is a question mark that must be raised when evaluating the scale of this dataset and the scope of applicability of the conclusions.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper adopts four standard metrics for classification tasks: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 Score (Table 3). The reason for selection is that the task is modeled as a 24-class closed-set classification—each word-level audio belongs to and only to one word. After the collection design of 24 words × 15 people × 10 times, the class distribution is naturally balanced, so both accuracy and F1 can unbiasedly reflect overall discrimination ability. However, from the perspective of evaluation conventions in the KWS field, this set of metrics has obvious gaps: real-world deployed KWS performs detection from continuous audio streams, and the domain-standard False Alarm Rate/False Rejection Rate (FAR/FRR) curves, DET curves, and operational metrics like "False Alarms per Hour" are all unreported; there are also no "unknown word/background noise" classes, so the system's ability to reject out-of-vocabulary speech cannot be evaluated. Additionally, the paper reports two deployment-side metrics: model size before and after quantization (Table 4, KB) and inference peak memory (Fig. 12, KB), which are correct supplements from an edge deployment perspective, but inference latency, throughput, and power consumption are also missing—these three are exactly the dimensions reported in focus by edge-side key works such as Reference [6], which are not measured in this paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Internal Three-Architecture Comparison (Table 3)**: CNN leads comprehensively, with Accuracy/Precision/Recall/F1 all at 97%; CNN+LSTM follows closely, with the four metrics at 96/97/96/96; LSTM falls significantly behind, with the four metrics at 85.6/87/86/86. The accuracy advantage of CNN over LSTM is 11.4 percentage points, and over the hybrid architecture is 1 percentage point. This ranking is worth expanding to explain "why": First, the discriminative information between words on the MFCC feature map is mainly reflected in local time-frequency patterns (the trend of energy peaks in specific Mel frequency bands over time), and convolutional kernels capture these patterns with very few parameters; Second, the training scale of 3600 entries is small for the gating parameters of three LSTM layers with 128 dimensions, and recurrent networks are prone to suboptimal convergence or overfitting (the paper did not use dropout or data augmentation, exacerbating this risk); Third, the hybrid architecture feeds convolutional features into two LSTM layers, inheriting the local feature advantage of CNN, so it is only 1 percentage point lower than pure CNN, but the parameter expansion of the LSTM part makes it neither more accurate nor smaller, having no existence value in edge scenarios—this is the complete logic for the paper selecting CNN as the object for quantization deployment and asserting that "CNN outperforms the other two models on our dataset." From the dimension of size (Table 4 before quantization), CNN at 1599 KB is also the smallest among the three, LSTM at 4040 KB is the largest, and CNN dominates in both accuracy and size, belonging to Pareto dominance.

**Comparison Before and After Quantization (Table 4 and Table 5)**: In terms of size, CNN is compressed from 1599 KB to 521 KB (3.07x), LSTM from 4040 KB to 1358 KB (2.98x), and CNN+LSTM from 3107 KB to 1035 KB (3.00x). The paper uniformly describes this as "about 3x reduction." In terms of accuracy, CNN remains unchanged at 97%, CNN+LSTM remains unchanged at 96%, and LSTM slightly increases from 85.6% to 86% (Table 5). The conclusion of zero accuracy loss is consistent with the general experience of INT8 post-training quantization: the 48 dB signal-to-noise budget of 8 bits is sufficient for classification decision boundaries. The phenomenon of LSTM increasing by 0.4 percentage points can be explained in two ways—quantization noise acted as a slight regularizer, or the test set itself only has 360 entries, where flipping one sample is about 0.28 percentage points, and this difference is within the magnitude of statistical noise. The paper did not perform significance testing on this.

**Horizontal Comparison with SOTA Numbers in Literature** (based on numbers given in the paper's literature review): The NAS solution [9] achieved 97.04% with 184,000 parameters, WAV2KWS [13] achieved 97.9% on Google's dataset, the Italian Whisper-small solution [10] achieved 95.9%, the French transfer solution [12] achieved 94.7%, the construction site solution [17] achieved 93.8%, the Arduino elevator solution [11] achieved 83.5%, and the Italian edge solution [7] had a word error rate of 15.6%. The 97% of CNN in this paper is at the upper end of this sequence, basically on par with the NAS solution; but it must be emphasized that comparability is limited—the paper's test set is 10% of the 3600 entries (about 360 entries), single recording device, close-talk, clean channel, same-distribution data, with difficulty far lower than public benchmarks involving cross-device, noisy, and out-of-vocabulary words. Thevalue of 97% cannot be directly equated with numbers on public datasets.

**Deployment-side Comparison (Fig. 12)**: The peak memory for inference of the three quantized models on edge devices is 12.35 KB for both CNN and LSTM, and the highest for CNN+LSTM is 12.92 KB. The paper argues based on this: common edge device memory exceeds 100 KB (the conclusion section states about 256 KB), so all three models can perform on-device inference on any edge device. It is worth noting the detail that the peak memory of CNN and LSTM is exactly the same (12.35 KB), implying that this profiling measures the activation/tensor buffer peak during inference runtime rather than weight residency; otherwise, the weight difference of 521 KB vs. 1358 KB could not fail to be reflected. The paper does not disclose the measurement methodology, which is a blank spot in result interpretation.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Strictly speaking, the paper did not design ablation experiments—there was no layer-by-layer breakdown, no feature substitution comparison, no hyperparameter sensitivity scan, and no control for quantization granularity (INT16/INT8/INT4) or quantization method (post-training quantization vs. quantization-aware training). The only two built-in controls that can bear the function of "ablation" are as follows, with their findings:

**Architecture Ablation (CNN vs. LSTM vs. CNN+LSTM, Table 3)**: Under fixed dataset, features, and hyperparameters, removing temporal modeling (pure CNN) performs best, adding recurrent structure (hybrid) slightly decreases performance, and pure recurrence (LSTM) decreases significantly. The translatable judgment from this finding is: for small dataset tasks of word-level isolated word classification, the inductive bias of MFCC local time-frequency patterns plus convolution is more important than long-range temporal modeling; the value of LSTM can only be realized on longer sequences, stronger temporal dependencies, or larger training sets.

**Compression Ablation (Before and After Quantization, Table 4, Table 5)**: INT8 quantization exchanges about 3x size reduction for negligible accuracy change (two models持平/unchanged, one slightly increased by 0.4 percentage points). The finding is: for KWS models of this scale, post-training INT8 quantization is a "free lunch" level compression method, deployable without retraining. Ablation dimensions not explored by the paper (and thus constituting space for future work) include: the impact of the number of MFCC coefficients and frame length/stride, the role of dropout and data augmentation, layer-by-layer quantization granularity, whether quantization-aware training can further compress to INT4, and whether the ranking of the three architectures remains under speaker-independent splits.

## Main Contributions

1. **First Odia KWS dataset and complete system for edge devices**. Under the condition of zero resources in the public domain, a dataset of 15 people × 24 words × 10 times totaling 3600 entries was self-built (Table 1). The vocabulary covers three categories of high-frequency command semantics: numbers, colors, and directions. A complete pipeline from silence cropping (Pydub/PRAAT), mono 16-bit conversion, MFCC extraction (Librosa) to classification is equipped (Fig. 2), filling the gap in the on-device KWS landscape for this Indian classical language.
2. **Controlled comparison of three architectures and clear architectural conclusions**. CNN, LSTM, and CNN+LSTM were horizontally evaluated on the same data, same features, and same hyperparameters (batch 32, learning rate 0.001, Adam, 100 epochs, no dropout), leading to the conclusion that CNN dominates in both accuracy (97%, Table 3) and smallest size (1599 KB, Table 4), providing direct evidence for architecture selection in low-resource language small dataset scenarios.
3. **Zero-accuracy-loss compression via INT8 quantization and edge empirical verification**. Quantization reduced the size of all three models by about 3x (Table 4), with accuracy remaining unchanged (Table 5); the quantized CNN is only 521 KB. All models were deployed and tested on Raspberry Pi 3B. Memory profiling showed that inference peak memory was only 12.35 KB and 12.92 KB (Fig. 12), demonstrating feasibility within the memory budget of typical edge devices.
4. **Demonstration of a low-cost general hardware route**. Contrasted with dedicated chips (Laika [8]) and large model transfer (Whisper [10], WAV2KWS [13]), this paper proves that the combination of "lightweight architecture + post-training quantization + general development board" is sufficient to land on-device KWS with 24 words and 97% accuracy on low-resource languages. The methodology can be directly migrated to other languages lacking public data.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Speaker-dependent and extremely narrow population coverage**: The dataset only has 15 speakers (10 males, 5 females), aged 20 to 30 (Table 1), with single Zoom H1n close-talk recording, no accent variation, no noisy channels, and no far-field reverberation. The model's generalization ability to unknown speakers, elderly/children voices, and noisy environments is completely untested. The 97% accuracy only holds within the training distribution.
- **Closed-set classification rather than true streaming detection**: The system recognizes pre-cut, silence-removed isolated words. There are no "unknown word/background noise/silence" classes, no streaming detection mechanism, so it cannot reject out-of-vocabulary speech, nor can it discuss FAR/FRR trade-offs—there is still a substantial distance from the form of actual KWS products.
- **Low model parameter efficiency**: The quantized CNN still weighs 521 KB (Table 4). Compared with the efficiency of the NAS solution (184,000 parameters, 97.04% [9]), this paper's model does not have much margin on MCU-level (hundreds of KB Flash) devices. The compression ratio of about 3x rather than the theoretical 4x also implies the presence of unquantized components in the model, which the paper did not break down layer by layer for further compression.
- **Reproducibility gaps in technical details**: The sampling rate contradicts itself between the main text (41,000 Hz) and Table 2 (44 KHz); key parameters of MFCC such as the number of coefficients, frame length, and frame shift are not reported by the paper; the measurement caliber for "peak memory" is not explained (CNN and LSTM are both 12.35 KB despite a 2.6x difference in weight size, suspected to be activation buffer peak); it is not stated whether the training and testing splits are isolated by speaker. These directly limit third-party reproduction and result verification.
- **Misalignment in hardware argumentation**: The deployment platform, Raspberry Pi 3B, has 1 GB of memory, belonging to a Single Board Computer rather than the microcontroller-level edge devices with "about 256 KB memory" mentioned in the paper. Running successfully on a Raspberry Pi and measuring 12 KB peak memory does not automatically imply "can be deployed on any edge device"—the latter requires actual testing on MCU-level platforms (e.g., Cortex-M with TFLite Micro), and latency and power consumption were not reported in this paper.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Lack of positive baselines against SOTA architectures**: Only the self-built three models were compared. Standard small architectures for edge KWS such as DS-CNN, cFSMN, and BC-ResNet were not introduced, nor was a fair comparison with aligned parameter counts performed. Therefore, the conclusion that "CNN is best" only holds within the trio.
- **Incomplete evaluation metric system**: No FAR/FRR/DET curves, no per-class error analysis (the confusion matrix is only shown as an image in Fig. 11, and the main text mistakenly describes it as fifteen classes, which does not match the 24 words), no measurement of inference latency/power/throughput, no variance and significance testing over multiple runs. A fluctuation of 0.4 percentage points under a single test of 360 samples cannot be distinguished from real differences.
- **No ablation and no robustness testing**: Design variables such as MFCC configuration, dropout, data augmentation, quantization granularity, and quantization-aware training were not ablated. Stress tests with added noise, added reverberation, and cross-device recording are all absent, leaving the model's failure boundaries unknown.
- **Methods listed in the literature review were not converted into control experiments**: The review details the accuracy numbers of various routes, but the experimental part did not reproduce any of them as runnable baselines. The horizontal comparison remains at the citation level.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Data-side expansion**: Expand the speaker scale from 15 to hundreds, covering multiple age groups and accents. Add enhanced and actual recording materials with noise, far-field, and multiple devices. Split the training and testing sets by speaker isolation, upgrading the evaluation from speaker-dependent to speaker-independent. Further introduce "unknown word" and "silence" classes to align the task form with real KWS.
- **Architecture and compression-side upgrades**: Introduce architectures designed for edge devices such as depthwise separable convolutions (DS-CNN class) or BC-ResNet and perform parameter-count-aligned comparisons. On top of INT8 post-training quantization, try combinations of quantization-aware training, INT4 mixed precision, pruning, and knowledge distillation to further compress the model to the tens of KB level. Break down layer-by-layer quantization overhead to explain the gap between 3x and 4x.
- **Deployment-side measurement completion**: Migrate to true microcontroller platforms such as Cortex-M level MCU with TFLite Micro. Supplement measurements of inference latency, throughput, peak current, and power consumption. Plot the accuracy-latency-power triangle trade-off and evaluate the operational point of streaming detection using FAR/FRR curves.
- **Methodology spillover**: Promote the landing template of "self-built small dataset + controlled comparison of three architectures + post-training quantization + development board profiling" to other low-resource Indian languages lacking public corpora (such as Santali, Ho, etc.), or combine cross-lingual transfer learning and self-supervised pre-trained features to alleviate the dependence on data volume for training from scratch.
