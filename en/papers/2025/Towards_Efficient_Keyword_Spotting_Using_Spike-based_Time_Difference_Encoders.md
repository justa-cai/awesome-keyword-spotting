## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the most typical task for "always-on" computing in the Internet of Things era: smart speakers, smartphones, and wearable devices all require a background-running voice interface to continuously listen for wake-up words. The paper opens by citing analyses from Thompson et al. (2020, 2021), pointing out that the trajectory of algorithmic progress in current AI is technically unsustainable alongside hardware energy consumption, and edge-side embedded systems are precisely the scenarios with the harshest constraints (Rabaey et al., 2019)—the dual pressure of low power consumption and low latency limits the deployment of traditional CPU/GPU architectures. Neuromorphic computing offers an alternative route: Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs), as the third generation of neural networks (Maass, 1997), possess two properties crucial for edge-side KWS: (1) asynchronous computation, which naturally exploits the sparsity of event-driven sensors—computation is triggered only when spikes arrive; (2) the computation compartments themselves carry intrinsic temporal dynamics such as leakage, where "time" here is an explicitly utilized computational resource rather than a dimension that needs to be flattened by sliding windows. Platforms such as IBM TrueNorth and Intel Loihi have demonstrated low-latency, low-power potential in tasks such as vision-electromyography gesture recognition, tactile sensing, and KWS (Blouw et al., 2019).

The entry point of this paper is more fundamental than simply "running KWS on different hardware": how valuable is temporal information in speech? Biological auditory research (Kayser et al., 2010; Schnupp et al., 2006; Panzeri et al., 2010, 2014) has repeatedly proven that millisecond-precise spike time encoding carries critical information from the peripheral auditory system to the cortex and directly participates in behavioral discrimination; whereas mainstream KWS pipelines (HMM+MFCC, or frame-level features fed into ANNs) essentially rely on rate and energy spectrum, with temporal structure only indirectly utilized in the sense of sliding windows. The engineering question the paper aims to answer is: Can a neuron model dedicated to "computing the time difference between two spike trains" make the temporal structure of speech a first-class citizen, while achieving order-of-magnitude savings in computational load on neuromorphic hardware?

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

- **Feedforward SNNs miss temporal information**: Pure feedforward CuBa-LIF (current-based Leaky Integrate-and-Fire) networks lack information pathways across time steps and can only "remember" a short history via the membrane potential time constant. In the paper's experiments, its accuracy was only 71.59% (Table 3), nearly 20 percentage points lower than the recurrent version, indicating that a significant amount of temporal information exists in this task that feedforward structures cannot utilize. Literature (Perez-Nieves et al., 2021; Bouanane et al., 2023; Müller-Cleve et al., 2022) also consistently confirms: recurrence brings improvements only when the dataset contains significant temporal information.
- **Recurrent SNNs are costly**: Recurrence allows LIF networks to achieve 90.04% accuracy (Table 3), but the cost is that the number of intra-layer connections grows quadratically with the number of hidden neurons (the LIFrec connection count in Equation 12 contains an $N_{L1}^2$ term), and spikes within layers are repeatedly amplified. Table 3 shows that the L1 layer of LIFrec, with only 65 hidden neurons, generates 151,076 synaptic operations (SynOps), totaling 175,548 for the entire network; whereas the TDE network, using 540 hidden units, requires only 14,417, a reduction of 91.79%. More troubling is that the activity of recurrent layers cascades downstream: the number and type of neurons in the L2 layer are identical for all three networks, but LIFrec's L2 SynOps (24,395) are still about 2.3 times that of TDE (10,768)—the paper specifically points out that this is a scalability hazard for deeper networks.
- **Fully connected layers with random initialization are neither interpretable nor prunable**: The weights $W_1$ from input to hidden layer in LIF/LIFrec are dense matrices learned during training, making it impossible to directly read out "which feature contributes to which class"; network scale can only be chosen empirically based on the number of neurons per layer, lacking a data-driven method for determining size.
- **Incomplete energy proxy metrics**: Literature commonly uses SynOps as a proxy for neuromorphic processor energy consumption, but generally only counts operations for "integrating input spikes." The paper points out that spike generation itself (e.g., membrane potential reset) also consumes dynamic energy (citing Lemaire et al., 2022), and should be included in the metric.

### Key Challenges to be Solved

The core challenge can be summarized in one sentence: **Achieving recurrent temporal modeling capabilities within a feedforward connection budget.** This is specifically broken down into four sub-problems: (1) What neuron model can directly convert the time difference between two input spikes into firing rate—i.e., the TDE cell; (2) How to fairly compare the three candidate networks—balancing them by total synaptic connections (a proxy for memory usage) rather than by number of neurons; (3) How to determine network size and provide physically grounded initialization starting from the data itself—mutual correlation-driven pruning; (4) How to make trained parameters interpretable—reverse-solving the output layer weight matrix into interpretable features of "frequency pairs + time constants."

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire pipeline (Fig. 1): Speech → Offline formant decomposition → 32-channel frequency-amplitude representation → L0 encoding layer converts to spikes → L1 hidden layer (three variants) → L2 output layer (11 neurons, one per class, decoding takes the most active firing one).

Regarding feature selection, the paper deliberately avoids MFCCs and uses formants: formants are defined in speech science as the main spectral frequency components corresponding to vocal tract acoustic resonances, with literature estimating that the first three formants are sufficient to distinguish vowels and semi-vowels (Welling and Ney, 1998; Laszko, 2016). The specific method involves using the Matlab Sinewave Speech Analysis Toolbox (Ellis, 2004) to extract the top three formants with the highest energy for each keyword and their amplitudes. Why choose formants: they compress the entire spectrum into "a few energy peaks moving over time," which perfectly matches the TDE's input assumption—the amplitude of each peak is modulated into a string of spikes, and the temporal relationship between two peaks is exactly what the TDE calculates. The frequency axis is quantized into 32 equal-width channels, covering the main human speech frequency band of 0–4 kHz, with a bandwidth of 125 Hz per channel; if two formants fall into the same channel, the maximum amplitude is taken—this is an intentional priority trade-off to avoid introducing pseudo-information from energy superposition. The amplitude time series of each frequency channel constitutes the spatiotemporal pattern being characterized.

L0 consists of 32 CuBa-LIF neurons, one per channel, converting the amplitude sequence of that channel into spikes via neuronal current. This is the unified "amplitude-to-spike" encoder for the entire network, with all channels using isomorphic parameters (Fig. 2 provides conversion examples for the words "oh" and "three"). Thus, the input space equals the frequency domain, and temporal evolution equals formant movement, forming a spatiotemporal spike code.

L1 is the only variable layer, with three configurations (Fig. 3): (i) TDE network—L1 consists entirely of TDE cells; (ii) LIFrec—CuBa-LIF with intra-layer recurrent connections; (iii) LIF—CuBa-LIF pure feedforward. The neuron types, quantities, and cellular parameters of the L0 and L2 layers are locked across all networks. This is a standard controlled variable design, aiming to attribute all differences in performance, energy efficiency, and interpretability solely to the neuron model and connection patterns of L1. Connections from L1 to L2 are fully connected, and the weights $W_2$ of this connection are trained in all networks.

Fairness design (Sec 2.4, Equation 12): Balancing the three types of networks by total synaptic connections—each TDE cell has only 2 inputs (facilitatory input and trigger input), so the connection count is $2 \cdot N_{L1} + 11 \cdot N_{L1}$; LIFrec is $32 \cdot N_{L1} + N_{L1}^2 + 11 \cdot N_{L1}$; LIF is $32 \cdot N_{L1} + 11 \cdot N_{L1}$. The authors' rationale is: the number of synapses approximates the memory occupancy of hardware implementation (synaptic weight capacity), which is closer to a true resource metric than the number of neurons. Verification: 540 TDE cells correspond to 7,020 connections ($540 \times 13$), 65 LIFrec cells also correspond to 7,020 ($65 \times 108$), and 163 LIF cells correspond to 7,009 (the closest value constrained by integer neuron counts); the largest tier of 992 TDE cells corresponds to 12,896 connections, requiring approximately 94 LIFrec cells and 300 LIF cells to balance (Table 4). The number 992 itself is significant: the total number of ordered frequency pairs for 32 channels is $32 \times 31 = 992$, exactly covering the maximum network where "each pair of frequencies builds one TDE in each direction."

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**CuBa-LIF Base (Equations 1–4)**: Membrane potential follows $\tau_{mem} \cdot dU/dt = -(U - U_{rest}) + RI$; synaptic current follows $\tau_{syn} \cdot dI/dt = -I + \sum_j W_{ij} \cdot S_j$ (feedforward input term) $+ \sum_j V_{ij} \cdot S_j$ (recurrent term, present only in LIFrec), where $S_j$ is the presynaptic spike sequence. Discretized as: $I(t) = \alpha I(t-1) + \sum_j W_{ij} \cdot S_j(t)$; $U(t) = (\beta U(t-1) + I(t)) \cdot (1 - U_{reset})$, where $\beta = \exp(-dt/\tau_{mem})$ is the membrane potential decay constant, $\alpha = \exp(-dt/\tau_{syn})$ is the synaptic current decay constant, and input resistance is set to $R = 1$.

**TDE Model (Equations 5–8)**: A dual-input presynaptic compartment is added before the CuBa-LIF. Each spike of the facilitatory input injects an exponential decay trace with time constant $\tau_g$ into the Gain compartment: $G(t) = \gamma G(t-1) + \sum_j W_{ij} \cdot S_{fac}(t)$, $\gamma = \exp(-dt/\tau_g)$; when a spike arrives from the trigger input, the increment of synaptic current is not a fixed weight but "the gain value at that moment": $I(t) = \alpha I(t-1) + \sum_j G(t) \cdot S_{trig}(t)$. Subsequent excitatory postsynaptic currents integrate and fire according to the CuBa-LIF membrane dynamics. The net effect: the shorter the interval between the two input spikes, the less the gain $G$ has decayed, the larger the injected current, and the higher the output firing rate—**the output firing rate is inversely proportional to the time difference between the two inputs**, and it is direction-sensitive: there is only an output if the facilitatory spike precedes the trigger spike, not the other way around. Therefore, a pair of frequencies naturally defines two TDE cells (each managing one direction), which is the source of $992 = 32 \times 31$.

**Training (Equations 9–11)**: BPTT (Backpropagation Through Time) with surrogate gradient learning, where the derivative of the spike firing nonlinearity is replaced by a fast sigmoid: $\sigma(U) = U / (1 + \lambda |U|)$ (Equation 9, $\lambda=5$, Table 1); the loss function is the softmax cross-entropy of the output layer spike counts (Equation 10); parameter updates cover both weights and TDE time constants: $W_{ij} \leftarrow W_{ij} - \eta \cdot \partial L/\partial W_{ij}$, $\tau_g \leftarrow \tau_g - \rho \cdot \partial L/\partial \tau_g$ (Equation 11), using the ADAM optimizer with dropout and weight decay. Hyperparameters (Table 1): time bin 0.015 s, simulation step $dt$ 0.015 s, membrane time constant $\tau_{mem}$ 0.002 s, synaptic time constant $\tau_{syn}$ 0.008 s, learning rate 0.0015, weight decay 0.0001, dropout probability 0.1. The trainable parameter sets for the three types of networks differ: TDE network learns $\tau_g$ and $W_2$; LIFrec learns $W_1$, $W_{11}$, $W_2$; LIF learns $W_1$, $W_2$.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Writing "Time Difference" into the Presynaptic Compartment—TDE as a Single-Cell Coincidence Detector

The TDE itself is not a new model (Milde et al., 2015 proposed it inspired by biological visual optic flow circuits, D'Angelo et al., 2020 used it for event-based motion detection); the new contribution of this paper is placing it on frequency pair inputs for KWS. The design logic chain is clear: Section 3.1 first uses information theory to prove that the pulse time pattern information in this data is sufficient, so the most economical architecture is not to let the network "learn out" temporal correlations itself (the route of recurrent LIF), but to make "two-spike correlation detection" the native function of the neuron—each TDE cell binds to a pair of frequency channels, specializing in answering "do these two formants appear sequentially on the time scale of $\tau_g$?" This is equivalent to moving feature engineering from the training stage into the cell definition, with the cost of an additional gain compartment per cell, but the benefit of reducing connections from quadratic in the hidden layer to constant per cell (2 inputs).

An important simplification decision: **The facilitatory side weights are fixed at 1 and do not participate in training.** Why—the authors explicitly state this is to "utilize the temporal dynamics of TDE through time constants rather than weight matrices." This choice achieves three benefits in one: reduces trainable parameters (for the 540-cell tier, TDE has 6,480 parameters, LIFrec has 7,020, Table 3); $\tau_g$ gains direct physical meaning (the time scale of that frequency pair); and the reverse-solving of output weights $W_2$ is not polluted by learned $W_1$, preserving interpretability. $\tau_g$ is trained independently per cell: low $\tau_g$ filters out inputs with long time differences (gain decays fast, gain is zero when intervals are long), high $\tau_g$ allows responses even with long intervals—effectively learning a dedicated time window for each frequency pair, with the network automatically adjusting the time constant dynamics for every frequency pair in the spectrum.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Data-Driven Network Sizing and Initialization (Mutual Correlation Pruning)

Since TDE outputs maximally when the two inputs are highly correlated (coincidence detector property), the authors further thought: directly measure the unbiased mutual correlation of the pulse sequences of each pair of frequency channels in the input data, sort all 992 candidate TDE cells by mutual correlation value, and prune starting from the lowest correlation pairs (Fig. 6); simultaneously, use the lag corresponding to the maximum mutual correlation for each frequency pair as the initialization for that cell's $\tau_g$. This simultaneously solves two engineering problems: (1) Network scale no longer relies on guesswork—given resource budgets and accuracy requirements, one can slide along the sorted table, and Table 2 is the complete scale-accuracy trade-off table; (2) Initialization has physical basis rather than being random. Control experiments show: pruning to 540 cells, mutual correlation pruning yields 87.86%, while random pruning yields only 85.83%; pruning to 186 cells, the gap widens to 83.7% vs 79.53%; pruning to 23 cells, it is 49.35% vs 38.47%, and the standard deviation of random pruning spikes to 6.30 (Table 2).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: SynOps Energy Proxy Metric Including Firing Operations

Equation 13: $SynOps = \sum_j S_{in,j} + \sum_j S_{out,j}$, counting both "integrating an input spike" (adding to membrane potential) and "firing an output spike" (resetting membrane potential) as event-driven operations. Rationale: SynOps in literature generally only count input integration, while the dynamic energy on the firing side is not negligible (Lemaire et al., 2022); including it makes the assumption closer to the true energy composition of asynchronous hardware. This metric compresses network activation sparsity and inter-layer connectivity into a single computational cost indicator (the Neurobench approach of Yik et al., 2023).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to LIFrec: TDE cells are more complex (one additional leaky compartment), but connections drop from quadratic per layer to constant per cell, and each cell's input semantics are fixed as frequency pairs. Compared to LIF/LIFrec using $W_1$ to learn features: TDE solidifies input-to-L1 connections according to frequency pairs, with feature selectivity coming from design rather than training—exchanging for interpretability and data efficiency. Compared to Blouw et al. (2019)'s Loihi KWS baseline: such works verify the energy efficiency upper limit of SNNs on hardware, with the neuron model itself being generic; this paper reverses the approach, first modifying the neuron model to then match neuromorphic hardware. Compared to traditional MFCC+HMM pipelines: formants plus spike time codes replace cepstral acceleration rate codes, and the classifier changes from a generative model to an SNN trainable via BPTT.

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

TIdigits corpus (Leonard and Doddington, 1993): Dictated digits from 225 adult speakers, 11 classes (digits 0–9 plus another reading of zero 'oh'), sampled at 20 kHz, with 450 repetitions per class. Word duration is about 400 ms, embedded in a fixed-duration 1500 ms window with random start times (preserving random speech onset positions). After formant preprocessing, each sample becomes a spatiotemporal spike pattern of 32 frequency channels × 1500 ms. Training and test sets are randomly split with balanced classes (same number of samples per class).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Classification Accuracy**: The reporting口径 (perspective) in the paper is somewhat special—the average of the top 25 epochs with the highest test set accuracy during training, reporting the mean and standard deviation of 3 independent random initializations. The authors' reason is to reduce the random fluctuation of test accuracy. This perspective is optimistic, as detailed in the limitations discussion.
- **Spike Count and Synaptic Operations per Layer**: Presented for the entire dataset, averaged per keyword, and counted after training completion (Table 3).
- **SynOps (Equation 13)**: Used as a proxy metric for asynchronous neuromorphic hardware energy consumption, counting both input integration and output firing operations, rationale see Innovation 3.
- **Information Theoretic Quantities $I_{rate}$ and $I_{pattern}$ (Fig. 5, method following Kayser et al., 2009)**: Calculating the mutual information for "retaining only rate information" and "retaining temporal pattern information" under different time bins $\Delta t$ for the spike pattern of 400 ms starting from the first spike of each neuron, taking the mean and standard deviation across the 32 frequency channel neurons.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

**Temporal Information Analysis (Fig. 5)**: Temporal pattern information is significantly higher than rate information under larger time windows, and they are comparable in the 15–20 ms range. This not only proves the importance of temporal information in the task but also inversely supports the choice of the 0.015 s time bin hyperparameter falling within a range of sufficient information content.

**Accuracy (Table 4, balanced by connection count)**:
- 12,896 connection tier (992 TDE / 94 LIFrec / 300 LIF cells): TDE 89.08 (std 1.25), LIFrec 91.11 (std 1.07), LIF 71.46 (std 0.44);
- 7,020 connection tier (540 / 65 / 163): TDE 87.86 (std 1.12), LIFrec 90.04 (std 0.80), LIF 71.59 (std 2.72);
- 2,418 connection tier (186 / 32 / 56): TDE 83.70 (std 1.55), LIFrec 83.03 (std 5.14), LIF 68.73 (std 0.34).

Three observations: (1) Feedforward LIF is suppressed at 68–72% across all scales, and experiments found that about 160 hidden neurons reach its capability limit—the lack of temporal modeling capability cannot be compensated by scale; (2) In the smallest scale tier, TDE surpasses LIFrec (83.70 vs 83.03), and LIFrec's standard deviation is as high as 5.14, indicating that recurrent networks are unstable in training at small scales; (3) In the largest scale tier, TDE lags behind LIFrec by about 2 percentage points.

**Inference Energy Efficiency (Table 3, 7,020 connection tier, averaged per keyword)**: In terms of spike count, TDE has 1,095 for the whole network, 53.19% less than LIFrec's 2,339; the L1 layer has 975 vs 2,213 (55.95% less). In terms of SynOps, TDE has 14,417 for the whole network, 91.79% less than LIFrec's 175,548 (i.e., the 92% mentioned in the abstract); the L1 layer has 3,572 vs 151,076 (97.64% less). Most notably in the L2 layer: the L2 neurons of the three architectures are identical, but LIFrec's L2 SynOps (24,395) are still about 2.3 times that of TDE (10,768)—the high activity of the recurrent layer "infects" downstream; if the network is deepened, this gap will amplify layer by layer, which is direct evidence for the paper's scalability conclusion. Another valuable counter-example warning: the spike count (898) and SynOps (21,771) of feedforward LIF are both lower than LIFrec, but the accuracy is only 71.59%—sparsity itself is not valuable; "operations per unit accuracy" is the key metric.

**Training Data Efficiency (Fig. 8)**: Reducing the training set from 100% to 75%, TDE shows no statistically significant change, while LIFrec shows a statistically significant drop; the overall decline slope is flatter for TDE. The authors' explanation is two-fold: having 7% fewer parameters is the surface reason; more essentially, TDE solidifies input-to-L1 connections by design, effectively injecting structural priors into the network, so feature mapping does not rely on learning from data.

**Interpretability (Fig. 9, taking the top-25 weights with the largest absolute value in trained $W_2$)**: (1) The distribution of selected frequency pairs is concentrated in the first half of the spectrum—0 to 1500 Hz, while the display range is 3000 Hz (Fig. 9 top-left), corresponding to the main frequency components of spoken words; (2) Overlap between classes is small—each point in the spectrum is basically occupied by only one class (color), forming localized population coding; (3) The distribution of $\tau_g$ has phonetic significance: for example, the characteristic time constant for "oh" is longer (energy peaks between two frequencies last longer on average), while "seven" corresponds to shorter time scales (Fig. 9 top-right); (4) An counter-intuitive finding: the discriminative frequency pairs learned by training overlap very little with the frequency pairs having the highest mutual correlation in the input analysis stage—the average distance of frequency pairs selected by the network on the spectrum is significantly larger than those selected by mutual correlation analysis (Fig. 9 bottom-left and bottom-right table), indicating that "maximum coincidence" does not equal "most discriminative power," and pure statistical data analysis cannot replace the training process.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The core ablation is the pruning strategy comparison in Table 2 (mutual correlation sorted pruning vs. random pruning, accuracy is the mean and standard deviation of 3 independent trainings):
- 992 cells (no pruning): 89.08 (std 1.25);
- Pruned to 676 cells: 87.49 (std 0.47) vs random 86.64 (std 0.97);
- Pruned to 540: 87.86 (std 1.12) vs 85.83 (std 0.46);
- Pruned to 378: 86.58 (std 1.22) vs 83.95 (std 0.92);
- Pruned to 186: 83.70 (std 1.55) vs 79.53 (std 1.77);
- Pruned to 68: 68.36 (std 0.54) vs 61.77 (std 0.63);
- Pruned to 23: 49.35 (std 0.98) vs 38.47 (std 6.30).

Three conclusions: (1) Information pruning outperforms random pruning at all levels, and the advantage is greater for smaller networks; (2) Pruning 45.5% of neurons and connections (992→540) costs only 1.22 percentage points of accuracy (89.08→87.86), which is precisely the basis for the paper selecting the 540-cell tier for subsequent detailed comparisons; (3) A cliff-like drop occurs below about 68 cells (68.36%), indicating that discriminative information is still hidden in low mutual correlation pairs, and mutual correlation sorting fails in the aggressive pruning interval—this corroborates the finding in Fig. 9 that "high mutual correlation does not equal high discriminative power."

## Main Contributions

1. **First introduction of the TDE neuron model into KWS**, proving that under the same synaptic budget (connection count balancing), it achieves accuracy close to recurrent LIF (89.08 vs 91.11, Table 4), while reducing synaptic operations by 92% (Table 3). The engineering implication of this number: on event-driven asynchronous hardware, operation count directly maps to dynamic energy consumption.
2. **Improved SynOps energy proxy metric**: Including firing-side operations in the metric (Equation 13), which is closer to the true energy composition of asynchronous hardware than the literature's conventional practice of only counting input integration.
3. **Proposed a data-driven TDE network sizing and initialization method**: Pruning based on input frequency pair mutual correlation sorting, with a 45.5% scale reduction costing only 1.22 percentage points of accuracy (Table 2), and outperforming random pruning at all pruning levels, providing neuromorphic engineers with a tool to "scale networks according to available resources."
4. **Training data efficiency**: The TDE network shows no statistically significant accuracy loss when training data is reduced to 75%, making it more robust than LIFrec (Fig. 8), which has direct value for edge-side small data scenarios.
5. **Interpretability**: Trained network parameters can be reverse-solved into "discriminative frequency pairs per class + characteristic time constant $\tau_g$" (Fig. 9), systematically revealing the deviation between features selected by training and those selected by input mutual correlation analysis—this is a negative piece of evidence against "using pure statistical analysis for feature selection."
6. **Quantified the temporal pattern information content of TIdigits after formant encoding using information theory** (Fig. 5), providing data support for "KWS should seriously utilize time encoding," and also explaining why feedforward LIF falls significantly behind.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Energy consumption arguments remain at the analytical level**: SynOps is a proxy metric; the paper itself admits in the conclusion that real energy consumption depends on specific implementation—digital neuromorphic processors need to update all leaky compartments at every algorithmic time step, introducing significant constant energy consumption; only asynchronous architectures can achieve "compute only when spikes arrive," and this property is already inherent in mixed analog-digital neuromorphic circuits: no algorithmic time steps, leakage is simulated by device physical properties (e.g., capacitance) (Indiveri et al., 2009; Chicca et al., 2014), with energy efficiency higher than digital implementation (Joubert et al., 2012). The paper does not report any measured energy consumption (joules or power), and hardware energy verification is explicitly listed as future work.
- **TDE cells themselves are more expensive**: Compared to CuBa-LIF, they have one additional synaptic compartment and one synaptic trace, which is not advantageous in digital synchronous implementation. The authors' argument is: on mixed analog-digital chips (such as DYNAPs, Moradi et al., 2017; Texel, Greatorex et al., 2024), the complexity cost of TDE cells is negligible relative to the 92% SynOps savings—but this argument chain has not yet been closed with actual measurements in this paper.
- **Accuracy still lags by about 2 percentage points**: 89.08 vs 91.11 in the largest tier (Table 4); the paper does not study mechanisms to further compress this gap.
- **Formant extraction is done offline**: Done on the host using the Matlab toolbox; an on-chip frontend is still missing for end-to-end edge deployment. The authors suggest directions in the conclusion: neuromorphic frequency decomposition like biological cochleae (Liu et al., 2010) plus resonant filters to extract formants (Chang et al., 1956; Durrieu and Thiran, 2011).
- **Hyperparameters were not tuned separately by architecture**: The three types of networks share the same set of hyperparameters borrowed from Müller-Cleve et al. (2022) (Table 1); the authors admit this may be unfair to LIF/LIFrec, and programmatic tuning is needed in the future.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Dataset is too clean and small**: TIdigits consists of 11 classes of clean read digits, with no noise, no reverberation, no far-field conditions, and only 450 repetitions per class; this differs significantly from real wake-word scenarios (custom vocabularies, continuous listening, false alarm rate constraints).
- **Lack of external baselines**: Only compared with its own three SNN variants, without comparing ANN-based KWS models or Blouw et al.'s Loihi baseline under the same features; nor did it report AUC, false rejection rate/false alarm rate, and other common KWS metrics—what tier the absolute accuracy is at on more common benchmarks is not reported.
- **Reporting perspective is optimistic**: "Average of the best test accuracy of the top-25 epochs" essentially uses the test set for model selection; the paper does not describe an independent validation set; the sample size of 3 repetitions is also small (the standard deviation for the small network tier of LIFrec has already reached 5.14).
- **Redundancy of the time window is not analyzed**: Only about 400 ms of the 1500 ms window is speech; the paper does not report the impact of silence segments on SynOps statistics and inference latency.
- **Only a single hidden layer**: The scalability conclusion (that the SynOps burden of recurrent layers will amplify with depth) is inferred from L2 layer data, without deep network experimental verification.
- **Pruning metric fails in the aggressive interval**: A cliff drop below 68 cells (Table 2), but the paper does not provide an alternative metric for discriminative sorting.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Hardware implementation and measurement**: Verify on Intel Loihi (TDE's Loihi implementation already exists in the intel-nrc-ecosystem model repository, Renner and Khacef, 2022), or measure energy consumption on mixed analog-digital chips like DYNAPs or Texel, closing the loop to verify the energy efficiency conversion of 92% SynOps savings.
- **Completing the on-chip frontend**: Silicon cochlea for frequency decomposition plus resonant filters to extract formants, moving the entire pipeline "speech → formants → spikes → classification" onto edge devices, achieving a fully event-driven system.
- **Deep dive into temporal precision**: The authors explicitly propose based on Fig. 5 that data binning time precision should be reduced to maximize temporal pattern information—the current 0.015 s bin may not have fully exploited the information theoretically; subsequent work will explore the design of binning and simulation time.
- **Programmatic hyperparameter tuning by architecture** and scalability verification on deeper architectures.
- **Discriminative sorting metrics**: Using training-derived discriminative frequency pairs to replace or fuse with mutual correlation sorting, improving performance in the aggressive pruning interval.
- **Moving towards real-world scenarios**: Expanding to noisy data and real wake-word vocabularies, supplementing false alarm/miss metrics, and exploring sizing methods under custom keyword conditions.
