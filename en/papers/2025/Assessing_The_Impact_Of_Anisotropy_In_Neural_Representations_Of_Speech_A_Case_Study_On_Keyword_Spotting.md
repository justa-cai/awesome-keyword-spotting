# Assessing the Impact of Anisotropy in Neural Representations of Speech: A Case Study on Keyword Spotting

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Clara Rosina Fernandez, Séverine Guillaume (LACITO, CNRS, Paris Cité University, France); Guillaume Wisniewski (LLF, CNRS, Paris Cité University, France)
- **Date**: June 2025 (arXiv:2506.11096v1, submitted 2025-06-06)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11096
- **Keywords**: anisotropy, keyword spotting, wav2vec2 / XLSR-53 representations, subsequence DTW, rogue dimensions, query-by-example, computational documentary linguistics

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Contextual pre-trained speech representations learned directly from raw audio—typically frame-level representations extracted by models like wav2vec2 and HuBERT—have become foundational components for numerous speech applications. However, multiple studies on Transformer representations (citations [3][4][5][6][7]) have consistently observed an anomalous phenomenon: these representations exhibit strong anisotropy. Anisotropy refers to the highly uneven distribution of vectors in the representation space, where certain directions are exceptionally prominent, causing the similarity between two randomly sampled embeddings to often approach 1. In more intuitive terms: massive amounts of representations do not uniformly fill the entire high-dimensional space but are instead squeezed into a narrow conical region. For representation spaces with thousands of dimensions, this is counter-intuitive—two random directions in high-dimensional space should be nearly orthogonal, yet a global directional collapse occurs here.

This phenomenon is not unique to the speech modality; text representation research has taken the lead. Ethayarajh (citation [8]) demonstrated that early contextual models such as BERT, GPT-2, and ELMo already exhibited strong anisotropy; Godey et al. (citation [7]) extended these observations to other modalities (especially speech) and newer models (such as T5), proposing that anisotropy is an inherent property of self-attention architectures rather than a byproduct of training accidents. Timkey and van Schijndel (citation [4]) provided a more detailed mechanistic explanation: anisotropy primarily stems from a very small number of so-called "rogue dimensions"—dimensions with abnormally large magnitudes and variances that completely dominate cosine similarity calculations, distorting the overall representation distribution.

The pain point lies in the application side. Intuitively, this geometric pathology severely limits the model's expressive power and usability: if the similarity between any two frames is compressed into a narrow interval, then all classic usage patterns based on similarity—retrieval, clustering, nearest neighbor search, template matching—seem destined to fail. Consequently, the academic community has proposed several methods to mitigate anisotropy (the paper mentions the existence of such methods but does not list specific names). However, a more fundamental question remains unresolved: how much actual harm does anisotropy cause to downstream tasks? No previous study has answered this question—related work has basically remained at the metric level of "measuring the average distribution of distances between two frame representations," completely ignoring how these representations are used in real tasks. A pathology at the metric level is not necessarily a pathology at the task level; this gap is precisely the entry point of this paper.

The application scenario chosen by the paper is computational documentary linguistics. One of the core needs for linguists recording and analyzing endangered and low-resource languages is to locate all occurrences of a specific word or phrase within a corpus, thereby studying phonological variants, lexical patterns, and language usage (citation [11] describes Creole documentary work). The problem is that for the vast majority of low-resource languages, it is impossible to perform complete transcription before retrieval: linguists often lack annotated data, available transcription systems, and the skills or computational facilities required to learn a transcription system. Therefore, the only feasible technical route is "query-by-example": directly taking a short query audio segment and comparing it against the representation space of the corpus to find all phonetically similar segments. This route relies entirely on similarity between representations, thus bringing the question of "whether anisotropy is harmful" to the forefront.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

- **Evaluation Gap in Anisotropy Research**: Existing works (citations [3] to [8]) only report the average distribution of representation distances, anisotropy indices, and the existence of rogue dimensions, never linking these metrics to the performance of any downstream task. A pathological metric does not imply task harm; without task-level evidence, the value of all mitigation methods cannot be judged.
- **Lack of Task-Level Benefit Evidence for Mitigation Methods**: The paper points out that various anisotropy mitigation methods have been proposed in the industry, but due to the aforementioned gap, no method has been proven to "improve task metrics after correcting the geometry." Mitigation has become an end in itself.
- **Traditional Keyword Spotting is a Classification Paradigm Dependent on Annotations**: Conventional KWS (e.g., wake-word detection for voice assistants) trains a classifier, requiring a large number of annotated samples for each target word. The documentary linguistics scenario lacks these conditions entirely—target words are specified by linguists on demand, making annotation and fine-tuning impractical. The classification paradigm cannot be directly transferred.
- **Classic Query-by-Example is Fragile to Handcrafted Features**: The classic approach without training uses handcrafted features like MFCCs combined with DTW template matching. Handcrafted acoustic features retain physical acoustic content and are highly sensitive to speaker timbre, microphone channel, and recording environment. The feature distance for the same word pronounced by different speakers can be very large. The paper quantifies this gap using an MFCC baseline under the same protocol (see Table 2 in the Experimental Results section).
- **The Layer Selection Problem for Representations is Ignored**: wav2vec2-like models have over twenty Transformer layers, with significant differences in the type of information encoded by each layer. Using simply the "last layer" or "average of all layers" may not be optimal. The paper's layer-by-layer experiments (Figure 2) show huge differences in retrieval performance across layers, making layer selection an unavoidable decision in practical deployment (the paper also cites Bordes et al.'s Guillotine regularization conclusion that self-supervised models need to remove some layers to improve generalization).
- **Representation Evaluation Methods are Disconnected from Actual Usage**: Previous evaluations measured distances at the "frame-to-frame" level, while real retrieval systems use "sequence-to-sequence" dynamic alignment scoring. Whether frame-level anisotropy remains fatal after path accumulation in DTW has never been measured.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

The paper transforms an unresolved geometric problem into three experimentally testable sub-problems. First, at the metric level: confirm that the speech representations of the multilingual pre-trained model XLSR-53 indeed exhibit anisotropy, locate it to specific rogue dimensions, and provide layer-by-layer, dimension-by-dimension quantitative evidence. Second, at the task level: on the representations with the strongest anisotropy (the last few layers, where the median cosine similarity between random frame pairs is as high as 0.96, Figure 1), use pure similarity-driven subsequence DTW for transcription-free, training-free, and fine-tuning-free word retrieval to test whether retrieval accuracy remains usable—if so, it indicates that although anisotropy makes the absolute values of similarity uninterpretable, it does not destroy relative ranking information. Third, at the factor decomposition level: decompose retrieval quality into three controllable factors—handcrafted features vs. pre-trained representations, isolated word representations vs. contextual representations, shallow layers vs. deep layers—to identify which factor supports retrieval performance. Overall, this is an analytical paper: it proposes no new architecture or loss function, but designs a controlled experimental protocol to answer a long-standing question in the community.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The paper's "system" is a five-step analytical pipeline, with each step having clear motivational constraints.

**Step 1: Corpus and Query Set Construction.** Take the Spanish subset of CommonVoice, select 30 target words, find 10 sentences containing each word in the transcription, forming 300 queries in total; additionally, randomly sample 700 sentences containing no target words as distractors, forming a retrieval pool of 1000 sentences. Each query represents different pronunciation instances of the same set of words, deliberately covering diversity in accent, context, and pitch. Design motivation: evaluation must be conducted under conditions of "same word, multiple speakers, multiple contexts" to test whether representations have truly learned linguistic structures beyond physical acoustics; simultaneously, ensure that the set of relevant sentences for each word includes the source recording of the query itself, to test whether the representation can reliably identify the "source recording" (this is the most direct touchstone for the information content of similarity).

**Step 2: Query Word Boundary Localization.** Use Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA, citation [14]) to perform forced alignment on the transcription to determine the start and end positions of each word in the audio signal. Motivation: query-by-example requires precise word-level audio segments, while CommonVoice only provides sentence-level transcriptions, necessitating word segmentation by an aligner. This implicitly assumes (explicitly stated in footnote 1 of the paper) that the corpus is already segmented by phrases, and queries consist of one or more words.

**Step 3: Construction of Three Types of Query Representations (see Key Technical Innovation 2)**: MFCC baseline, XLSR-53 word-level representation, and XLSR-53 contextual representation. On the corpus side, the retrieval side uniformly uses XLSR-53 to encode full sentences, obtaining frame-level representation sequences for each sentence—XLSR-53 has 24 layers, each frame is a 1024-dimensional vector, and each vector corresponds to 1/49 seconds of speech (approximately 20.4 milliseconds).

**Step 4: Retrieval.** For each query, use subsequence DTW with cosine similarity as the frame-level base metric to rank the 1000 sentences from most similar to least similar. There are three motivations: First, the query sequence is naturally a subsequence of the target recording, with variable word length, speech rate, and pronunciation; nonlinear time alignment must be allowed, and DTW is the textbook-standard solution (Müller, citation [12]). Second, cosine similarity is precisely the metric invaded by anisotropy—anisotropy is a disease of cosine; if the diseased cosine is still effective in DTW, it directly proves that the actual harm of anisotropy is limited, pitting the "lesion" and the "efficacy" against each other on the same metric, which is the cleanest logic. Third, the entire process involves zero training and zero fine-tuning, matching the reality of documentary linguistics ("no annotations, no computational facilities"), allowing the conclusion to be attributed to the pre-trained representation itself rather than any adaptation process.

**Step 5: Evaluation.** Verify whether the retrieval results contain the target word according to the transcription, and calculate macro-averaged Precision@k and Recall@k; for each k, report the performance of the layer with the highest F1.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Anisotropy Metric.** The paper adopts the quantification method from literature [4], defining the anisotropy metric for representations at each layer (Formula 1):

$$A = E_{i \neq j}\left(1 - \cos(x_i, x_j)\right)$$

where $(x_i, x_j)$ are two word or audio representations randomly sampled from the corpus, and $E$ is the expectation over all random pairs. The paper's interpretation is: $A$ close to 1 indicates that the representation is highly anisotropic and concentrated in a restricted region of the space; $A$ approaching 0 indicates that the representation is more uniformly distributed in all directions. Author's Note: This interpretation has a sign inconsistency with the literal form of the formula—according to the formula, the closer the cosine similarity of random pairs is to 1 (i.e., the more anisotropic), the closer $1 - \text{cosine}$ should be to 0. From the paper's data (the value reported in the last few layers in Figure 1 is 0.96, and the overall sampling mean is 0.46), the reported value is only consistent with the text's discussion that "the last few layers have the strongest anisotropy" if understood as the "expected cosine similarity itself." That is, the paper actually measures and discusses the average cosine similarity of random frame pairs: approximately 0.46 overall, and as high as 0.96 in the last few layers. When reading, it is advisable to rely on the data and treat the formula notation with caution.

**Subsequence DTW Retrieval.** Given a recording representation sequence of $n$ frames and a query sequence of $m$ frames, the algorithm operates on an $n \times m$ similarity matrix, where each element is the cosine similarity between a frame of the recording and a frame of the query. Subsequence DTW, under the condition of allowing nonlinear time warping, finds the optimal alignment between the query and a continuous subsequence within the recording, allowing skipping of prefix and suffix frames in the recording that are unrelated to the query, thereby naturally adapting to changes in speech rate and pronunciation; the accumulated score of the alignment path is the similarity score of the query for the recording, used for global ranking of the 1000 sentences. The paper does not provide implementation details such as recursive formulas, step constraints, or bandwidth; the complete algorithm definition is cited from Müller's music information retrieval textbook (citation [12]); the paper also does not report the computational overhead per query or the total retrieval time.

**Rogue Dimension Analysis.** For the representation matrix of each layer, statistics are calculated dimension by dimension: the values of the two dimensions with the largest means, the median of the means of all dimensions, and the dispersion of values within the largest dimension (Table 1). The criterion is: if the mean of the largest dimension is several orders of magnitude higher than that of the second-largest dimension, it proves the existence of rogue dimensions that dominate similarity calculations (mechanistic explanation from citation [4]).

**Baseline Features.** The MFCC baseline takes 13-dimensional coefficients with a 25-millisecond sliding window, applied to the word-segmented audio cut by MFA (Experimental Setup section).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Task-Level Evaluation Paradigm—Turning "Is Anisotropy Harmful?" into a Testable Question

This is the paper's largest contribution point, with value in paradigm rather than algorithm. The paper's reasoning chain is quite ingenious: the alignment score of DTW is entirely accumulated from frame-level cosine similarities; if anisotropy truly destroys similarity information (the similarity between all frame pairs is almost equal), the alignment of DTW would be meaningless, and ranking would fail to distinguish recordings containing the target word from those that do not; conversely, as long as retrieval can hit sentences containing the query word with high precision, it indicates that these DTW similarities, which "rely on subtle differences," still carry effective information, leading to the conclusion: the actual impact of anisotropy is limited—although the absolute values of similarity are difficult to interpret (because they are all squeezed together), their relative magnitudes are still sufficient to identify linguistically similar recordings. This argument structure turns a geometric intuition problem into a yes/no question that can be directly answered by retrieval accuracy. It also provides a replicable template for anisotropy research: any question of "whether representation geometry pathology is harmful" can be replaced with "measure it on the downstream task that relies most on that metric."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Controlled Query Representation Construction Protocol with Three Factors

The paper constructs three types of representations for each query word, forming two controlled comparisons. First, MFCC representation (baseline): directly calculate 13-dimensional MFCCs on the word segment cut by MFA, representing handcrafted acoustic features without pre-training. Second, XLSR-53 word-level representation: cut the word segment audio separately and feed it into XLSR-53 for encoding, representing pre-trained representations but stripped of context. Third, XLSR-53 contextual representation: first encode the full sentence audio containing the word, then extract the frame vectors at the word's location according to alignment information, retaining the full context. Thus, MFCC vs. XLSR word-level isolates the factor of "pre-training or not," answering what pre-training brings; word-level vs. contextual-level isolates the factor of "context or not," answering the degree of dependency of XLSR representations on context. A methodological detail accompanies this: sentence representations on the corpus side are always calculated in full context, so when the query uses word-level representations, there is a systematic context mismatch between the query side and the corpus side—the paper explicitly points out that this mismatch is the reason for the poorer performance of word-level representations, rather than hiding it as an experimental flaw.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Dual-Scale Geometric Analysis of Layers and Dimensions

The paper does not stop at a single anisotropy index but performs analysis in two orthogonal directions. Longitudinal layer-by-layer: from Layer 0 (non-contextual Transformer input, i.e., the output of the convolutional feature extractor) to Layer 24, report the similarity distribution (Figure 1, distinguishing frame pairs from the same recording vs. different recordings) and rogue dimension statistics (Table 1) for each layer, and measure retrieval accuracy layer by layer (Figure 2). Transverse dimension-by-dimension: statistically analyze the distribution of the mean of each dimension to locate rogue dimensions. This dual-scale design makes "anisotropy" no longer a vague label: it reveals that anisotropy monotonically worsens with layer depth (median cosine similarity rises from 0.47 at Layer 0 to 0.96 at Layers 22-24, Figure 1), rogue dimensions exist in every layer except Layer 0 and are most extreme at Layers 22 and 23 (Table 1), while retrieval performance fluctuates significantly across layers, showing an unexplained sharp drop at Layers 15-17 (Results section). Geometric pathology and task performance are not synchronized at the layer dimension, which itself is an empirical counterattack against the "harm theory" of anisotropy.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to NLP anisotropy literature (citations [3] to [8]): those works diagnose or propose corrections at the metric level in the text modality; this paper is the first (to the authors' knowledge) to evaluate impact at the task level in the speech modality; citation [6] (Ait-Saada and Nadif) once questioned the harm of anisotropy in text clustering tasks; this paper advances similar skepticism to speech retrieval and provides sequence alignment evaluation closer to representation usage than clustering. Compared to documentary linguistics system works (citation [11], query-based retrieval for Creole): the goal is not to build a system, but to use this task as a measuring instrument, with conclusions falling on representation geometric properties. Compared to the classification paradigm of conventional KWS: no target word annotations, training, or fine-tuning are needed; new words can be queried immediately, at the cost of betting everything on the similarity structure of pre-trained representations. Compared to the route of "ASR transcription followed by text retrieval": it completely bypasses acoustic models, pronunciation dictionaries, and language models, which are the most lacking components in low-resource languages; the paper does not conduct comparative experiments with the zero-shot ASR route, which is an open gap. Compared to common layer usage conventions: the paper does not take the last layer nor average layers (footnote 3 states that layer averaging yields consistent conclusions but is hard to justify); instead, it scans all layers layer-by-layer and selects the layer with the highest F1, explicitly exposing layer selection as an application problem that needs to be faced.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

**Anisotropy Analysis Part**: Randomly sample 1000 recordings from the Spanish subset of CommonVoice, encode them with XLSR-53 (a wav2vec2 model pre-trained on 53 languages and 56,000 hours of multilingual audio, citation [10]), obtaining frame vector sequences of 24 layers, each layer 1024-dimensional, where each vector represents 1/49 seconds of speech; then randomly sample 1000 pairs of vectors to calculate cosine similarity. **Retrieval Experiment Part**: The same corpus, 30 target words × 10 sentences containing each word = 300 queries, plus 700 distractor sentences containing no target words, totaling a retrieval pool of 1000 sentences; the goal for each query is to find the 10 relevant sentences from the pool. The paper does not report the specific version of CommonVoice, total duration, number of speakers, or sentence duration distribution; it does not report the list of 30 target words and selection criteria; nor does it report the acoustic and dictionary configurations used by MFA for alignment and their alignment error rates.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The main metrics are macro-averaged (averaged per query) Precision@k and Recall@k: the former is the proportion of target words contained in the top k retrieval results, and the latter is the proportion of the 10 relevant sentences appearing in the top k. Rationale (paper footnote 2): these metrics directly correspond to the actual usage of documentary linguists—linguists use retrieval results to analyze word distribution and usage, and top-k hit rate and recall rate are the system's usability itself; finer-grained "whether the word is precisely located within the recording" can be evaluated via DTW alignment paths, but the authors found their results harder to interpret, so they were not adopted. **Layer Selection Protocol**: For each k, select the layer with the highest F1 to report results (explained in Table 2), which is equivalent to an oracle upper bound on layer selection; footnote 3 adds that the direction of conclusions remains unchanged after averaging all layers. The paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests, nor does it report variance across repeated experiments.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The paper does not compare with any external SOTA systems (no ASR baseline, no dedicated QbE system baseline); comparison is entirely internal among the three self-built representations, with core results in Table 2 (taking the layer with the highest F1 for each k):

| k | Precision MFCC | Precision XLSR-53 | Recall MFCC | Recall XLSR-53 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100.0% | 100.0% | 5.7% | 5.7% |
| 2 | 57.2% | 85.1% | 6.4% | 9.6% |
| 3 | 40.4% | 78.3% | 6.8% | 13.5% |
| 5 | 27.5% | 68.9% | 7.5% | 19.8% |
| 10 | 16.8% | 54.7% | 8.8% | 31.2% |
| 20 | 11.5% | 37.2% | 11.6% | 42.5% |
| 50 | 8.1% | 19.9% | 19.3% | 55.0% |
| 100 | 6.4% | 12.1% | 29.8% | 64.8% |

(Data from paper Table 2.)

Several key readings. First, both representations have P@1 of 100%: the top-ranked result always hits the target word, where the top result of the contextual representation is always the source recording of the query (Figure 2b and Results section text), and MFCC achieves this through physical self-matching with the source recording. Second, as k increases, both precisions decrease, but XLSR-53's decay rate is much slower than MFCC's: at k=10, XLSR-53 precision is 54.7% vs. MFCC's 16.8%; at k=100, it is 12.1% vs. 6.4%—this is where pre-trained representations truly leave handcrafted features behind: not only can they find the source recording, but they can also cluster the same word from other speakers and contexts into the neighborhood. Third, recall rates are generally low: even at k=100, XLSR-53 recall is only 64.8% (there are only 10 relevant sentences per word, the expected recall for random retrieval at k=100 is 10%, and XLSR-53 is 6.5 times that), indicating that variants of the same word are not all tightly clustered, and there is still significant variability within the representation. Fourth, readings given in the paper text but outside the table: under the neighborhood scale of k=10, more than half of the layers have precision greater than 45% (Results section), indicating that high clustering in the neighborhood is common across layers. The supplementary material shows the "distance distribution of two pronunciations of the same word" under the three representations: as layers deepen, the distance and variance between two occurrences of the same word shrink synchronously, and the comparison of P@1 indicates that this shrinkage captures mainly linguistic information rather than acoustic details—collectively supporting the paper's core judgment: XLSR-53 constructs a more structured space, representations of the same word are closer, and representations are more abstract and less dependent on physical factors such as speaker timbre, microphone, and recording environment.

**Author's Verification Note**: According to the paper's definition (each query has exactly 10 relevant sentences), macro-averaged Precision@k and Recall@k should approximately satisfy $R@k \approx k \times P@k / 10$ (e.g., when k=1, P=100%, R should be about 10%). However, the numbers in Table 2 imply approximately 17 to 18 relevant sentences (e.g., at k=100, $100 \times 12.1\% / 64.8\% \approx 18.7$; at k=5, $5 \times 68.9\% / 19.8\% \approx 17.4$, highly consistent across k), which contradicts the text's setting of "10 sentences per word." The paper does not explain this discrepancy in口径 (scope/definition), so readers should be cautious when citing specific values.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Although the paper has no formally named ablation table section, substantive ablation runs throughout the text, which can be summarized into four groups. **Layer Ablation** (Figure 1, Figure 2, Table 1): Anisotropy intensity increases monotonically with layer depth—layer-by-layer median cosine similarity rises from 0.47 at Layer 0, through a low range of 0.30 to 0.47 in middle layers, to 0.59 at Layer 20, 0.78 at Layer 21, and 0.96 at Layers 22-24 (layer-specific values in Figure 1); negative similarities are almost non-existent. However, retrieval performance is not synchronized with anisotropy intensity: performance fluctuates significantly across layers, with a particularly obvious drop at Layers 15, 16, and 17, for which the authors explicitly state they found no explanation; even in the last few layers where anisotropy is strongest and cosine similarity variance is almost zero, retrieval remains effective. The authors thus emphasize that practical applications must carefully select layers, citing Guillotine regularization work (citation [15]) as corroborating evidence. **Representation Ablation** (Table 2, as above): Pre-trained representations show advantages over handcrafted features, which widen as k expands. **Context Ablation** (Figure 2a vs. 2b): P@1 for word-level (no context) representations is not always 1, whereas the nearest neighbor for contextual representations is always the source recording; combined with the fact that the corpus side always carries context, the paper attributes the gap to the context mismatch between the query side and the corpus side, proving that contextual information plays a key role in XLSR-53 representations. **Dimension Ablation** (Table 1): Rogue dimensions exist in every layer except Layer 0 (convolutional feature output, where max and second-max means are both 18)—the mean of the largest dimension is several orders of magnitude higher than the second-largest; the median of means for all dimensions in Layers 1-19 is only about 10 to 16, while the mean of the largest dimension swells to thousands in middle layers (e.g., approx. 7988 at Layer 9, approx. 9116 at Layer 12), and goes completely out of control at Layers 22 and 23: the means of the two largest dimensions are approx. 27151 vs. 129132920, and 28145 vs. 126904352, with the largest being about three and a half orders of magnitude higher than the second-largest, and the median of means also soaring to 7948 and 8154. **Author's Note on Two Data Doubts**: First, the last column of Table 1 reports -458.7 and -473.1 for Layers 22 and 23, but standard deviation cannot be negative according to the column name (standard deviation of values within the largest dimension), suggesting a typesetting or numerical sign error in the original text; second, Table 1 shows almost no rogue dimensions at Layer 24 (max mean is only 2), but the median cosine similarity at this layer in Figure 1 is as high as 0.96; the paper does not discuss this tension.

## Main Contributions

- **First Bridge Between Anisotropy Research and Downstream Tasks**: To the authors' knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the actual impact of anisotropy on downstream tasks, advancing the debate from "whether metric distribution is pathological" to "whether the task is truly harmed," and providing an affirmative answer—not harmed, at least in the word retrieval task.
- **A Clean and Replicable Argument Structure**: Using the syllogism "retrieval accuracy ⇒ similarity relative ranking still carries information ⇒ anisotropy harm is limited," it transforms geometric intuition into an experimentally falsifiable proposition; this template can be directly transferred to the evaluation of other representation geometry problems.
- **Empirical Characterization of wav2vec2 Representation Robustness**: Although XLSR-53 is trained with a reconstruction-based self-supervised objective (predicting masked or corrupted inputs, seemingly requiring retention of all signal details), it actually constructs abstract representations that capture high-level speech structures and generalize across speakers—the representation distance for the same word remains small even when pronounced by different speakers.
- **Empirical Warning on Pre-training Value and Layer Selection**: Under the same protocol, the advantage of pre-trained representations over handcrafted features widens as the neighborhood expands (Table 2), proving that pre-training learns linguistic structures rather than physical acoustic details; meanwhile, layer-by-layer results show that layer selection is crucial for practical applications, and one cannot casually use the last layer.
- **A Usable Technical Route for Documentary Linguistics**: A query-by-example word retrieval process (MFA word segmentation + XLSR representations + subsequence DTW) that is transcription-free, annotation-free, training-free, and fine-tuning-free, directly serving the recording work of low-resource and endangered languages, with P@1 reaching 100% and precision at k=10 of 54.7% (Table 2).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Only One Model Family Tested**: All experiments are based on a single wav2vec2-family model, XLSR-53; HuBERT, mentioned in parallel in the abstract, has no experiments, nor do other self-supervised speech models like WavLM. It is questionable whether the conclusions can be generalized to other pre-training objectives (masked prediction, contrastive learning, distillation).
- **No Control Group for Anisotropy Correction**: The paper proves that "diseased cosine is still usable," but does not test whether "it is better after correction"—running the same retrieval protocol after whitening, spectral transformation, or removing rogue dimensions is required to fully answer the marginal harm of anisotropy. The existing positive conclusions cannot rule out the possibility that "performance could still improve after correction."
- **Performance Ceiling of Zero-Training Route is Obvious**: Recall at k=100 is only 64.8% (Table 2); variants of the same word are not all clustered; there are no learnable components (even layer selection for query representations relies on F1 oracle), leaving space for lightweight adaptation (such as unsupervised layer weighting, metric learning) unexplored.
- **Computational Overhead Not Reported**: Each query requires one n × m DTW for each of the 1000 sentences, with a frame rate of about 49 frames per second, making the matrix scale for long sentences considerable; the paper does not report retrieval time, nor does it discuss the feasibility of approximate acceleration (such as lower-bound pruning, frame-level approximate nearest neighbor pre-filtering), raising doubts about scalability to real field corpora (often tens of hours).
- **Dependence on Pre-component Quality**: Query construction depends on MFA alignment accuracy and the assumption that "the corpus is already segmented by phrases" (footnote 1); the impact of alignment errors on retrieval is not quantified; CommonVoice consists of read-aloud, clean-channel speech, which differs greatly from the spontaneous speech, far-field, and noisy recordings actually faced in documentary linguistics.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Small Scale, Single Language, Single Domain**: 30 words, 300 queries, 1000-sentence retrieval pool, experimented only on one language, Spanish, and all from read-aloud corpora; the language-independence and domain robustness of the conclusions are unverified, which are precisely the properties most needed in documentary linguistics scenarios.
- **No External Baselines**: No comparison with any ASR transcription retrieval, dedicated QbE systems, or unsupervised acoustic word segmentation methods, making it impossible to locate the absolute position of this route in the method landscape; the advantages or disadvantages of "pre-trained representations + DTW" relative to these alternatives can only be inferred.
- **Lack of Statistical Rigor**: Variance, confidence intervals, or significance tests are not reported; the abnormal drop at Layers 15-17 in layer-by-layer results is unexplained, and small-sample fluctuations cannot be ruled out.
- **Several Data Consistency Issues Unclear (Author's Verification)**: The notation of Formula 1 is opposite to the interpretation direction in the text (see Author's Note in Methodology section); the precision and recall in Table 2 imply about 17 to 18 relevant sentences, inconsistent with the text's setting of 10 sentences per word (see Author's Note in Experimental Results section); Table 1 shows negative standard deviations, and the tension between the disappearance of rogue dimensions at Layer 24 and its high anisotropy is not discussed; citation numbers for CommonVoice are inconsistent (citation [10] in Section 2, [13] in Section 3); even the title of Section 3 has a spelling error (original: "Is Anistropy Harmfull?"). These flaws do not affect the direction of the main conclusions, but specific numbers should be verified against the original text when cited.
- **Coarse Trade-off in Evaluation Scope**: The authors themselves admit (footnote 2) abandoning the finer-grained evaluation of "whether the word is accurately located within the recording" because it is hard to interpret; but for documentary linguistics, location accuracy directly determines manual verification costs, and ignoring half of the practicality by only looking at sentence-level retrieval is a significant loss.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Expand Model and Task Matrix**: Repeat the same protocol on other self-supervised models like HuBERT and WavLM to test the architecture-independence of the conclusions; further extend to other tasks heavily dependent on similarity, such as clustering, speaker diarization, and acoustic event retrieval, upgrading "anisotropy is harmless" to a cross-task regularity or boundary condition.
- **Add Correction Control Groups**: Run retrieval again after whitening representations, removing rogue dimensions, or spectral normalization, quantifying the "task-level benefit of geometric correction," giving anisotropy mitigation research its first task-level evaluation benchmark.
- **Move to Real Field Data**: Verify on real endangered language recordings featuring spontaneous speech, multiple speakers, and far-field noise, while introducing comparisons with ASR transcription routes and dedicated QbE systems; this is the final exam for the documentary linguistics scenario the paper serves.
- **Unsupervised Layer Selection and Lightweight Adaptation**: Break free from oracle layer selection based on F1, explore unsupervised layer selection criteria (such as compactness based on same-word distance distribution), and attempt annotation-free metric fine-tuning to address the variant dispersion problem exposed by the 64.8% recall at k=100.
- **Efficiency Engineering**: Systematically study DTW lower-bound pruning, frame-level approximate nearest neighbor pre-filtering, representation dimensionality reduction or quantization, pushing this route from a 1000-sentence prototype to field-level corpora; simultaneously quantify the waste of computation and storage caused by rogue dimensions (especially those out-of-control dimensions at Layers 22 and 23).
