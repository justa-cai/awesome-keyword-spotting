# Low-resource keyword spotting using contrastively trained transformer acoustic word embeddings

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Julian Herreilers, Christiaan Jacobs, Thomas Niesler (Department of Electrical and Electronic Engineering, Stellenbosch University, South Africa)
- **Date**: June 2025 (arXiv:2506.17690v1, submitted June 21, 2025, Interspeech-style short paper)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.17690
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, acoustic word embeddings (AWE), contrastive learning, NT-Xent loss, query-by-example (QbE), low-resource languages, self-supervised learning

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The scenario presented in this paper is both highly specific and practical: for years, the United Nations has funded keyword spotting systems designed for **humanitarian aid broadcast monitoring** (as mentioned in the paper's introduction, references [1][2]). These systems are deployed in developing regions lacking internet infrastructure, where local communities rely on community radio stations to convey demands, opinions, and information. Relief organizations need to automatically retrieve specific keywords (e.g., terms related to disasters, epidemics, or refugees) from massive amounts of broadcast audio to monitor public sentiment and aid needs.

This scenario imposes three stringent constraints simultaneously. First, **extreme scarcity of language resources**: the languages involved (such as Luganda in Uganda and Bambara in Mali) are typically severely under-resourced, with almost no transcription data, pronunciation dictionaries, or language models, making traditional approaches relying on Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) plus text search unfeasible. Second, **tight time constraints**: the urgency of humanitarian crises requires systems to be built in a very short time, leaving no opportunity to collect and annotate corpus from scratch for a new language. Third, **poor audio quality**: the search corpus for the evaluation languages comes from local radio broadcasts, containing significant transmission distortion and higher noise levels than English development corpora (as explicitly stated in Section 4.2 of the paper).

Under these constraints, **query-by-example (QbE)** keyword spotting becomes the almost only viable route: it requires only one (or a very small number of) spoken keyword template audio clips to search for similar words in a completely unannotated audio library. Template audio is easy to obtain in a new language—simply ask a local person to read a few times. Classic QbE uses Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) to align and match query templates with search audio, but DTW is computationally expensive. The author team's previous work explored two improvement routes: one was using DTW scores to train a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to accelerate retrieval (references [4][5][6]), and the other was introducing human feedback to correct some retrieval errors, known as "listen-and-confirm" (reference [7]).

**Acoustic Word Embeddings (AWE)** represent a newer route: mapping variable-length audio segments into fixed-length vectors, so that retrieval only requires comparing the distance between query vectors and segment vectors, completely eliminating the repeated alignment calculations of DTW. AWE can be implemented using various structures such as RNNs or Transformers; in low-resource scenarios, an effective path is to train a multilingual AWE model on resource-rich languages and then transfer it directly to the target low-resource language without any adjustments (references [14][16][17]). Recently, AWE schemes that directly pool features from large-scale self-supervised pre-trained models (wav2vec2.0, HuBERT family) have emerged (references [18][19]), which is particularly attractive in environments where even fine-tuning data for the target language is insufficient.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing solutions into four types and points out their shortcomings one by one (these shortcomings are also the hypotheses that the experimental section aims to quantify and verify):

- **Computational cost and accuracy bottleneck of DTW baselines**: DTW requires repeated elastic alignment for each query-segment pair, which is computationally expensive; even the enhanced DTW baseline using Bottleneck Features (BNF) (reference [7]) achieves only the following MAP on the development set: English 23.37%, Luganda 22.44%, Bambara 40.90% (Table 3), indicating a low accuracy ceiling.
- **Direct pooling of self-supervised features lacks task shaping**: meanpooling (averaging all feature frames within a segment) and subsampling (concatenating K equidistantly sampled feature vectors from the sequence) require no training, but they are merely geometric aggregations of pre-trained features, **without any optimization for the retrieval geometry of "similar words close, dissimilar words far"**. Moreover, layer selection is very sensitive (Fig. 2 shows huge performance differences across layers); subsampling concatenates 10 vectors, resulting in an AWE dimension as high as 7680 (Section 5.3), which is not only high-dimensional but also performs worse than meanpooling (Table 3: Luganda MAP 32.45% vs. 43.87%).
- **Reconstruction-based AWE (CAE-RNN) uses proxy tasks**: The contrastive autoencoder RNN lets the decoder reconstruct another instance of the same word type; the training signal acts on the reconstruction error in the **input feature space**, and the geometry of the embedding space benefits only indirectly. The paper's experiments show it is significantly weaker than contrastive models on both evaluation languages (Table 3: Luganda MAP 47.19% vs. 65.74%/69.91%).
- **Contrastive RNN (ContrastiveRNN) is limited by encoder structure**: Reference [14] has already applied the NT-Xent contrastive loss to RNN encoders, but RNNs process sequences sequentially and are difficult to parallelize. Furthermore, the authors' previous contrastive learning schemes have not been combined with Transformer encoders and large-scale self-supervised input features in this new pipeline.
- **Lack of systematic comparison**: The authors explicitly state in their contributions that this is the **first work to compare multiple embedding technologies on the "broadcast keyword spotting" task**—previously, each method was validated on different tasks (word discrimination, hate speech detection), leaving engineers unable to judge which route is most effective under real broadcast noise.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the core question the paper aims to answer is: **Under the conditions of zero transcription and zero annotation for the target language (the only annotated data being a small number of isolated keyword templates, Table 1), and requiring rapid deployment, can we design an AWE model that, trained only on annotated word pairs from resource-rich languages and frozen for transfer to unseen languages, stably outperforms three categories of existing solutions (DTW, SSL feature pooling, and reconstruction-based AWE) on broadcast search corpora with transmission distortion?** This is specifically broken down into three sub-challenges: (1) The geometry of the embedding space must be directly optimized to be "retrieval-friendly," rather than formed indirectly via reconstruction or pooling; (2) It must be transferable to completely unrelated languages (Bambara belongs to the Mande family, with no related languages in the training set, Section 5.2); (3) The strategy for combining training languages must yield actionable conclusions, because in low-resource scenarios "there is no time to try configurations for each language" (as stated at the beginning of Section 6.3).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall pipeline consists of four stages. **Stage 1** is a frozen self-supervised frontend: features are taken from the 10th layer Transformer output of mHuBERT-147 (MHB, a compact multilingual HuBERT of BASE size, reference [28]) as input to the AWE model. **Stage 2** is the authors' proposed ContrastiveTransformer: an encoder-only 3-layer Transformer encoder with 16 attention heads per layer, followed by a linear layer outputting 256-dimensional AWE (Section 5.2). **Stage 3** is the retrieval mechanism: a sliding window of variable length between 10 and 65 frames scans the search speech with a step size of 5 (no word boundaries are available, so variable-length windows are used to cover different word lengths); each window segment is embedded independently. **Stage 4** is the decision: the cosine distance between the keyword template embedding and each window embedding is calculated as similarity, and a threshold is applied to determine if the keyword appears (Section 3).

The "why" behind each design decision:

- **Why use an encoder-only Transformer instead of the teacher-student contrastive Transformer in reference [15]**: The contrastive Transformer proposed in reference [15] requires a complex encoder-decoder and teacher-student structure; the authors found it could be significantly simplified—keeping only the encoder and applying the NT-Xent loss directly in the embedding space (Section 2.4). This retains the advantages of Transformer parallel training and global self-attention modeling of long-range dependencies within words, while converging the training objective to the retrieval geometry itself.
- **Why prepend a trainable all-1 vector to the sequence**: This is a technique borrowed from BERT's [CLS] token (references [15][22]). To convert variable-length sequences into fixed-length vectors, the conventional approach is pooling, but pooling dilutes discriminative information; using a dedicated aggregation token allows the attention mechanism to learn "how to aggregate the discriminative information of the entire word to this position," and finally, the **first vector** of the final layer output is taken as the AWE (Fig. 1). The motivation for this design is to make the aggregation process learnable rather than manually specified.
- **Why use MHB layer 10 for input features**: The authors conducted preliminary experiments (Section 5.2) and found that MHB layer 10 performs comparably to XLS-R layer 13 on unseen languages (consistent with reference [20]), but better on seen languages (Swahili); meanwhile, MHB is a smaller BASE configuration but has been trained on more languages than XLS-R. That is, this is an optimal trade-off of "smaller but broader."
- **Why normalize templates at the speaker level and search speech at the utterance level**: Section 5.3 explains that normalization prior to AWE extraction leads to "significant performance improvements" (consistent with the observation in reference [18]). Since there are few templates, statistics can be computed per speaker; broadcast audio is normalized per utterance, which better matches its channel drift—the essence is using MVN to suppress speaker and transmission channel differences, making the cosine distance focus more on the word content itself.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

Training uses the NT-Xent (normalised temperature-scaled cross entropy) loss. A batch consists of N different positive sample pairs: $(X^{(a,i)}, X^{(p,i)})$, $i = 1, 2, \ldots, N$, where each pair contains an anchor sample $X^{(a,i)}$ and **another instance of the same word type** $X^{(p,i)}$, corresponding to embeddings $w^{(a,i)}$ and $w^{(p,i)}$. The loss form is (Equation 1 in the paper):

$$L_i = -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(w^{(a,i)}, w^{(p,i)})/\tau)}{\sum_{\forall w \in \mathcal{W}} \exp(\text{sim}(w^{(a,i)}, w)/\tau)}$$

where $\mathcal{W} = \{w^{(a,j)}\} \cup \{w^{(p,k)}\}$, $j \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$ and excluding $j = i$, $k \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$; $\text{sim}(\cdot)$ is cosine similarity, and $\tau$ is the temperature coefficient. The loss is summed over all pairs in the batch.

Translating this formula into plain language: **the numerator pulls "two pronunciations of the same word" closer in the embedding space, while the denominator pushes the anchor sample away from all other word embeddings in the same batch**, with temperature $\tau$ controlling the softness/hardness of this push-pull. The other $2N-2$ embeddings in the batch naturally serve as negative samples, eliminating the need for explicit negative sampling. This corresponds exactly to the schematic in Fig. 1: during training, "minimize the embedding distance between two instances of the same word type, and maximize the distance between embeddings of different word types."

During inference (Sections 3 and 5.3): the search utterance is cut into overlapping segments by a sliding window of 10–65 frames with a step size of 5, each passing through the same encoder to obtain AWE; the keyword template (a few isolated recordings, Table 1) also obtains an AWE; cosine distance is calculated pairwise to obtain a similarity curve; applying a threshold to the similarity allows for the decision of "whether the keyword appears." Since the threshold is scanned on the development set for each keyword, metrics like AP naturally do not depend on a single threshold choice.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Moving Contrastive Training from RNN to Transformer Encoder (The ContrastiveTransformer Itself)

Reference [14] has already proven that NT-Xent can train RNN embedders capable of zero-shot transfer, and reference [15] has proven that Transformers can do AWE (but using a teacher-student contrastive reconstruction framework). The combination in this paper is the first to **hang NT-Xent directly on an encoder-only Transformer** and use **large-scale multilingual self-supervised features as input**. The significance of this combination lies in three layers of叠加 (superposition): the self-supervised frontend provides acoustic representations robust to unseen languages (learned on tens of thousands of hours of multilingual audio); the Transformer encoder provides stronger sequence aggregation capacity and parallel training efficiency; NT-Xent provides a training objective **completely isomorphic** to the downstream retrieval objective (cosine distance ranking)—the metric optimized during training is the same as that used during inference, with no proxy misalignment like reconstruction error. The architecture of 3 layers, 16 heads, and 256-dimensional output was determined by searching on the development set (Section 5.2), deliberately kept small because the training data only has 400,000 word pairs, and larger models are prone to overfitting to the training languages.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Quick Decision Strategy for Training Language Combinations (Section 6.3)

The biggest practical obstacle in low-resource deployment is "when a new language arrives, it is impossible to try all training language combinations." The authors conducted systematic ablations with ContrastiveTransformer on the development set (Table 5), providing two actionable conclusions: **(1) If related languages exist, prioritize using related languages** (e.g., Luganda paired with Swahili, both belonging to the Bantu branch); **(2) If no related languages exist, prioritize selecting language combinations with large differences, rather than stacking multiple closely related languages** (Bambara paired with sw+afr, two languages with large differences, achieved MAP 75.02%, higher than the four-language mix of 74.53%, while adding closely related Southern Bantu languages xho+sot actually harmed performance). The intuition behind the second conclusion is: language diversity expands the transformation space that the embedding space needs to maintain the invariance of "synonyms are same cluster," forcing the model to learn cross-lingually stable acoustic correspondence relationships, rather than overfitting to the specific pronunciation patterns of a single language family.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Unified Evaluation Protocol for Five-Way AWE Schemes

The paper places five embedding schemes—CAE-RNN, meanpooling, subsampling, ContrastiveRNN, and ContrastiveTransformer—into the same QbE pipeline (same sliding window, same normalization, same metrics) and uses DTW+BNF as an external baseline. The value of the unified protocol is that it excludes interference from engineering details, making "embedding quality" the only variable—this is also the first contribution listed by the authors (first comparison of multiple embedding technologies on the broadcast KWS task).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

| Dimension | DTW+BNF | meanpooling/subsampling | CAE-RNN | ContrastiveRNN | ContrastiveTransformer (This Paper) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Training Signal | None (template matching) | None (frozen feature aggregation) | Input space reconstruction error | Embedding space NT-Xent | Embedding space NT-Xent |
| Requires Annotated Word Pairs | No | No | Yes (resource-rich languages) | Yes (resource-rich languages) | Yes (resource-rich languages) |
| Target Language Data | Templates only | Templates only | Templates only | Templates only | Templates only |
| Embedding Dimension | — | 768 (pooling) / 7680 (subsampling concat) | RNN hidden state | RNN hidden state | 256 |
| Retrieval Metric | DTW distance | Cosine | Cosine | Cosine | Cosine |

Key differences focus on two aspects: whether the training signal acts directly on the retrieval geometry of the embedding space (yes for contrastive, no for reconstruction and pooling), and whether the aggregator is learnable and has sufficient capacity (Transformer aggregation token vs. RNN final state and fixed pooling).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

**AWE Training Corpus** (Section 4.1): NCHLT corpus (reference [23]), containing 11 official spoken languages of South Africa, of which 9 belong to the Southern Bantu family and are distantly related to Luganda (Northeast Savanna Bantu); additionally, a Swahili subset of similar scale to a single NCHLT language was extracted from Common Voice (reference [24]) (Swahili is a well-resourced Central African Bantu language, related to Luganda). The selection reason comes from the conclusion in reference [17]: training AWE with languages related to the target language improves downstream performance. Word-level alignment for Swahili was newly created using Montreal Forced Aligner (reference [29]), while alignment for other languages already exists. Each AWE model was trained on the same 400,000 word pairs from Afrikaans (afr), Swahili (sw), Xhosa (xho), and Sesotho (sot), with $(X, X')$ and $(X', X)$ paired bidirectionally (Section 5.2). Additionally, Tsonga (tso) and Venda (ven) were used as development languages, using a same-different task (reference [30], testing whether the model can give similar embeddings for the same word spoken by different speakers) to tune the model without contaminating the evaluation languages.

**Keyword Spotting Corpus** (Section 4.2, Table 1 and Table 2): Three languages, with English as the development language (for hyperparameter selection), and Luganda and Bambara as evaluation languages. Isolated keyword template corpus (the **only annotated data** used by the KWS system, Table 1): English 24 speakers, 40 word types, 1920 recordings, 34.27 minutes; Luganda 16 speakers, 18 word types, 603 recordings, 13.83 minutes; Bambara 139 speakers, 30 word types, 8335 recordings, 2.05 hours. Broadcast search corpus (Table 2): English development set 4220 utterances / 7.80 hours, test set 5005 utterances / 10.28 hours; Luganda development set 6052 utterances / 5.57 hours, test set 1420 utterances / 1.99 hours; Bambara development set 11715 utterances / 7.45 hours, test set 12790 utterances / 7.77 hours. To ensure P@10 is meaningful, only keywords appearing in at least 10 utterances in both the development and test sets were retained, resulting in 36, 12, and 23 word types for English, Luganda, and Bambara respectively entering the evaluation (Section 5.3).

**Pre-trained Models** (Section 5.1): Four self-supervised models—English monolingual wav2vec2.0 LARGE (W2V2), multilingual XLS-R, newly released multilingual mHuBERT-147 (MHB, BASE configuration but trained on more languages than XLS-R), and English monolingual HuBERT BASE (HB, used to evaluate the impact of multilingual training). An important experimental control point: **Luganda is in the pre-training language list of MHB and XLS-R, while Bambara is not in any of them**—this makes Bambara a true "unseen language" test.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

For each keyword, the average precision (AP) is calculated by scanning thresholds over all similarity scores; simultaneously, the precision at top 10 (P@10, the proportion of the top 10 retrieved items containing the word) and P@N (precision at N, where N is the total number of utterances where the word truly appears, i.e., precision at the ideal recall upper limit) are reported; finally, the mean is taken over all keywords to obtain MAP, mean P@10, and mean P@N (Section 5.3). The rationale for selection: QbE is essentially a retrieval ranking problem on unannotated corpora; AP integrates over all thresholds and does not depend on a single operating point, reflecting the overall ranking quality of the embedding space; P@10 reflects the efficiency of "reviewing the top retrieved items" in actual use; P@N reflects the completeness of "finding all utterances that truly contain the keyword." The authors specifically use the improvement in P@N in result interpretation to illustrate that the Transformer model "more stably retrieves utterances that truly contain the keyword" (Section 6.2).

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

**Full Development Set Comparison (Table 3)**: The DTW+BNF baseline (reference [7]) achieves MAP of 23.37/22.44/40.90 for Eng/Lug/Bam; MHB layer 8 meanpooling is 84.34/43.87/54.85; MHB layer 8 subsampling is 79.77/32.45/44.43; CAE-RNN is 78.56/47.19/62.53; ContrastiveRNN is 86.41/65.74/70.96; ContrastiveTransformer is **89.16/69.91/74.53**. Four conclusions can be drawn: (1) All five AWE schemes outperform the DTW baseline; (2) The absolute MAP improvement of the two contrastive models over the DTW baseline is about 47 percentage points for Luganda and about 34 percentage points for Bambara (as stated in Section 6.2, 69.91−22.44≈47.5, 74.53−40.90≈33.6, consistent with Table 3); (3) All three multilingual AWE models exceed the direct pooling of self-supervised features on both evaluation languages; (4) Contrastive models dominate CAE-RNN, especially on Luganda (69.91 vs. 47.19).

**Test Set Comparison (Table 4)**: Only the two best-performing systems on the development set are reported. Luganda: DTW baseline 24.3/40.8/26.3 (MAP/P@10/P@N), ContrastiveRNN 60.6/75.0/60.8, ContrastiveTransformer **65.3/75.8/63.4**; Bambara: DTW baseline 42.4/59.1/42.3, ContrastiveRNN 68.9/81.3/67.6, ContrastiveTransformer **69.9/82.2/68.1**. ContrastiveTransformer exceeds ContrastiveRNN on all metrics on the test set, validating the generalization of the development set conclusions. A noteworthy detail: on the development set, Luganda's P@10 showed ContrastiveTransformer (87.50) slightly lower than ContrastiveRNN (89.17) (Table 3), but MAP and P@N were still higher, and on the test set it led comprehensively—indicating that the advantage comes mainly from overall ranking quality rather than individual top hits.

**English Control Findings (Section 6.2)**: Unlike the two low-resource languages, the lead of multilingual AWE models over the mHuBERT pooling scheme on English is much smaller (89.16 vs. 84.34). The authors' explanation is that the MHB pre-training set contains a large amount of English, so the pooled features themselves are already good enough. This indirectly confirms: **the benefits of contrastive training are greatest precisely in "languages unseen by pre-training,"** which is exactly the property needed most in low-resource scenarios.

**Layer-by-Layer Analysis (Fig. 2)**: Development set curves for meanpooling layer-by-layer on four pre-trained models show that middle layers encode information most useful for AWE (consistent with references [18][31]); the W2V2 monolingual model shows the largest improvement on English (as expected); for the two low-resource languages, MHB clearly exceeds the DTW baseline, with layer 8 being generally optimal; crucially, on the unseen language Bambara, **the smaller, less data-trained MHB actually exceeds XLS-R**—the authors thereby endorse "small and broad multilingual pre-training."

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

**Training Language Combination Ablation (Table 5, ContrastiveTransformer, 100k word pairs per language, development set MAP)**: Monolingual sw 68.09 (Lug) / 72.97 (Bam); monolingual afr 54.21 / 72.24; monolingual xho 62.15 / 69.89; bilingual sw+afr 67.59 / 75.02; bilingual sw+xho 64.90 / 73.25; four-language afr+sw+sot+xho 69.91 / 74.53. Three findings: (1) **The Swahili monolingual model is the best monolingual model on both Bambara and the related Luganda**—the related language dividend is clear; (2) The best multilingual configuration for Bambara is two languages with large differences sw+afr (75.02), while adding closely related Southern Bantu languages xho+sot to sw+afr actually drops it to 74.53, proving that "stacking closely related languages is harmful"; for Luganda, conversely, the four-language mix 69.91 is highest, and more related languages are better; (3) Afrikaans (afr, West Germanic) trained alone is very poor for Luganda (54.21) but not bad for Bambara (72.24), indicating that "language diversity is more important than language family affiliation when there are no related languages." Additionally, the stable advantage of meanpooling over subsampling (all three languages in Table 3) is also an ablation nature: fixed equidistant concatenation loses fine-grained temporal structure beyond duration normalization, and the 7680-dimensional concatenated vector suffers from dimension explosion.

## Main Contributions

1. **First systematic comparison of multiple AWE technologies on the broadcast keyword spotting task**: Including self-supervised feature pooling (meanpooling/subsampling), reconstruction-based CAE-RNN, contrastive RNN, and DTW baseline, providing a unified evaluation protocol and complete numbers (Table 3/4), offering direct basis for engineering selection in low-resource broadcast monitoring.
2. **Proving that contrastively trained AWE consistently outperforms previous reconstruction-based schemes**: Applying NT-Xent directly to embedding space optimization, achieving a 22.72 percentage point MAP improvement on the Luganda development set relative to CAE-RNN (69.91 vs. 47.19, Table 3).
3. **Proposing ContrastiveTransformer and achieving best results across three languages**: The minimal combination of encoder-only Transformer plus trainable aggregation vector plus NT-Xent, paired with frozen mHuBERT-147 layer 10 features, achieves the largest performance improvement across all metrics for all three languages (Eng/Lug/Bam) (Table 3, Table 4), and works in new languages with only a very small number of isolated keyword templates.
4. **Providing actionable conclusions for training language selection (Table 5)**: Use related languages if they exist; if not, use language combinations with large differences, avoiding stacking closely related languages—directly responding to the practical constraint of "cannot try configurations for each language" in rapid low-resource deployment.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Retrieval mechanism is still brute-force sliding window scanning**: Without word boundary detection, the 10–65 frame sliding window with a step size of 5 (Section 5.3) generates a large number of overlapping segments for each search utterance, embedding and comparing them one by one. The paper claims AWE is more efficient than DTW, but **does not report any runtime, throughput, or computational cost numbers (not reported in the paper)**; the "more efficient" claim remains at the level of citing previous conclusions; the inference acceleration of ContrastiveTransformer relative to RNN is also not quantified.
- **Risk of binding hyperparameters and development languages**: The architecture (3 layers, 16 heads, 256 dimensions) and input layer selection were determined on the development set, while the development language is English (Sections 4.2, 5.2). The acoustic differences (tones, syllable structure) between English and the two evaluation languages are large; configurations tuned on English may not be optimal for Bantu and Mande languages, and the paper does not perform robustness checks across development languages.
- **Training details such as temperature coefficient, batch size, and number of training epochs are not disclosed**: The values of $\tau$ and N in Equation (1) are not given (not reported in the paper), affecting reproducibility and analysis of loss behavior.
- **Relies on the premise that "resource-rich related languages are available"**: ContrastiveRNN/ContrastiveTransformer both require 400,000 pairs of word-aligned annotated word pairs (Section 5.2). For isolated languages like Bambara with no related languages, one must fall back on language diversity heuristics (Table 5), and the upper limit of benefit is unclear; the paper does not explore the possibility of using unsupervised Same-Different pseudo-labels to replace annotated word pairs.
- **SSL frontend is completely frozen**: MHB layer 10 features are fixed and not updated; the room for improvement in embedding quality is constrained by this layer's representation; reference [19] has explored continued pre-training and learnable pooling, which this paper does not follow up on for comparison.
- **Keyword-by-keyword calibration of threshold decision**: Actual deployment requires setting thresholds for new keywords in new languages; the paper avoids the operating point selection problem using AP, but does not report false alarm/missed detection performance when development set thresholds are transferred to the test set (not reported in the paper).

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Only two evaluation languages, and few keywords**: Only 12 keyword types for Luganda entered the evaluation (Section 5.3), with a small sample size, casting doubt on the statistical stability of MAP; the paper does not provide significance tests or repeated runs with multiple random seeds (not reported in the paper).
- **Pre-training data leakage is not fully discussed**: Luganda is in the pre-training language list of MHB and XLS-R (Section 5.1), meaning the SSL frontend has "seen" Luganda's unannotated speech. Although this is itself a legitimate zero-annotation usage, it places the "Luganda results" and "Bambara results" on different transfer difficulty levels; the difference in scores between the two languages (69.91 vs. 74.53, Table 3) cannot be directly attributed to the difficulty of the languages themselves.
- **Luganda test set is biased/small**: 1420 utterances / 1.99 hours (Table 2), and although it is also broadcast audio like the development set, the scales are unequal, limiting the generalization of test set conclusions.
- **No direct comparison with ASR routes**: The authors argue that "ASR is infeasible," but reference [20] previously compared AWE with fine-tuned ASR on similar tasks (ASR becomes competitive with just 1 hour of target language data); this paper does not provide a boundary analysis of "if there is a little annotation budget, which route is more cost-effective."
- **Noise impact is not quantified**: Section 4.2 mentions that evaluation language broadcasts contain transmission distortion, but performance is not analyzed in layers by signal-to-noise ratio, making it impossible to judge the degradation rate of each method under poor channels.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Introduce word segmentation or candidate generation**: Use lightweight acoustic change point detection to replace blind sliding windows, reducing the number of segments and improving the alignment of windows with true word boundaries, balancing efficiency and accuracy; this would also provide empirical support for the paper's efficiency claims.
- **Unsupervised or semi-supervised contrastive signals**: Use pseudo-word pairs generated by spoken term discovery to replace annotated word pairs for training NT-Xent, extending the method to isolated languages "even without resource-rich related languages"; the same-different development protocol (tso/ven) already has ready-made evaluation infrastructure.
- **Lightweight adaptation for target languages**: Even with only 18–40 keyword templates (Table 1), one could explore template-driven test-time adaptation or few-shot fine-tuning (e.g., temperature scaling, embedding space re-centering) to further close the gap with fully supervised methods without sacrificing the "no transcription needed" selling point.
- **Automatic layer selection and learnable pooling**: The layer-by-layer analysis (Fig. 2) currently relies on manual layer selection (meanpooling uses layer 8, training model input uses layer 10); this could be changed to differentiable layer weighting or learnable pooling (direction of reference [19]), eliminating manual steps.
- **Integration with human feedback loop**: The authors' previous listen-and-confirm framework (reference [7]) is naturally complementary to ContrastiveTransformer—the contrastive embedding is responsible for high-confidence initial screening, and a small amount of annotation from human confirmation can be used to fine-tune the embedder back, forming an active learning loop in low-resource settings.
- **Expand language coverage and statistical testing**: Re-verify the language combination conclusions of Table 5 on more language families (especially more Mande and isolated languages), and supplement with significance tests, upgrading the observation that "diversity is better than closely related stacking" from a two-language observation to a transferable universal law.
