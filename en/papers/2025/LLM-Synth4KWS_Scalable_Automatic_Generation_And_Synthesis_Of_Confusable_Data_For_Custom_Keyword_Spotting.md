# LLM-Synth4KWS: Scalable Automatic Generation and Synthesis of Confusable Data for Custom Keyword Spotting

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Pai Zhu, Quan Wang, Dhruuv Agarwal, Kurt Partridge (Google DeepMind, New York, USA)
- **Date**: May 2025 (arXiv:2505.22995v1, submitted May 29, 2025)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22995
- **Keywords**: custom keyword spotting, confusable keywords, LLM data generation, TTS synthesis data augmentation, GE2E loss, c-AUC (vowel group evaluation metric), vowel grouping

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The proliferation of voice-controlled devices (smartphones, wearables, home smart devices) requires Keyword Spotting (KWS) models to satisfy both low power consumption and low memory footprint under always-on listening conditions. Traditional KWS solutions only detect factory-defined keywords (e.g., "Alexa", "Hey Google"), but the rising demand for personalization in smart devices has spawned Custom Keyword Spotting (custom KWS): users select trigger words from an open vocabulary based on personal preference to activate devices or software. The technical foundation of this paradigm is representation learning—speech utterances are mapped to fixed-length vectors (embeddings), and the device compares the embedding of the user's enrollment speech with that of the streaming input audio; if the similarity exceeds a preset threshold, a hit is判定 (judged). Current state-of-the-art custom KWS models are generally trained using contrastive learning (triplet loss or GE2E loss), constructing contrastive batches by randomly sampling keywords from the training vocabulary during training.

The core pain point of such systems lies in confusable keywords: the model has no trouble distinguishing word pairs with large phonetic differences (the paper's example: "blue" vs. "goat"), but the error rate rises sharply when facing word pairs with highly similar pronunciations ("blue" vs. "glue"), manifesting as a high False Accept rate in production systems. The paper further points out (Section 2.2) that the false accept problem is particularly severe when enrollment utterances contain only few-syllable words—few-syllable words inherently have fewer acoustic cues for distinction, so any phonetically similar word can deceive the similarity comparison. Key empirical observations from the author team's online demo tests and user feedback indicate that custom KWS models struggle particularly with words that share vowels. This observation serves as the starting point for the method and metric design throughout the paper.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper systematically reviews the defects of four categories of existing solutions, each pointing to the common bottleneck of "difficulty in scaling the acquisition of confusable keyword training data":

- **Contrastive training with random sampling naturally misses confusable words**. Training batches randomly sample X words from the entire training vocabulary V (noted in the paper; MSWC has 38k phrases). Under the condition that ||V|| is much larger than X, the probability of two mutually confusable words falling into the same batch is extremely low (Section 2.2). That is, the contrastive loss almost never exerts pressure on "word pairs that need to be distinguished the most," and the model's ability to discriminate confusable words relies entirely on generalization luck.
- **Training-time retrieval based on edit distance (Levenshtein distance) is too slow**. Existing work (References [10][11]) searches for confusable samples within the training set using edit distance within each training batch to construct contrastive sentence pairs. Although this improves confusable sentence detection, the O(N) search complexity per batch significantly slows down the training process (Abstract and Introduction).
- **Lookup-table-based Chinese solutions have high memory overhead and are not scalable**. Chinese custom KWS has similar approaches (Reference [12]), which accelerate training by maintaining a lookup table of confusable samples, but the runtime memory overhead makes this method resource-intensive and difficult to scale.
- **Existing TTS confusable keyword solutions suffer from severe lack of word diversity**. References [20-22] explored using TTS to improve confusable keyword detection, but these methods focused on multi-speaker speech synthesis—augmenting utterances sampled from 7,000 speaker embeddings, while the confusable words were only 12 manually created Chinese words. The lack of word diversity limits the model's generalization ability on the confusable KWS task (Introduction). In other words, these methods spent their diversity budget on "who speaks," while the dimension of "what is said" remained a very small set enumerated manually.
- Furthermore, crowdsourcing confusable data requires months of cycles and high costs (Introduction: crowdsourcing projects take months), making it impossible to customize on demand.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The paper aims to solve three coupled challenges simultaneously: First, how to automatically generate a large-scale, structurally grouped vocabulary based on confusability in a zero-manual-cost, scalable manner, and ground this vocabulary into real, usable training speech; Second, how to integrate confusable data into the contrastive training process without introducing additional overhead during training—neither performing online similar-word retrieval per batch nor maintaining expensive lookup tables; Third, how to define an evaluation metric that truly reflects user experience—existing EER/AUC metrics average the registration comparison across 35 keywords, diluting poor performance on confusable words with a large number of non-confusable comparisons, thus failing to expose the model's true weaknesses.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire framework (Figure 1) consists of two pipelines. The first is the data generation and training augmentation pipeline (Figure 1a): LLM generates confusable word groups by vowel (e.g., vowel group 1 /u:/ includes blue, glue, clue, luke) → style sampling (locale set to en-US, prosody taking variants like "luke?" with punctuation, speaker taking #61, etc.) → Virtuoso TTS engine synthesizes speech → synthetic data (TTS data) and real data (MSWC) are mixed according to weights into GE2E contrastive training, and the trained Conformer KWS model outputs 64-dimensional embeddings. The second is the vowel group evaluation pipeline (Figure 1b): registration utterances (e.g., 10 utterances) calculate embedding centroids, which are compared with test embeddings using cosine similarity; DET curves are drawn for each vowel group, and the area under the curve (AUC) is calculated, finally averaging the AUC of all vowel groups to obtain the new metric c-AUC.

Each design decision corresponds to a clear "why":

- **Why group by vowel rather than edit distance or other similarities**: Because user research and online demo feedback directly indicate that the model struggles most with words sharing vowels, and vowels are the main source of English confusability (Section 2.2). Grouping by vowel as an anchor precisely hits the real pain point.
- **Why use LLM to generate the vocabulary**: LLMs possess speech and lexical knowledge, can batch-generate words containing specified vowels according to prompts, and naturally support multiple languages at near-zero cost (Introduction: hours and with a very low cost).
- **Why use TTS synthesis rather than crowdsourced recording**: Modern TTS (the paper mentions MAESTRO [14] and Virtuoso [15]) can synthesize speech with high fidelity from a wide distribution of speaker characteristics, with speaker, accent, and prosody all controllable and samplable, turning "diversity" into a programmable knob.
- **Why mix two data sources at the batch level rather than the utterance level**: The paper's approach is that each training batch comes entirely from either the MSWC or the LLM/TTS dataset (Section 2.4), and all utterances within the LLM/TTS batch come from the same vowel group—this ensures the purity of intra-group comparison. If mixed at the utterance level, the timbre differences between real and synthetic speech themselves would become a shortcut, interfering with the model's learning of "word vs. word" rather than "sound vs. sound" distinction.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Base Model and Features**. The baseline is a 420KB quantized Conformer model optimized for always-on devices (Section 2.1, citing Reference [23]). Each utterance extracts 40-dimensional log-Mel filterbank energies, with a frame length of 25ms (Section 3.1). Training uses the TensorFlow/Lingvo framework, with weights and activations compressed to 8-bit integers via dynamic range quantization. Conformer was chosen over lighter CNN/RNN models because of its strong mixed modeling capability of convolution and self-attention in streaming speech tasks, and the 420KB quantized version already meets edge constraints; GE2E was chosen over triplet loss because the paper states it outperforms traditional triplet methods in both performance and computational cost, and has been adapted for the KWS task (GE2E-KWS, Reference [9]).

**GE2E Batch Construction and Loss**. Section 2.1 describes the mechanism in text: construct a batch containing X phrases, with Y utterances per phrase; for each phrase, use the embeddings of half (Y/2 utterances) to build an enrollment centroid, and then compute cosine similarity with the embeddings of the other half (Y/2 test utterances). Its mathematical form originates from the GE2E work in speaker verification (Reference [24]) and was adapted for KWS in Reference [9]: let the enrollment centroid of the k-th phrase be $c_k = \frac{2}{Y}\sum_{j=1}^{Y/2} e_{k,j}$, and the similarity between test embeddings and centroids forms an $X \times X$ similarity matrix $S_{i,k} = \cos(e_i, c_k)$. The loss forces each test embedding to be most similar to its own phrase's centroid (a softmax cross-entropy structure with positive diagonal and negative off-diagonal elements). The paper emphasizes that this "enrollment centroid + diverse test utterances within batch" construction significantly reduces loss variance, leading to more stable convergence and better accuracy—this is precisely why the subsequent group-aware training can directly reuse this loss.

**DET Curve and Threshold-Independent Metrics**. Compare the cosine similarity scores between registration and test embeddings with a threshold, scanning the threshold from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.01 (Section 3.4.1), to plot the DET curve of False Accept Rate (FAR) and False Reject Rate (FRR). EER is the value where FAR equals FRR, and AUC is the area under the DET curve; both should be as small as possible.

**Definition of c-AUC**. Group the keywords of Speech Commands by vowel (Table 1, total 9 groups). For each group g, calculate the AUC where "registration is compared only with test utterances from the same group," denoted as intra-group AUC; c-AUC is defined as the arithmetic mean of all group AUCs: $\text{c-AUC} = \frac{1}{9}\sum_{g=1}^{9} \text{AUC}_g$. The key difference from the full-set AUC lies in the construction of negative samples: the negative samples for the full-set AUC are the remaining 34 words (the vast majority of which have large phonetic differences), while the negative samples for the intra-group AUC come only from the 2-4 neighbor words in the same vowel group, resulting in a completely different difficulty structure.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: LLM-Driven Vowel Group Confusable Word Generation

English has 20 vowels (Figure 2, citing Reference [25]'s vowel chart). The paper sends a prompt independently to Gemini 1.5 Pro (called via Google AI Studio, Reference [26]) for each vowel, with the original text as follows (Section 2.2):

> Can you generate a group of words? The group should have 100 simple but distinguishable words. All words in the group should contain the vowel [u:]

Repeating this prompt for all 20 vowels yields 20 groups of structured confusable word lists, each with 100 words (partial results in Figure 3; the /u:/ group includes blue, glue, clue, luke, etc.). Two constraint words are worth noting: "simple" (simple) combined with the prompt's requirement to "avoid generating complex or long words" is to make the acoustic model focus on subtle differences between similar pronunciations—if words are too long, other phonemes within the word provide distinguishability shortcuts, and the model doesn't need to learn subtle differences in vowels or neighboring consonants; "distinguishable" (distinguishable) avoids the LLM generating truly homophonic words, preventing the injection of impossible tasks into training. The deep significance of this design is that the construction of the confusable word list changes from "manual enumeration of 12 words" (References [20-22]) to an automated process of "one prompt × 20 vowels," increasing the vocabulary scale and diversity by more than two orders of magnitude at near-zero marginal cost.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: TTS Style Sampling Synthesis

The Virtuoso TTS engine (Reference [15]) is used to ground the word list into speech. The paper lists its four characteristics: naturalness, intelligibility, prosody control, and language coverage (139 locales). For each keyword generated by the LLM, 100 utterances are generated using Virtuoso, sampled from 726 speakers with diverse demographic backgrounds and accents, covering 5 types of prosody (Section 2.3). Prosody control is achieved through punctuation marks, following the settings in Reference [27]—the example in Figure 1a is prosody "luke?": a question mark turns a declarative sentence into an interrogative rising intonation contour, giving the same word diversity in the intonation dimension. Based on this calculation, the total synthetic data volume is 20 vowels × 100 words per group × 100 utterances per word = 200,000 utterances. Figure 1a also illustrates the data mixing weights (TTS data weight 0.3, real data weight 0.7), while the experimental configuration in Section 3.2 takes Pmswc = 0.5 and Ptts = 0.5 equally, indicating that the mixing ratio is a tunable knob, and the experiment selected an equal setting.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Group-Aware GE2E Training Scheme

With the synthetic data structured by vowel groups, the modification on the training side is very light (Sections 2.4 and 3.2): the baseline batch randomly samples X = 8 keywords and Y = 10 utterances per keyword from the entire MSWC vocabulary, totaling 80 utterances; the LLM/TTS batch narrows the sampling domain to within a single vowel group—randomly selecting 8 words and 10 utterances per word within that group. The proposed model's training batch is drawn entirely from MSWC with probability Pmswc = 0.5, and entirely from the LLM/TTS dataset with probability Ptts = 0.5. In this way, in half of the training batches, the negative samples of the GE2E loss are all confusable words from the same vowel group, and the contrastive pressure is directly applied to the hardest word pairs; and since the confusability relationships are "pre-compiled" into the grouping structure during data generation, there is no need for online Levenshtein retrieval during training, nor for maintaining a confusable lookup table, keeping training efficiency on par with the baseline. This is a direct embodiment of the engineering idea of "moving online search to offline data generation."

### Key Technical Innovation 4: c-AUC North Star Metric

The paper's user research found that custom KWS models are particularly difficult to distinguish words sharing vowels, and this pain point is not reflected in existing evaluation metrics (Introduction). The grouping rules for c-AUC (Section 3.4.2) have two conditions: very similar vowels (e.g., i: and I) are merged into the same group; each group must have at least 3 words, otherwise it is discarded. The final 9 groups cover 32 words (Table 1): /a:/ group includes marvin, on, stop; /o:/ group includes dog, four, off; /e/ group includes yes, seven, left, bed; /æ/ group includes cat, backward, happy; /i:/ and /I/ merged group includes sheila, tree, three, visual, six; /E:/ and /@/ merged group includes forward, bird, learn; /aI/ group includes right, nine, five; /aU/ group includes wow, down, house; /oU:/ and /u:/ merged group includes go, no, follow, zero, two. During evaluation, registration embeddings are compared only with test utterances from the same group; for example, the registered word "right" is compared only with "nine" and "five" from the /aI/ group. The significance of this metric is to isolate "the type of error that users are truly likely to trigger" from averaged metrics, becoming a north star connecting real user experience with model optimization.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

The differences with four categories of related work can be summarized as differences in "the source of confusable knowledge and the timing of injection": (1) Compared with edit-distance retrieval methods (References [10][11]), this paper moves the discovery of confusable pairs from O(N) online search per batch during training to a one-time offline data generation before training, incurring zero overhead in the training loop; (2) Compared with Chinese confusable garbage models (Reference [12]), using data files instead of runtime lookup tables eliminates the memory overhead that makes it unscalable; (3) Compared with TTS solutions for manually created confusable words (References [20-22], 12 manually created Chinese words + 7,000 speaker embeddings), this paper upgrades word diversity from manual enumeration to LLM-structured generation by vowel (20×100 words), and the grouping basis (vowel) comes directly from user research rather than intuition; (4) Compared with previous works in the same group, Synth4KWS (Reference [18]) uses synthetic speech to solve data scarcity in low-resource KWS, while this paper focuses on the specific failure mode of confusable words and provides a new metric; GraphemeAug (Reference [22]) systematically synthesizes hard negatives at the grapheme level, while this paper utilizes LLM's lexical and speech knowledge to organize confusable groups by vowel, the two being complementary. Additionally, within the baseline comparison framework, the difference lies in: traditional triplet samples only one positive pair and one negative pair per batch, while GE2E-style batch construction (8 words × 10 utterances) generates an 8×8 similarity matrix at once, with smaller loss variance, which is also the prerequisite for the low-cost implementation of the group-aware scheme.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

- **Training Data (Real Part)**: MSWC (Multilingual Spoken Words Corpus, Reference [28]), totaling 5.3 million utterances, 38k phrases, 27k speakers (Section 3.1). Features are 40-dimensional log-Mel filterbank energies, 25ms frame.
- **Training Data (Synthetic Part)**: LLM generates 20 vowel groups, 100 words per group; TTS synthesizes 100 utterances per word, sampled from 726 speakers and 5 prosodies (Sections 2.2, 2.3), totaling approximately 200,000 synthetic utterances.
- **Test Set 1**: Speech Commands (Reference [30]) test split, over 11,000 utterances, 35 different phrases; for each test phrase, 10 utterances are randomly selected as the registration set, and the rest are used for testing (Section 3.3).
- **Test Set 2**: A subset of LibriPhrase (Reference [31]), LibriPhrase-1s. The original dataset contains 39k positive pairs (same keyword), 39k easy negative pairs (different keywords, e.g., "apartment" vs. "water"), and 39k hard negative pairs (different keywords, e.g., "apartment" vs. "abatement"); the subset retains only utterances with durations between 0.9 and 1.1 seconds, consistent with the model's 1-second training configuration (Section 3.3)—this duration alignment itself is the paper's explicit handling of the importance of "consistency between training and inference sequence lengths."

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **EER and AUC (Speech Commands)**: Threshold-independent metrics, aggregated over 35 keywords (Section 3.4.1). The rationale for selection is that they do not bind to a single operating point and can characterize the DET curve as a whole; but the paper explicitly points out their limitation—registration utterances are mostly compared with non-confusable keywords, diluting performance in confusable scenarios.
- **c-AUC (Speech Commands)**: The average of intra-group AUCs calculated for each of the 9 vowel groups in Table 1 (Section 3.4.2). The rationale comes directly from user research: words sharing vowels are the main source of real false triggers, requiring a dedicated north star metric to measure confusable detection.
- **Easy-AUC and Hard-AUC (LibriPhrase-1s)**: Calculated from DET curves of "positive pairs + easy negative pairs" and "positive pairs + hard negative pairs" respectively (Section 3.4.3). Hard-AUC plays a similar role to c-AUC—both concentrate evaluation pressure on phonetically similar negative samples, with the two datasets corroborating each other.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Main results are in Table 2. The baseline model is trained only on MSWC, and the augmented model (AugModel) is trained jointly on MSWC and LLM/TTS synthetic data:

- **Speech Commands**: EER decreased from 2.49% to 2.38% (relative decrease of 4.4%), AUC decreased from 0.490% to 0.472% (relative decrease of 3.7%), and c-AUC decreased from 0.915% to 0.812% (relative decrease of 11.3%). The paper's explanation for the contrast between "marginal relative improvement in AUC" and "significant improvement in c-AUC" is: the majority of comparisons in the full-set AUC occur between non-confusable words, which are not on the direct optimization path of data augmentation; whereas c-AUC exactly measures detection within confusable groups, which is the target area of augmentation (Section 4.1). Another notable absolute magnitude: the baseline's c-AUC (0.915%) is almost twice the full-set AUC (0.490%), confirming that intra-group evaluation is indeed harder.
- **LibriPhrase-1s**: Easy-AUC decreased from 0.012% to 0.0% (complete elimination of errors, relative improvement of 100%), and Hard-AUC decreased from 14.4% to 12.6% (relative improvement of 12.5%). The direction and magnitude of improvement in Hard-AUC are consistent with c-AUC on Speech Commands, indicating that the improvement in confusable detection capability holds across datasets and is not overfitting to a single test set (Section 4.3).
- **DET Curve Details (Figure 4)**: The augmented model outperforms the baseline at most operating points, but performs slightly worse at the extremely low false accept end (FAR < 1%, matching very high thresholds). The paper's hypothesis: TTS data introduces a bias towards synthetic speech, and the shift in prior distribution causes the model to lack confidence in real utterances, leading to increased FRR when evaluating real Speech Commands utterances at high thresholds (Section 4.1). This is a classic side effect of synthetic data augmentation, which the paper frankly discloses rather than hides.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

The paper does not report strict ablation experiments—for example, scanning the Ptts mixing probability, generating word lists with LLM only without grouping, increasing word table diversity with TTS only without expanding the word list, sensitivity to number of speakers and prosody types, etc., control variable experiments did not appear, and the rationale for each design choice (e.g., 100 words per group, 100 utterances per word, batch X=8×Y=10) is not reported by the paper. The two analyses closest to ablation are:

- **Results Decomposed by Vowel Group (Table 3)**: Augmentation benefits are highly uneven across groups—the /o:/ group AUC decreased from 1.044% to 0.636% (relative improvement of 39.10%, the largest among all 9 groups), /aI/ group from 0.873% to 0.583% (33.23%), /a:/ group from 0.227% to 0.163% (28.15%), /aU/ group from 0.727% to 0.528% (27.35%), /E:/ and /@/ merged group from 1.136% to 1.007% (11.37%), while /e/ group only 2.89%, /æ/ group only 3.12%, /oU:/ and /u:/ merged group almost no change (0.19%), and /i:/ and /I/ merged group actually degraded (increased from 2.221% to 2.435%, relative -9.67%), with a nine-group average of 11.3%. The paper attributes the inter-group differences to the fact that Speech Commands has only 3-5 words per group after grouping, limiting keyword diversity (Section 4.2).
- **Attribution Analysis for Degradation in /i:/ and /I/ Group**: Surveys show that the degradation mainly comes from the two words "three" and "tree". They both exist in the augmented dataset, a potential reason being that TTS synthesized almost identical sounds for "three" and "tree", while training still forced the model to distinguish them—the model was forced to execute a task that was inherently unsolvable in the training data (Section 4.2).
- **Duration Robustness Analysis (Section 4.3)**: On the full LibriPhrase with a wider duration range (mean utterance duration 0.63 seconds, standard deviation 0.11 seconds), the baseline model's performance degraded significantly, and the augmented model did not bring visible metric improvements. The paper concludes that "consistency between training and inference sequence lengths is crucial," admitting that the 1-second training configuration limits duration generalization.

## Main Contributions

1. **New North Star Metric c-AUC**: For the first time, the evaluation of custom KWS is refined from "full vocabulary average" to "intra-vowel group average," directly measuring confusable keyword detection performance, bridging the gap between real user experience and traditional metrics (first item in the Introduction contribution list).
2. **Scalable, Zero-Manual Confusable Data Generation Framework**: Using LLM prompts to generate structured confusable word groups by 20 vowels (100 words per group), and using Virtuoso TTS to synthesize 100 utterances per word from a style space of 726 speakers, 5 prosodies, and 139 locales, with no manual enumeration or crowdsourcing throughout.
3. **Group-Aware Efficient Training Scheme**: Modifying GE2E batch construction so that all utterances within LLM/TTS batches come from the same vowel group, allowing the contrastive loss to act directly on confusable word pairs; no similar word search or confusable lookup table needed during training.
4. **Tangible Performance Gains**: Relative improvements of 3.7% in AUC, 11.3% in c-AUC, and 4.4% in EER on Speech Commands; 100% improvement in Easy-AUC (dropping to zero errors) and 12.5% improvement in Hard-AUC on LibriPhrase-1s (Table 2).
5. **Engineering Usability Claims**: Training efficiency (no retrieval, no lookup table), scalability (LLM and TTS multilingual capabilities can be extrapolated to 100+ languages, especially resource-constrained languages), economy and availability (crowdsourcing takes months, LLM+TTS takes hours and is very low cost) (Introduction advantages list and Conclusion).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Regression at the extremely low false accept end due to TTS bias**: Figure 4 shows that the augmented model is slightly worse than the baseline in the FAR < 1% region, where synthetic speech priors cause the model to lack confidence in real utterances (Section 4.1). For mass-produced voice assistants that aim to suppress the false trigger rate to extremely low levels, this operating point is precisely the critical interval, and the bias cannot be ignored.
- **Insufficient TTS synthesis fidelity for extremely similar words**: The -9.67% degradation in the /i:/ and /I/ group exposes the most fragile link in the chain—when two words differ by only one consonant (the /θ/ in "three" vs. the /r/ in "tree"), TTS may produce nearly homophonic samples, forcing training to distinguish pairs that are "indistinguishable in the training data." This suggests that the vowel-grouped word list contains consonant-level near-pairs, and the generation pipeline did not perform difficulty verification or filtering for such pairs.
- **Blind spots in the granularity of vowel grouping**: Grouping is organized only by vowels, and the dimension of consonant differences (th/r, s/sh, etc.) is not explicitly modeled; the fact that "three vs tree" is assigned to the /i:/ and /I/ groups precisely illustrates that vowel anchors cannot explain all confusable sources within the group.
- **Limited duration generalization**: The model is trained with a 1-second configuration, and augmentation benefits disappear on the full LibriPhrase (mean duration 0.63 seconds, standard deviation 0.11 seconds) (Section 4.3), indicating that benefits partly depend on duration consistency between training and testing.
- **The degree of alignment between the speaker distribution of synthetic data and real distribution is not reported by the paper**: How much the coverage of 726 TTS speakers differs from real user acoustic conditions is not analyzed in the paper.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Lack of formal ablation**: Hyperparameters such as Ptts probability, number of words per group, number of utterances per word, number of speakers, number of prosodies, and coverage range of vowel groups have no sensitivity experiments, and the optimality of each design choice cannot be verified (not reported by the paper).
- **Multilingual claims not experimentally verified**: The scalability to 100+ languages is a method-level inference (based on the multilingual capabilities of LLM and TTS), but experiments were only executed on English, and the effectiveness in other languages is not reported by the paper.
- **Limited statistical power of group metrics**: Speech Commands has only 35 words, with 3-5 words per group (Table 1), so the estimation variance of group AUC is naturally large; the paper itself admits that limited keyword diversity leads to fluctuations in the magnitude of improvement between groups (Section 4.2).
- **Training efficiency advantage not quantified**: The comparison with retrieval-based methods (References [10-12]) remains at a qualitative level (qualitative descriptions of O(N) search and lookup table memory), without reporting positive numerical comparisons of training time and memory usage under the same experimental setup, nor competing in accuracy with these SOTA confusable training methods (not reported by the paper).
- **Single comparison with baseline**: All accuracy comparisons are only against their own MSWC baseline, lacking horizontal data from contemporaneous SOTA custom KWS methods (such as LETR, Rybakov streaming KWS, etc.) under the same setup (not reported by the paper).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Adversarial training to mitigate TTS overfitting**: The paper explicitly plans to adopt adversarial training methods (Reference [32], the group's "Adversarial training of keyword spotting to minimize TTS data overfitting") to align the representations of synthetic and real data, fixing the regression at the extremely low FAR end (Section 4.1).
- **More clearly distinguishable TTS**: Exploring TTS systems that can synthesize clearer, more distinguishable sounds between extremely similar words, solving near-homophone problems like "three"/"tree" from the data source (Section 4.2).
- **Multi-duration training**: Retraining the model with the LibriPhrase training set to improve generalization to different durations (Section 4.3).
- **Multilingual expansion**: Leveraging the multilingual capabilities of LLM and TTS to extend the entire vowel group generation pipeline to 100+ languages, especially resource-constrained languages with scarce real data (Conclusion).
- **Natural extensions at the method level** (not explicitly elaborated by the paper, but inferable from its limitations): Introducing phoneme-level difficulty verification and filtering at the word list generation stage to avoid putting TTS-indistinguishable pairs into training; expanding vowel grouping to a two-dimensional confusable structure of vowel plus consonant, complementary to GraphemeAug's grapheme-level hard negative synthesis; performing systematic ablation on Ptts and word list scale to determine the saturation point of benefits. These directions are all built upon the core paradigm of this paper "offline generation of confusable structure, online zero-overhead training," indicating that the paradigm itself has space for further excavation.
