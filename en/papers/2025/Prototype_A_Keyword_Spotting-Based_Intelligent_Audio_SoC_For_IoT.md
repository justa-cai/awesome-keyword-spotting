## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The rapid evolution of the Internet of Things (IoT) and edge computing has driven an explosive demand for intelligent voice systems (Section I). As the entry point for voice interaction in intelligent devices, Keyword Spotting (KWS) plays a "standby anytime, respond on call" role: it must continuously monitor microphone data streams in an always-on mode, yet provide a response the moment the user speaks a wake-word or command. The superposition of these two attributes dictates that the hardware implementation of KWS must simultaneously satisfy three stringent conditions. First, the power budget is extremely tight: always-on means the module consumes battery power year-round; for battery-powered IoT devices, continuous power consumption in the microwatt range is often the red line determining whether a product form factor is viable. Second, latency must be sufficiently low: the human ear tolerates a maximum lag of only a few hundred milliseconds from the end of the wake-word to a perceptible device response; the window allocated for single-frame algorithm processing is typically in the millisecond range, and any frame processing timeout will cause pipeline backlog. Third, chip cost must be low: IoT devices have low unit prices, and chip area directly determines wafer cost and yield; every square millimeter must be carefully calculated. Based on this, the paper points out that implementing KWS using FPGA or ASIC to achieve higher energy efficiency has become a clear trend in this field (Section I). It is worth noting that this is a demo paper, with a length of two pages; its core goal is to demonstrate a complete system prototype that can be demonstrated on-site, rather than to展开 (unfold) a complete academic argument for algorithms or circuits. This positioning determines the depth of its experiments (the phrase "not reported in the paper" will be encountered repeatedly in the experimental section below).

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing solutions into two types and points out their pain points one by one (Section I). The first type is DNN-based KWS accelerators, with representative works being two chips from top solid-state circuit journals (JSSC): one is AAD-KWS (28nm CMOS, sub-microwatt power consumption, embedding an acoustic activity detector into the MFCC path with an adjustable detection window, JSSC 2023, Reference [1]); the other is a 1.5µW fully integrated KWS SoC based on Skip-RNN and a fast, stable analog frontend (28nm, adaptive frame skipping, JSSC 2024, Reference [2]). The paper acknowledges that these schemes push recognition accuracy to higher levels, but explicitly points out the cost is "simultaneous increases in latency, power consumption, and chip area"—the main body of DNN inference is large matrix multiplication, which corresponds to large-scale multiply-accumulate arrays, weight storage, and data fetching paths in hardware; no matter how much they are compressed, their resource baseline is significantly higher than that of classic methods without deep models; the deeper the network and the longer the window, the harder it is to reduce pipeline fill time and frame latency. The second type is integrated intelligent SoCs, such as the work from DAC 2023 (Reference [3]), which uses a combination of CPU, DSP, and AI engines to handle both KWS and image classification. This route of "stacking multiple engines" has good versatility, but the paper points directly to its pain points: higher latency and higher chip cost (the paper explicitly states that cost is positively correlated with area and power consumption)—three sets of programmable computing units mean an area tax for three sets of instruction decoders, data paths, and storage systems, while the data flow of KWS itself is highly regular; running regular data flows with general-purpose engines is equivalent to paying electricity bills for flexibility for free. Furthermore, from the paper's own evaluation, a third implicit baseline can be deduced: running unoptimized algorithms directly on embedded general-purpose processors. Section III reports that when deployed on the Nuclei E203 RISC-V processor, the optimized algorithm reduced computation time by 98.2% and memory usage by 98.3%—this number inversely indicates that the time and memory overhead of the unoptimized software implementation on this level of processor is unacceptable, and the pure software route basically has no survival space in always-on scenarios.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Converging the metrics scattered in Sections II and III, this problem can be stated as: achieving four mutually constraining goals on a single 40nm chip—ultra-low latency (frame latency 2.98 ms, Section III), ultra-low power consumption (KWS accelerator 28.3 µW, Section III), ultra-low cost (accelerator 0.28 mm², SoC core 1.34 mm², on-chip storage only 128 kB, Section III), while maintaining usable recognition rates (over 300 one-second test commands, highest recognition accuracy 89%, Section III). The core contradiction lies in the fact that the hardware cost of higher-accuracy DNN methods rises with accuracy, while the accuracy ceiling of lightweight classic methods is relatively low. The paper's approach is a radical three-layer synergy: avoiding large-parameter DNNs in algorithm selection (choosing the MFCC+VQ+DTW template route), performing three types of compression in algorithm implementation (data downsampling, algorithm simplification, offline parameter optimization, Section II-A), and customizing modules one by one in hardware micro-architecture (7-stage FFT pipeline, sparse Mel filtering, DCT dataflow enhancement, fixed diagonal DTW, Section II-B). In other words, the key challenge of this paper is not a breakthrough in a single point technology, but a systems engineering project of "letting go a little at each layer, yielding an order of magnitude in total": using an 89% upper limit on recognition rate to exchange for hardware results with an area one order of magnitude smaller, frame latency ten times faster, and power consumption compressed to the microwatt level.

## Methodology

This section is organized by "SoC Architecture Modules": first giving the overall architecture and motivation of the three layers of algorithm-accelerator-SoC, then giving the mathematical skeleton of the algorithm pipeline (MFCC-VQ-DTW), and then expanding on the key technical innovations of three modules: the accelerator frontend FEU, the accelerator backend TCU, the analog frontend audio path, and power management. Finally, it summarizes the technical differences with existing methods. It needs to be explained: this is a digital SoC, the analog frontend AFE is an off-chip device driven by a custom Audio module inside the SoC (Section II-C); the paper does not report the specific model and parameters of the AFE; the paper also does not describe a dedicated power management unit; low-power means are mainly reflected in clock domain division and architecture-level offloading, which will be distinguished truthfully below.

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall design is a three-layer synergy of "Algorithm Layer—Accelerator Layer—SoC Layer" (Section II, Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

**Motivation for Algorithm Layer Selection**. The paper states clearly at the beginning: "Unlike methods bound to large-parameter DNN models, this paper adopts a lightweight method," combining MFCC (Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients) feature extraction, VQ (Vector Quantization) codebook, and DTW (Dynamic Time Warping) template matching (Section II-A). Why dare to return to this textbook-old route in 2025? Because its hardware friendliness is structural rather than tactical: the main body of MFCC calculation is FFT, filtering, logarithm, and DCT, which can be implemented with fixed-point addition, shifting, and a small number of regular multiplications, without large matrix multiplication; VQ quantizes per-frame feature vectors into codebook indices, after which template matching is performed at the low-dimensional index sequence level, with small storage volume; DTW only requires distance calculation, accumulation, and comparison, with no iterative inference and weight loading. The three types of operations are highly regular, with sufficient data reuse, and are naturally suitable for fixed-function circuits with area and power constraints. This decision at the selection level is equivalent to cutting off the three main cost items of DNN chips—"multiply-accumulate array scale, parameter storage capacity, and fetch bandwidth"—from the root.

**Organization of the Accelerator Layer**. A dedicated KWS accelerator is designed based on the optimized algorithm (Section II-B, Fig. 1), consisting of two main processing units: the Feature Extraction Unit (FEU) executes MFCC, and the Template Classification Unit (TCU) executes DTW. This "feature-classification" dichotomy corresponds one-to-one with the two stages of the algorithm pipeline. The benefit is that the two stages can be deeply customized for data flow: FEU is a per-frame streaming transformation (time domain to cepstral domain), while TCU is sequence alignment (test sequence to template library); their parallelism and memory access patterns are completely different, and they are designed separately without constraining each other. The architecture-level improvements listed by the paper include: a seven-stage pipeline to address the bottleneck of 128-point FFT calculation, Mel filter optimization based on sparsity, and dataflow enhancement for each stage of DCT; in the TCU, DTW is refined into fixed diagonal distance calculation, reducing area by 99.2% and power consumption by 84.2% (Section II-B).

**Integration at the SoC Layer**. The KWS accelerator is integrated into the SoC to support more complete applications (Section II-C, Fig. 2): the Nuclei E203 RISC-V core is responsible for flexible control, scheduling, and data preprocessing; the KWS accelerator acts as a coprocessor to handle high-parallelism speech processing; a custom Audio module is specifically used to drive the analog frontend (AFE) to complete speech input and output; all components collaborate efficiently through interrupt mechanisms. From Fig. 2, the bus and storage organization can also be read: the RISC-V core is connected to the ITCM/DTCM tightly coupled instruction/data memory and interrupt controller via the ICB bus, the KWS accelerator is hung on the AHB high-speed bus, and peripherals such as GPIO, UART, I2C, and AUDIO (I2S RX/TX) are hung on the APB peripheral bus. The motivation for this "simple single core + single coprocessor" division of labor is straightforward: control flow (peripheral configuration, task scheduling, data movement, result processing) is variable in form and suitable for programmable processors; data flow (per-frame feature extraction and template matching) is regular and has high parallelism, suitable for fixed-function circuits; the interrupt mechanism allows the CPU to sleep or do other work when the accelerator is busy, avoiding polling idling and burning electricity. Compared to the three-engine stack of CPU+DSP+AI engine in Reference [3], here only one RISC-V core and one dedicated coprocessor are retained, cutting off the area and decoding power consumption of two complete sets of programmable computing engines—the result that "power consumption is only 47.7% of [3]" (Section III) mainly comes from this architectural saving.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The entire algorithm pipeline can be completely read from the module sequence in Fig. 1: framing → preemphasis → windowing → 128-point FFT → power spectrum → mel filter → log → dct, with the output sent to the TCU for template matching. The mathematical form and design intent are given step by step:

- **Framing and Pre-emphasis**: Under the short-time stationary assumption of speech, it is cut into overlapping frames; pre-emphasis is a first-order high-pass filter $y[n] = x[n] - a \cdot x[n-1]$, used to compensate for the approximately 6 dB high-frequency drop per octave in the speech spectrum, making the energy of subsequent spectrum bands more balanced, which is conducive to the utilization of the dynamic range of fixed-point circuits. The paper does not report frame length, frame shift, and pre-emphasis coefficient $a$.
- **Windowing**: The Hamming window multiplies the frame data point by point to suppress spectral leakage caused by truncation; the windowing module in Fig. 1 is annotated with "hamming window" and coefficient ROM (Coef).
- **128-point FFT**: This is the computational bottleneck named by the paper (Section II-B). Under the base-2 Cooley-Tukey decomposition, $128 = 2^7$, which exactly corresponds to 7 stages of butterfly operations, with each stage containing 64 butterflies. The core of the butterfly is complex multiply-add $X = A + W \cdot B$ ($W$ is the rotation factor). The paper's "7-stage pipeline" (Section II-B) corresponds exactly to the seven-stage butterfly decomposition of $2^7$: one operation stage is made into one pipeline segment, and the butterfly operations of that stage are unfolded in parallel within the segment. The FFT part of Fig. 1 draws components such as input cache (Input Cache), butterfly processing unit array (Butterfly PE), interconnection network (Interconnection), rotation factor storage (Coef Memory), and bit-reversal rearrangement (data reverse), which is consistent with the organization of standard FFT processors. Why is FFT the bottleneck: in the entire MFCC pipeline, it is the stage with the largest amount of computation and the most complex multiplications; without pipelining, every frame would have to idle and wait for the entire butterfly tree to finish calculating, and the frame latency would be eaten up by serial latency.
- **Power Spectrum**: $|X[k]|^2 = \text{Re}^2 + \text{Im}^2$; the real/imag register groups are visible in Fig. 1, performing only multiplication and addition.
- **Mel Filtering**: A set of triangular filters equally spaced on the Mel scale, aggregating the power spectrum into Mel band energy. Its key mathematical property is **sparsity**: each triangular filter covers only a small segment of frequency points (bins) on the frequency axis. In the multiplication of the Mel coefficient matrix and the spectrum vector, most frequency points correspond to zero coefficients—most multiplications in a complete dense matrix-vector multiplication are "multiply by zero". This is the basis for the paper's "sparsity-based Mel filter optimizations" (Section II-B): the hardware only traverses the non-zero frequency points covered by each filter according to the boundary index, skipping all zero multiplications. The hardware evidence given in Fig. 1 is the separation of boundary memory and coef memory,配合 (paired with) boundary cnt / coef cnt counters and odd/even dual data paths: the boundary table drives the traversal range, and the coefficient table provides non-zero weights. The amount of calculation is proportional to the actual coverage width of the filter, not to the length of the spectrum. The paper does not report the number of filter groups and the specific sparsity ratio.
- **Logarithm**: Changes the multiplicative resonance structure into an additive one, compresses the dynamic range, and also compresses the Mel energy domain into a narrow bit width usable by fixed-point circuits. The log unit and Qtr annotation (suspected to be a piecewise approximation such as quarter approximation) are visible in Fig. 1; the paper does not report the specific approximation scheme for logarithmic operations.
- **DCT**: Performs discrete cosine transform on the Mel logarithmic spectrum, removes correlation between adjacent frequency bands, concentrates energy into low-order cepstral coefficients, and taking the first few dimensions constitutes the MFCC vector. The DCT part of Fig. 1 draws five stages from stage #0 to stage #4 and annotations for scaling/encoding of ×8, ×8, ×4, ×2, ×1; the paper body only generally wrote "dataflow enhancements for the DCT stages" (Section II-B), and the functional division of each stage and the meaning of scaling are not expanded.
- **VQ Codebook**: The paper uses VQ to train codebooks to represent multiple keywords (Section II-A)—mapping each frame feature vector to the index of the nearest codeword in the codebook, after which the representation degrades from a continuous vector to a codeword index sequence. Mathematically, this changes the encoding problem of probability distribution to a nearest neighbor quantization problem: $\min ||x - c_i||$, where $c_i$ is the codeword. The benefit is double: template storage degrades from a floating-point matrix to a short index string, and the distance calculation in the TCU can also be completed in the quantized domain. The paper does not report the codebook size, quantization distortion, and training corpus.
- **DTW Template Matching**: Classic DTW searches for the optimal warping path between the test sequence and the template, with the recurrence relation $D[i,j] = d(i,j) + \min(D[i-1,j], D[i,j-1], D[i-1,j-1])$, where $d(i,j)$ is the distance between the $i$-th frame and the $j$-th frame of the template. Complete dynamic programming requires maintaining an $O(N \cdot M)$ cost grid and performing three-direction backtracking, which corresponds to large-capacity row caches and complex control flow in hardware. The paper **refines it to a fixed diagonal distance computation** (Section II-B): only accumulating the distance of frame-by-frame alignment along the diagonal, shrinking the search space from $O(N \cdot M)$ to $O(N)$, and the three-direction comparison and backtracking storage disappear as a whole. This is exactly the source of "area reduced by 99.2%, power consumption reduced by 84.2%" (Section II-B)—what is saved is not the precision of the operator, but the storage array of the entire DP grid and its read/write power consumption. The TCU part of Fig. 1 shows template storage (Template Memory), distance calculation unit (D Elem), MAC, multiple sets of PEs and MUX selection, and Zero/Encoder scaling logic, which is a regular array with "distance calculation—accumulation—selection" as the main trunk.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: FEU Feature Extraction Unit—7-Stage FFT Pipeline, Sparse Mel Filtering, and DCT Dataflow

The FEU undertakes the entire MFCC pipeline. The paper has a clear "why" for its three architectural improvements. **First, the 7-stage pipeline deals with the 128-point FFT** (Section II-B): FFT is the only segment in the pipeline dense with complex multiplications. If not pipelined, the wall-clock time for per-frame feature extraction is dominated by the serial latency of FFT, and the frame latency indicator cannot be achieved; cutting the pipeline segments according to the $2^7$ butterfly stage count, with registers separating the segments, allows overlapping execution of consecutive frames when they arrive. While throughput increases, the latency per frame is also diluted. **Second, sparsity-based Mel filter optimization** (Section II-B): As mentioned above, the frequency domain support of triangular filters is naturally local; a dense implementation is a waste of silicon area; the boundary-table-driven sparse implementation makes the number of multiplications proportional to the actual coverage width of the filter, while saving the energy consumption of reading zero coefficients—memory access is saved, calculation is saved, and coefficient storage is shrunk from a full matrix to a non-zero value table. **Third, dataflow enhancement for each stage of DCT** (Section II-B): DCT coefficients are fixed, and inputs arrive in a per-frame streaming manner, suitable for distributed arithmetic or pulsating array organization using coefficient lookup tables plus shift-and-add. The structure of multi-stage stages and decreasing scaling (×8 to ×1) in Fig. 1 implies a fixed-point implementation with step-by-step bit-width compression, but the paper does not provide details for each stage. The comprehensive effect is: the FEU stabilizes the rhythm of "outputting a set of cepstral features for every incoming frame," laying the foundation for the system's 2.98 ms frame latency (Section III). Why is frame latency so important: the response time of an always-on detector is approximately equal to "the duration of speaking the complete sentence + the processing latency of the last few frames"; for every additional millisecond of frame processing latency, the perceptible wake-up lag for the user increases by one millisecond, which is a hard indicator of voice interaction experience.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: TCU Template Classification Unit—Radical Simplification from DTW to Fixed Diagonal Distance

This is the sharpest cut in the entire paper, and also the hardest single-variable comparison with the most solid numbers: after constraining the DP grid search of complete DTW to a fixed diagonal path, the TCU area is reduced by 99.2% and power consumption is reduced by 84.2% (Section II-B). Why dare to do this: First, in the KWS usage scenario, test speech and templates are usually the same isolated word, with similar frame counts and limited differences in speaking speed; the diagonal path already covers most real alignment needs, and the bending capability of complete DP is idle most of the time; Second, the row and column caches of the DP grid are the largest block of storage in the TCU; cutting it contributes far more to area reduction than cutting multipliers; Third, fixed diagonal degenerates control flow into simple counting, saving the power consumption of state machines and backtracking logic. The cost is equally clear: when the speaker's speaking speed differs greatly from the template, there are pauses in the word, or the length of keywords varies, the diagonal assumption fails, and distance mismatch directly translates to missed detections or false detections—this is a typical trade-off of "exchanging alignment flexibility for area and power consumption." The paper bears this cost with an 89% upper limit on recognition rate (Section III), but does not quantify how big this cost is (no accuracy comparison before compression, see the experimental section). The hardware organization of the TCU (Fig. 1) is centered on template storage. The PE array calculates the per-frame distance between test frames and multiple templates in parallel, the MAC performs accumulation, and the MUX and comparison logic complete the selection among multiple templates, with the output being the recognition result. Since VQ has compressed the frame representation into codeword indices, distance calculation is performed in the quantized domain, and both the multiplier bit width and template capacity are compressed synchronously—the VQ at the algorithm layer and the PE array at the micro-architecture layer form a typical cross-layer cooperation here.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Three Strategies of Algorithm-Hardware Co-Design Compression—Downsampling, Algorithm Simplification, and Offline Parameter Optimization

The paper's algorithm compression (Section II-A) explicitly lists three items: data downsampling, algorithm simplification, and offline parameter optimization. The quantitative effect is in Section III: deployed on the E203 RISC-V processor, the optimized algorithm reduces computation time by 98.2% and memory usage by 98.3%. The design intent of the three strategies can be dismantled one by one. **Data downsampling**: Reduces the data volume of each subsequent stage from the signal side—every reduction in sampling rate or feature dimension will compound along the entire pipeline, causing the FFT point count, filter group width, DCT scale, and template length to shrink accordingly; the paper does not report the specific downsampling position and ratio. **Algorithm simplification**: This is the action of "replacing the algorithm itself with an equivalent/approximate form that is cheaper to compute," such as the fixed diagonal DTW mentioned above, finding free lunches in mathematical properties (sparsity, diagonal alignment). **Offline parameter optimization**: All quantities that can be determined during the training/compilation period—windowing coefficients, Mel boundaries and weights, DCT coefficients, VQ codebooks, DTW templates—are pre-calculated and solidified into ROM tables. During runtime, only lookup tables and arithmetic are performed, without any floating-point or coefficient generation. The motivation for this item is to turn "general computation" into "data movement plus fixed arithmetic," which only specialized hardware can achieve in its simplest form. It should be noted that 98.2%/98.3% is the **total effect** of the three strategies and is a software comparison on the RISC-V. The paper does not break down the contribution of each strategy item by item, nor does it report the impact of these compressions on accuracy (no comparison of recognition rates before and after compression is seen).

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Analog Frontend Audio Path and SoC Low-Power Integration

From the physical audio signal to digital features, the path is: Microphone → Analog Frontend AFE (including Codec) → Custom Audio module of SoC (I2S RX receive) → KWS Accelerator. The division of labor in the paper is: the custom Audio module is specifically responsible for driving the AFE to complete speech input and output (Section II-C). Fig. 2 shows that the AUDIO peripheral includes two channels of I2S RX and I2S TX, with the analog frontend and Codec drawn off-chip and configured via I2C. The motivation for choosing standard digital audio interfaces like I2S and leaving the analog part off-chip is cost and process: making an analog frontend with a digital 40nm process would increase mask and design costs, and an external AFE also allows the whole machine manufacturer to flexibly select models according to noise indicators; the cost is that system-level power consumption cannot be integrated into the chip for unified optimization like in Reference [2] (the paper does not report the AFE model, gain strategy, and power consumption). Regarding power management, the paper does not report a dedicated power management unit or power gating/multi-power domain design; the actual low-power landing points are in three architectural facts: First, **dual clock domains**—the SoC main clock is 50 MHz, while the KWS accelerator clock is only 400 kHz (Section III). Dynamic power consumption is approximately proportional to frequency. The accelerator works under the 400 kHz slow clock, directly compressing power consumption to 28.3 µW (Section III). This is feasible precisely because the real-time computational volume of the template route is small enough, and there is no need for a high main frequency to chase throughput; Second, **interrupt-driven coprocessor architecture**—the CPU can idle after configuring the accelerator, and the accelerator reports the result through an interrupt (intr signal in Fig. 1, Interrupt Ctrl. in Fig. 2) after calculation, eliminating polling idling; Third, **storage footprint without DNN**—128 kB of on-chip storage (Section III) is sufficient to accommodate all codebooks, templates, and programs, with static power consumption and storage area simultaneously limited. The overall power consumption of the SoC is 12.4 mW @ 50 MHz (Section III), of which the accelerator's 28.3 µW accounts for only about two thousandths, indicating that the energy focus of always-on voice monitoring is no longer on KWS itself, but on the processor and peripheral subsystems—this also implies that the subsequent system-level optimization space lies in the CPU-side sleep strategy, which the paper does not expand on.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared with DNN chips represented by References [1][2], the difference is route-based: [1][2] use deep models (including techniques such as AAD gating and Skip-RNN frame skipping) to exchange for higher recognition accuracy, while this paper uses the template matching route to exchange for an order-of-magnitude advantage in area and latency—frame latency 2.98 ms, ten times acceleration relative to [1], normalized area is about one-sixth of [2] (Section III). Compared with the multi-engine SoC of Reference [3], this paper retains only a single RISC-V core plus a single coprocessor, removing the DSP and general AI engine, resulting in "smallest area, lowest latency, power consumption only 47.7% of [3]" (Section III)—the essence is judging that the KWS data flow is regular enough, and it is not worth paying the multi-engine tax for versatility. Compared with "pure software KWS running on an MCU," the coprocessor of this paper cuts computation time and memory by about 98% each (Section III). Compared with Reference [4] (AIMMI, 2MB on-chip MRAM audio-image multimodal SoC), this paper abandons multimodality and large storage, focusing on the extreme compactness of a single KWS task (listed as a comparison object in Section III, claiming optimal area and latency). In summary, the core of the technical difference can be summarized in one sentence: **At the point in time of 2025, using the "oldest" algorithm paired with the "most customized" circuit, winning simultaneously in the three dimensions of PPA, and leaving the loss in the accuracy dimension to be digested by the application layer**.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The paper does not report the training dataset, the corpus for constructing the VQ codebook and DTW templates, the number of speakers, channel and recording conditions, nor does it report the audio sampling rate and bit width (AFE/Codec parameters are not reported). Regarding evaluation data, the paper reports: the system performs stably on over 300 one-second test cases, with a command recognition accuracy of up to 89% (Section III, "over 300 1-second test cases, correctly recognizing up to 89% of commands"); the test commands are in both Chinese and English, covering categories such as system wake-up, home appliance control, and robotic arm gestures (Section III demonstration part). The total number of command words and the distribution of the number of cases per word are not reported by the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metrics used by the paper can be categorized into three types (all from Section III). **Performance metrics**: Command recognition accuracy (up to 89%, over 300 cases); frame latency 2.98 ms ("frame latency", i.e., the end-to-end time from processing a frame of speech features to producing the result); KWS average processing time 0.4 ms and SoC average processing time 0.5 s. Rationale for selection: Latency metrics directly correspond to the experience of "whether the wake-up response follows the hand"; the relationship between 0.4 ms and 2.98 ms is not explained by the paper (the former is suspected to be the pure computation time of the accelerator, and the latter is the frame-level latency including pipeline fill, which is an inference); the composition of the 0.5 s SoC-level time (proportion of audio transmission, peripheral interaction, wireless reporting, etc.) is not broken down by the paper. **Power and Area Metrics (PPA)**: The SoC consumes 12.4 mW at 50 MHz under 40nm process, and the KWS accelerator consumes 28.3 µW at 400 kHz; the accelerator area is 0.28 mm², the SoC core area is 1.34 mm², and the on-chip storage is 128 kB. Rationale for selection: Hard currency indicators for chip work, directly mapping to chip cost and battery life. **Relative Improvement Metrics**: The optimized algorithm reduces computation time by 98.2% and memory by 98.3% relative to the unoptimized version (deployed on E203); the TCU fixed diagonal simplification reduces area by 99.2% and power consumption by 84.2% relative to complete DTW (Section II-B). It is worth noting that the commonly used false alarm rate, miss rate, ROC/DET curves, and robustness curves under different signal-to-noise ratios in the KWS field are not reported by the paper at all—a single recognition accuracy is not sufficient to completely characterize the reliability of an always-on detector (the damage of false alarms to user experience is often greater than that of missed detections), which is an obvious gap under the volume of a demo paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The horizontal comparison of the paper (Section III) covers four works: First, for Reference [1] (AAD-KWS, 28nm JSSC 2023, sub-microwatt): The paper's accelerator frame latency is 2.98 ms, achieving a ten-fold speedup ("delivering a 10× speedup over [1]"). Second, for Reference [2] (Skip-RNN 1.5µW SoC, 28nm JSSC 2024): The paper's accelerator area is 0.28 mm², which is about one-sixth of its area after normalization ("1/6 of the area of [2] after normalization"); the normalization method (how to convert different processes/frequencies to the same caliber) is not reported by the paper. Third, for Reference [3] (CPU+DSP+AI engine TinyML methodology of DAC 2023): The paper has the smallest area and lowest latency among the compared IoT audio SoCs, with power consumption only 47.7% of it ("just 47.7% of the power consumption of [3]"). Fourth, for Reference [4] (AIMMI, 2MB MRAM multimodal SoC, JSSC 2024): Listed as a comparison object for IoT audio SoCs, claiming optimal area and latency (Section III), without providing item-by-item numbers. Three issues of caliber that must be pointed out: First, this paper is 40nm while the comparison objects are 28nm. The process generation gap is unfavorable for power and area comparison (usually more advanced processes have the advantage). Achieving the above comparison results under these conditions indicates that the architectural advantage is real, but re-testing under the same process would be more persuasive; Second, the accuracy dimension is completely absent—the recognition accuracy of [1][2] was not re-tested on the test cases of this paper, and the gap with DNN methods can only be discussed qualitatively; Third, the platform caliber of the power consumption comparison (SoC vs. SoC or accelerator vs. accelerator) is not clarified in some comparisons.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not report a formal ablation experiment table (limited by demo length, there are no numbered tables in the entire paper). There are three pieces of "quasi-ablation" evidence that can be extracted, and they are all very informative. **First, the total effect of algorithm compression** (Section III): On the same E203 processor, the optimized algorithm reduces computation time by 98.2% and memory by 98.3% relative to the unoptimized version—this quantifies the combined benefits of the three strategies of downsampling, algorithm simplification, and offline parameter optimization, but is not broken down item by item, so readers cannot judge which item is the main part. **Second, the single-variable comparison of the TCU** (Section II-B): After changing complete DTW to fixed diagonal distance, the area is reduced by 99.2% and power consumption is reduced by 84.2%—this is the only "change one module, report two hard indicators" comparison in the entire paper, directly proving that the DP grid storage and control logic are the dominant costs of DTW hardware, not the multipliers of distance calculation; at the same time, it indirectly quantifies the "gold content" of the diagonal simplification: this simplification contributes the vast majority of the area reduction of the TCU. **The third is the missing ablation**: The 89% recognition rate is the final state after the superposition of all radical compressions. The paper has neither the accuracy baseline before compression nor the accuracy after retreating any single compression item. "How much accuracy was lost by radical compression, and which compression hurts accuracy the most" is completely not reported by the paper. For engineers who want to reuse this method, this is the experiment that needs to be supplemented by themselves: the accuracy loss of fixed diagonal DTW on custom corpus should be measured against complete DTW and band-constrained DTW respectively.

## Main Contributions

1. **A complete, demonstrable low-cost intelligent audio SoC**: 40nm process, RISC-V (Nuclei E203) main control + KWS coprocessor + custom Audio module driving external AFE, AHB/APB dual bus and interrupt mechanism collaboration (Section II-C, Fig. 2); core area is only 1.34 mm², on-chip storage is 128 kB (Section III), with the smallest area, lowest latency, and power consumption of 47.7% of [3] among the compared IoT audio SoCs (References [3][4]).
2. **A complete three-layer implementation demonstration of algorithm-hardware co-design**: Algorithm selection avoids large-parameter DNNs (MFCC+VQ+DTW template route) → Three strategies of algorithm compression (data downsampling, algorithm simplification, offline parameter optimization, reducing computation time by 98.2% and memory by 98.3%) → Micro-architecture customization module by module (7-stage FFT pipeline, sparse Mel filtering, DCT dataflow enhancement, fixed diagonal DTW reducing TCU area by 99.2% and power consumption by 84.2%). This methodology of "letting go a little at each layer, yielding an order of magnitude in total" is directly transferable to other always-on perception tasks (acoustic event detection, vibration monitoring, etc.).
3. **Strong results at the PPA level**: Frame latency 2.98 ms, ten-fold acceleration relative to Reference [1], normalized area is about one-sixth of Reference [2], accelerator 28.3 µW @ 400 kHz (Section III), demonstrating that the template route still has latency and area lower bounds that are difficult to reach for the DNN route under 2025 process conditions.
4. **A complete evidence chain from chip to demonstration**: Not satisfied with estimation after synthesis, but built an FPGA prototype (Xilinx Artix-7 XC7A100T, Fig. 3) and evaluation platform, running over 300 real one-second voice cases (highest 89% accuracy, KWS average processing 0.4 ms, SoC average 0.5 s), demonstrating wake-up, home appliance control, and robotic arm gesture control of Chinese and English commands on-site. Components are wirelessly interconnected via Bluetooth, recognition results are displayed on the host computer, and actions are executed by the robotic arm and LEDs (Section III)—proving that this is a system that can truly interact, not just a paper architecture.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**The accuracy ceiling is route-based**. The 89% command recognition accuracy (Section III) is sufficient for demonstration scenarios, but there is a visible gap compared to the levels usually achieved by DNN solutions; the discriminative power of template matching is weaker than that of deep models, and it degrades more steeply under conditions of noise, accent, and far-field reverberation—the paper does not report any experiments under noise conditions, which is exactly the most fatal dimension in the real deployment of smart speakers and wearable devices. **Fixed diagonal DTW sacrifices alignment flexibility**. Differences in speaking speed, pauses in words, and variations in the length of multi-syllable words will cause the diagonal path assumption to fail; intermediate forms such as band constraints (e.g., Sakoe-Chiba band) developed by DTW over decades could have recovered some bending capability with a small area cost. The paper does not explore this design space, nor does it quantify the miss rate caused by the failure of the diagonal assumption. **The scalability of the template route is limited**. Adding new keywords requires re-collecting corpus, retraining the VQ codebook and DTW templates, and there is no convenient custom wake-word path from "text to model," which contradicts the current product trend of user-defined wake-words; the paper does not report the upper limit of the number of command words supported by the system and the capacity constraints of template storage. **The "up to" expression of 89% implies uneven accuracy for each command word**, but the paper does not report the per-word accuracy distribution, so it is impossible to judge which word is the短板 (short board), whether it is a template quality problem or an alignment problem. **The relationship between algorithm compression and accuracy is opaque**: Downsampling and algorithm simplification will inevitably affect accuracy, but the paper has no accuracy baseline before compression, and the "safe boundary" of the compression strategy (whether it can still be compressed, where the limit has been reached) cannot be judged.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

In addition to the above missing items, there are several caliber issues. **Data and feature parameters are completely blank**: The source of training/testing corpus, number of speakers, sampling rate, frame length and shift, number of Mel filter groups, number of cepstral dimensions, VQ codebook size, and DTW template length are all not reported by the paper, making it impossible for third parties to reproduce. **Detector metrics are absent**: There is no false alarm rate, miss rate, or operating characteristic curve; none of the core reliability indicators of an always-on detector are present; the scale of over 300 one-second cases is also on the small side, and the confidence interval of 89% will be relatively wide. **Lack of caliber details in cross-paper comparison**: The area comparison with Reference [2] is noted as "after normalization," but the normalization model (process scaling factor, frequency normalization) is not reported; the process generation gap of 40nm vs. 28nm is not discussed. **The 0.5 s SoC-level processing time is not broken down into its composition**, and the relationship with the 0.4 ms KWS processing time and 2.98 ms frame latency is not explained, so readers cannot judge whether the system-level bottleneck is in the audio path, peripheral interaction, or wireless reporting. **Power consumption test conditions are not reported**: The test vectors for 12.4 mW and 28.3 µW, the proportion of dynamic and static power consumption, and the state of the CPU during accelerator work (sleeping or idling) are not given, so the system-level energy optimization space cannot be evaluated.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the paper's own framework, the most direct next steps are five. **First, band-constrained diagonal DTW**: Relax the band search by a few frames on both sides of the fixed diagonal path, exchanging a small amount of row cache (area slightly rebounds) for bending capability, expected to directly lift the recognition rate on corpus with diverse speaking speeds; or do two-stage matching—first coarse screening with fixed diagonal, then fine alignment with complete DP for candidate templates, with the average overhead still very low. **Second, ultra-low power gating front-end**: Drawing on the idea of Reference [1] to embed acoustic activity detection into the feature path, turn off the FEU or even the entire clock domain during silence, further lowering the 28.3 µW always-on baseline—this paper currently has no AAD/VQ cascade, which is a standard move in the microwatt race. **Third, programmable templates and custom wake-words**: Make the template storage software-writable, cooperating with the RISC-V core to implement a template enroll process of "register after speaking three times," turning the scalability short board of the template route into a differentiated advantage of "no retraining, immediate effect"—this is one of the few dimensions where the template method is naturally superior to DNN. **Fourth, a two-stage hybrid architecture of template + DNN**: Use the template method as the first stage for always-on (microwatt-level always-on), and wake up the DNN for second-stage confirmation (milliwatt-level instantaneous) after triggering, combining power consumption and accuracy. Such cascading is already a mature model in the industry, and the 28.3 µW accelerator of this paper is exactly an ideal first stage. **Fifth, completion of the evaluation system**: Supplement noise/far-field conditions, false alarm rate, per-word accuracy distribution, and compression strategy ablation, and re-test the PPA comparison under the same 28nm process, making the conclusion of "order-of-magnitude advantage" more irrefutable. Overall prospect: The value of this paper lies not in the novelty of a single technology—MFCC, VQ, DTW, and pipelined FFT are all decades-old tools—but in that it proves with a complete system that can be demonstrated on-site that "under the correct combination of constraints, old algorithms plus extremely customized circuits are still a powerful answer for the IoT voice entry in 2025," and that it has turned algorithm-hardware co-design into a transferable three-layer methodology.
