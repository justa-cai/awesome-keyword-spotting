## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is a key component in modern speech recognition technology, tasked with recognizing specific words or phrases within a continuous audio stream. Unlike traditional speech recognition systems that transcribe entire dialogues, KWS systems are designed to detect preset keywords efficiently and with low overhead. Consequently, they are widely deployed in voice wake-up assistants like Amazon Alexa and Apple Siri, as well as in monitoring and emergency response systems.

The paper’s starting point is the intersection of two demand curves. On one hand, continuous advancements in Internet of Things (IoT), Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), Speaker Verification (SV), and Text-to-Speech (TTS) technologies have driven a surge in the usage of intelligent voice assistants. On the other hand, this increased usage has directly heightened user demands for **privacy** and **personalization**. Specifically, this contradiction unfolds as follows: Conventional KWS (C-KWS) only recognizes factory-preset keywords and does not customize for individual users—meaning all household members are forced to share the same wake-up word, where anyone calling can trigger the device. In scenarios where devices are always listening, "anyone can wake it up" inherently implies privacy and security exposure (e.g., others impersonating commands, high risk of false triggers). An ideal edge-side voice interface should have a dual threshold: a **customizable keyword** (hard to guess) and the **owner’s voiceprint** (hard to mimic), requiring both conditions to be met simultaneously for activation. This requires a single lightweight network to possess both open-vocabulary keyword detection and speaker discrimination capabilities, while edge devices lack the computational budget for two independent systems.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing work into four technical routes in the introduction and points out their respective deficiencies:

- **Two-stage methods**: These perform acoustic modeling first, followed by complex keyword search, where Weighted Finite State Transducers (WFST) have become the dominant graph search method. The common issue is that the search phase requires considerable computational resources, which directly conflicts with the computational constraints of edge-side deployment.
- **End-to-end Query-by-Example (QbyE)**: This compares input speech with a single audio sample registered by the user. While this is a classic end-to-end implementation, registration requires providing audio, and the registration quality is affected by acoustic conditions at the time (environmental noise, channel, speaker state), resulting in high user operational costs.
- **Cross-modal methods (Audio and Text combination)**: These register keywords using text. The advantage is that it is simple for users and the registration process does not depend on any specific acoustic conditions. The disadvantage is the difficulty in handling phonetically similar words, making them prone to false triggers from confusables or phonetically similar phrases—an inherent weakness of the text-registration route.
- **Metric learning methods**: These represent variable-length words using fixed-dimensional vectors, where similarity is directly related to the relative distance within the embedding space. This route can adapt to open-set keywords (comparing distances for words not seen during training), but it **does not explicitly consider user identity**, failing to answer "is this word spoken by this person?"
- **Multi-task methods for joint KWS and SV**: Existing work binds keyword detection and target user identification together through multi-task learning networks. This is the category closest to the paper’s goal, but their common gap is that keywords remain a fixed set, **failing to provide a personalized experience where individual users can freely customize keywords**.

In summary, while open-vocabulary capabilities and speaker discrimination capabilities each have mature approaches, they have never coexisted within the same lightweight network—this is the gap the paper aims to fill.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

The paper aims to complete both KWS and SV tasks within a single lightweight network, supporting three usage modes: C-KWS (Closed-set Keyword Spotting), OV-KWS (Open-Vocabulary Keyword Spotting), and PCOV-KWS (Personalized Customizable Open-Vocabulary Keyword Spotting). This presents four progressively layered technical challenges: First, the challenge of training criteria—softmax-based losses contain zero-sum competition between classes, and the number of classes is fixed during training, which is fundamentally incompatible with the open-vocabulary setting where new keywords unseen during training appear at inference. A metric learning-based criterion independent of fixed class numbers is required. Second, the multi-task challenge—KWS focuses on phonetic content, while SV focuses on timbre; the loss gradients of the two tasks may conflict. Direct equal-weight addition will drag each other down, requiring a conflict-aware loss weighting strategy. Third, the inference mode challenge—the three tasks have completely different dependency ratios for "keyword confidence" and "voiceprint confidence." The same model must be able to switch fusion methods at inference time based on the task. Fourth, the resource constraint challenge—all of the above must fit within fewer parameters (final 211k/376k, compared to PhonMatchNet’s 655k, see Table III) and lower computational cost (1.13M/1.8M FLOPs, see Table IV) than existing OV-KWS models.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

As shown in Fig. 1 of the paper, the overall data flow of PCOV-KWS is: Mel Log Filter Bank Features enter the audio encoder; the encoder consists of a **Shared Encoder** and two **Linear Sub-encoders (KWS Linear Sub-encoder / SV Linear Sub-encoder)**, which produce Keyword Embedding ($e_i^k$) and Voiceprint Embedding ($e_i^v$), respectively; the two embeddings enter cosine classifiers with a Margin Scheduler for SphereFace2 metric learning; the inference end fuses the two output confidences into a PCOV-KWS decision through a Confidence Integration Block (CIB).

The "why" behind this hierarchical design is worth breaking down:

- **Hard Parameter Sharing (HPS) at the bottom**. The inputs for multi-task training are organized as multi-label samples $\{x_i, y_i^k, y_i^s\}$, where $x_i$ is the input audio feature, and $y_i^k$ and $y_i^s$ are the keyword label and speaker label for the respective tasks—each forward pass receives supervision from both paths simultaneously. The motivation for bottom-level sharing is that KWS and SV have highly common needs for low-level acoustic features (spectral texture, formant trajectories, short-term energy patterns). Sharing the bottom level saves parameters and implicitly regularizes both supervision paths. The benefit of this choice is quantifiable in Table II: the multi-task version of TDResNeXt has only 211k parameters, whereas "two independent networks" (Vanilla+SV) require 396k (approximately double a single network’s 198k), saving about 47% of parameters.
- **Top-level split into two isomorphic linear sub-encoders**. The paper explicitly states that "KWS and SV differ in top-level characteristics," so the top of the encoder is copied and separated into two structurally identical linear sub-encoders, each learning task-specific high-level features. This is a low-cost compromise between hard and soft sharing: differentiation occurs only at the top level, avoiding parameter doubling from layer-by-layer soft sharing, while retaining task-specificity in high-level semantics.
- **Cosine classifier replacing the fully connected classification head**. The classification head is no longer "embedding × class weight matrix + softmax," but rather the normalized dot product of the embedding and trainable prototype vectors. This change is a prerequisite for open-vocabulary capability: the judgment of new keywords no longer depends on fixed class dimensions but on similarity comparison with registered embeddings.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Cosine Classifier (Eq. 1)**. For the keyword embedding $e_i^k$, the output of the cosine classifier for the $k$-th class is:

$$C(e_i^k)_k = \text{sim}(e_i^k, W_i^k)$$

where $\text{sim}(\cdot)$ is the dot product of the normalized $e_i^k$ and the trainable weight $W_i^k$ of the $i$-th binary classifier, i.e., cosine similarity.

**Similarity Dynamic Range Expansion Function (Eq. 2)**. During training, the cosine similarity distribution of positive and negative sample pairs is inconsistent: negative pair scores have small variance and are concentrated, while positive pair scores have larger variance. The two distributions overlap in the middle region, making it difficult to set a clear decision threshold. To solve the overlap problem, the paper adopts the function proposed in [24] to expand the dynamic range of similarity:

$$g(z) = 2 \cdot \left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)^t - 1$$

where $z$ is the input cosine value, and $t$ is a hyperparameter controlling the intensity of distribution adjustment. This function is monotonic on $[-1,1]$. Through power-law nonlinearity, it remaps and expands the originally clustered negative pair scores, pulling apart the overlap region of positive and negative distributions, making threshold selection clear. Experiments (Fig. 2a) show that $t=5$ yields optimal performance.

**SphereFace2 Binary Loss (Eqs. 3–5)**. For a mini-batch containing $N$ samples, with $y_i \in \{1,2,\ldots,K\}$, the loss consists of positive and negative sample terms:

$$L_{ki}^{+} = \lambda \cdot \log\left(1 + e^{-s\cdot g(C(e_i^k)_{y_i}, t)+b}\right)$$

$$L_{ki}^{-} = (1-\lambda) \cdot \sum_{j \neq y_i} \log\left(1 + e^{s\cdot g(C(e_i^k)_j, t)+b}\right)$$

$$L_k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(L_{ki}^{+} + L_{ki}^{-}\right)$$

where $s$ is the scaling factor, $b$ is the bias; $L_v$ (loss for the speaker task) is constructed in the same form. The $K$ classes are thus transformed into $K$ mutually independent binary classification targets: the target class data serves as positive samples, and all other class data serves as negative samples. $\lambda$ controls the loss weight of positive and negative samples; Fig. 2b shows that $\lambda = 0.7$ yields optimal performance.

**Gradient Conflict Detection and Projection (Eqs. 6–7)**. Let the gradients of $L_k$ and $L_v$ be $g_k$ and $g_v$. First, calculate the inner product $g_{kv} = g_k^T g_v$ to detect conflict: if $g_{kv} < 0$ (the two task gradients are in conflict), project out the component of $g_k$ that conflicts with $g_v$:

$$g_k \leftarrow g_k - \frac{g_{kv}}{\|g_v\|^2 + \epsilon} \cdot g_v$$

where $\epsilon$ is a small value to prevent division by zero. This is the core of Gradient Surgery (PCGrad): projecting one task’s gradient onto the normal plane of the other task’s gradient, retaining only the non-interfering components.

**Conflict-Driven Adaptive Loss Weighting (Eqs. 8–9)**. Beyond projection, the paper further uses the same conflict metric to update the loss weights $\omega = [\omega_k, \omega_v]$ (initially $[1,1]$):

$$\omega_k \leftarrow \omega_k - \frac{g_{kv}}{\|g_v\|^2 + \epsilon}$$

Symmetric processing is applied to $\omega_v$, and the final loss is $L = \omega_k \cdot L_k + \omega_v \cdot L_v$.

**Confidence Integration Block CIB (Eq. 10)**. At inference, the model’s two output confidences $\Phi_k$ (keyword) and $\Phi_v$ (voiceprint) are linearly fused:

$$\Phi = \alpha \cdot \Phi_k + (1-\alpha) \cdot \Phi_v$$

$\alpha$ is selected via grid search on the validation set to minimize EER.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Rewriting Multi-class Classification into Multiple Binary Classifications using SphereFace2 to Eliminate Inter-class Competition

The abstract lists this as the first core design: "A training criterion distinct from softmax loss, transforming multi-class classification into multiple binary classifications, thereby eliminating inter-class competition." The deep logic is: the probability normalization of softmax loss places all classes in zero-sum competition—an increase in one class’s score necessarily suppresses others, and the classification head dimension is bound to the number of training classes, making it impossible to compute scores for new words encountered at inference. OV-KWS requires the ability to "compare similarity for keywords not seen during training." The K independent binary targets of SphereFace2 exactly decouple inter-class relationships: each embedding only needs to compute one cosine similarity with its corresponding prototype, combined with the dynamic range expansion in Eq. 2 to solve the overlap of positive/negative distributions and unclear thresholds. Eqs. 2 and 3–5 form a self-consistent open-vocabulary training criterion. Additionally, Fig. 1 labels the cosine classifier as "SphereFace2 with Margin Scheduler," but the main text does not provide the specific functional form of the margin scheduler. This detail is inherited from the original SphereFace2 literature [24], which the paper does not report.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Conflict-Aware Multi-task Loss Weighting (Extended Usage of PCGrad)

The core obstacle in multi-task training is the conflict in gradient directions between tasks. The paper adopts PCGrad as the loss weighting strategy, comparing it with Equal Weighting (EW, i.e., $L = L_k + L_v$). Technically, the paper does two things: first, standard gradient projection (Eq. 7), where conflicting components are surgically removed; second, using the conflict magnitude $g_{kv}$ to synchronously update loss weights (Eq. 8)—the more severe the conflict, the greater the reduction in the weight of the relevant task, allowing the trade-off at the loss level to automatically follow the degree of conflict at the gradient level. Fig. 2c and Fig. 2d show the FNR-FPR curves for PCGrad vs. EW on the OV-KWS and PCOV-KWS tasks, respectively, with PCGrad performing better on both tasks.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Confidence Integration Block CIB—One Model Serving Three Tasks

Inspired by [23], CIB addresses the inference mode problem: different tasks have completely different dependencies on the two confidences. Pure OV-KWS only cares about what word was said (ideally $\alpha=1$), pure SV only cares about who is speaking (ideally $\alpha=0$), and personalized PCOV-KWS requires both. CIB unifies these three modes into the same model via a linear interpolation (Eq. 10), with $\alpha$ determined by grid search on the validation set. Its value is validated in the ablation in Table II: removing CIB (manually fixing $\alpha$ by task: PCOV-KWS takes 0.5, OV-KWS takes 1, SV takes 0) causes SV’s EER to worsen from 3.85% to 4.16%, and PCOV-KWS’s EER to worsen from 3.85% to 3.98%—indicating that the "optimal fusion ratio for the personalized task" is not intuitively 0.5 vs. 0.5, and the value found by grid search is indeed superior.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: TDResNeXt Audio Encoder—NAS Search Plus ConvNeXt Modernization Recipe

The audio encoder is modified starting from TC-ResNet. The paper cites AutoKWS [28] applying NoisyDARTS [29] to the search results on TC-ResNet, then stacking several optimization techniques from ConvNeXt V1 and V2 [30][31], ultimately obtaining TDResNeXt (the name inherits the temporal convolution of TC-ResNet and the grouped convolution of ResNeXt; the main text uses "separate d.s. conv" as the final step of modification; the paper does not provide the full expansion of this abbreviation). Table I records every step of modification in 17 rows, showing accuracy and FLOPs on MSWC (means of five trials with standard deviation). This step-by-step chain of evidence is the part with the highest engineering content in the paper; detailed numbers are below in the ablation section. The overall conclusion: from TC-ResNet14-1.5’s 78.21% (6.86M FLOPs) to TDResNeXt’s 85.87% (1.13M FLOPs), accuracy increased by 7.66 percentage points while FLOPs decreased by approximately 83.5%.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Compared to four categories of existing work, PCOV-KWS’s differentiated positioning is very clear. Compared to previous KWS+SV multi-task works [21][22][23]: their keywords are a fixed set; this paper uses a metric-learning-based open-vocabulary head to achieve "arbitrary user-defined keywords + voiceprint dual threshold." Compared to cross-modal zero-shot methods (e.g., PhonMatchNet [14]): PhonMatchNet is phoneme-guided with 655k parameters; this paper’s PCOV-KWS-S requires only 211k, and PCOV-KWS-M requires 376k (Table III), while additionally possessing the speaker discrimination dimension. Compared to QbyE: this paper follows the text-registration route, where registration does not depend on acoustic conditions, while using the voiceprint threshold to partially counteract the text route’s weakness of "phonetically similar words being easily triggered." Compared to two-stage WFST schemes: it is fully end-to-end, with no search phase overhead. The most unique difference is "one training, three usages": the same model can serve as an OV-KWS, SV, and personalized KWS system by switching $\alpha$ in CIB, whereas the Vanilla+SV scheme requires maintaining a separate network for each task (396k vs. 211k in Table II).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

**Training Data**. The model is trained on the English subset of MSWC (Multilingual Spoken Words Corpus). The full MSWC contains over 23.4 million one-second audio clips, covering approximately 340,000 keywords, 115,000 speakers, and 50 languages. The paper uses G2PE (Grapheme-to-Phoneme conversion tool, abbreviation not expanded in the main text) to perform fine-grained filtering on the training data: only keywords with more than 5 phonemes are retained, and it is ensured that each keyword has at least 30 samples per speaker. The filtered dataset contains over 1.3 million one-second audio samples, 7,757 keywords, from 7,908 speakers. The "why" behind the filtering logic can be explained from the perspective of the two tasks: overly short words ($\le$5 phonemes) inherently have less discriminative information and are the main source of phonetic confusion; removing them first purifies the open-vocabulary training signal. "Each word, each speaker $\ge$30 samples" ensures sufficient speaker diversity within each keyword entry, which is a prerequisite for the SV branch to learn "speaker discrimination within words."

**Evaluation Data**. Three datasets correspond to the three task scenarios: Google Speech Commands v1 (G) [32] is used for the C-KWS closed-set task; LibriPhrase-easy ($LP_E$) and LibriPhrase-hard ($LP_H$) [12] are used for OV-KWS, forming the standard protocol for open-vocabulary evaluation from easy to hard; the PCOV-KWS task generates LibriPhrase-PCOV ($LP_P$) on LibriSpeech [33]—positive sample pairs are defined as samples that are "both the target keyword and the target speaker," while all others constitute negative sample pairs. The "hardness" of $LP_H$ relative to $LP_E$ is reflected in baseline performance: in Table III, CMCD’s EER worsens from 10.48% on $LP_E$ to 29.34% on $LP_H$, indicating that the hard set poses a significantly stricter test for phonetic confusion. The number of classes and scale of the GSC v1 dataset itself are not reported in the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The primary metrics are Equal Error Rate (EER, the error rate when False Acceptance Rate FAR equals False Rejection Rate FRR) and Area Under the Curve (AUC). The rationale: OV-KWS and PCOV-KWS are essentially problems of embedding similarity and threshold decision. In actual deployment, different operating points will be selected between false wake-ups and missed wake-ups depending on the scenario; single-point accuracy cannot characterize full-threshold behavior. EER provides the error level at the symmetric operating point, and AUC summarizes discriminative power across all thresholds; together, they provide a complete description of "threshold-independent detection quality." For the C-KWS closed-set multi-class task, Accuracy Acc is used (Table IV), consistent with public baseline conventions for this task, allowing direct comparison.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**OV-KWS Task (Table III, $LP_E$ / $LP_H$, higher AUC is better, lower EER is better)**. Baselines are taken from [12][14][34], and the evaluation sets are generated using the exact same construction method to ensure comparability. Results: CMCD (653k parameters) AUC 95.63 / 77.60, EER 10.48 / 29.34; CLAD (parameters not reported) AUC 97.03 / 76.15, EER 8.65 / 30.30; PhonMatchNet (655k) AUC 99.29 / 88.52, EER 2.80 / 18.82. This paper’s PCOV-KWS-S (211k) AUC 99.77 / 88.50, EER 1.85 / 19.33; PCOV-KWS-M (376k) AUC 99.88 / 89.46, EER 1.32 / 18.38. There are three layers to interpreting this: First, the small model PCOV-KWS-S is roughly on par with PhonMatchNet on $LP_H$ (AUC 88.50 vs. 88.52, EER 19.33 vs. 18.82 slightly worse), but with only about one-third of the parameters. Second, the medium model PCOV-KWS-M leads comprehensively on all tasks ($LP_H$ EER 18.38 vs. 18.82). Third, on $LP_E$, the EERs of both models (1.85 / 1.32) are significantly better than the three baselines (best 2.80), indicating that under conventional open-vocabulary conditions, this paper’s method has a larger discrimination margin.

**C-KWS Task (Table IV, GSC v1, zero-shot capability comparison)**. Baselines are taken from [14][27][35]–[40]. Full-sample trained baselines: Att-RNN 95.6% (202k, 22.3M FLOPs), ResNet-15 95.8% (238k, 894M), TENet12 96.6% (100k, 2.9M), TC-ResNet 96.6% (305k, 6.7M), MHAtt-RNN 97.2% (743k, 22.7M), MatchBoxNet 97.5% (93k, 11.3M), BC-ResNet8 98.0% (312k, 89.1M); PhonMatchNet, also zero-shot, achieves 96.8% (655k, FLOPs not reported). This paper’s PCOV-KWS-S zero-shot achieves 96.6% (211k, 1.13M FLOPs), and PCOV-KWS-M achieves 96.9% (376k, 1.8M). Three conclusions: First, zero-shot models surpass several full-sample models (e.g., ResNet-15’s 95.8%, matching TENet12 and TC-ResNet’s 96.6%, surpassing Att-RNN), and are close to SOTA (BC-ResNet8’s 98.0%) with a gap of only 1.1–1.4 percentage points. Second, among zero-shot models, PCOV-KWS-M’s 96.9% is better than PhonMatchNet’s 96.8%, with parameters being only about 57% of the latter. Third, and most indicative of lightweighting—the computational cost of 1.13M FLOPs is only about one-tenth of MatchBoxNet’s (11.3M) and about 1/79 of BC-ResNet8’s (89.1M), indicating that open-vocabulary capability does not come at the cost of computational overhead.

**Keyword Length Analysis (Fig. 3)**. The paper evaluates on the LibriPhrase dataset grouped by keyword length (1 to 4 words). The legend includes four curves: CTC, Attention, CMCD, and this paper’s method, showing the change in AUC and EER with word count. The conclusion is that this paper’s method maintains stable and strong detection performance regardless of keyword length, without significant degradation as length increases (numerical values per word count are not given in table form in the paper). This robustness is important for practical use: user-customized wake-up words are often not just single words but include short and long phrase commands.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

**Finding 1: Personalization capability must rely on an explicit SV branch (Table II)**. The Vanilla (pure keyword model) performs close to random guessing on the PCOV-KWS task: TC-ResNet14-1.5 backbone AUC is only 51.01%, EER 46.52%; TDResNeXt backbone AUC 58.63%, EER 44.02%—an AUC near 50% is random level, proving that an embedding space learned only for keywords contains absolutely no speaker information. After adding SV multi-task (PCOV-KWS row), the PCOV-KWS AUC for the TC-ResNet14-1.5 backbone jumps from 51.01% to 98.32% (EER drops from 46.52% to 6.12%), and the TDResNeXt backbone reaches 99.34% / 3.85%. Meanwhile, an interesting side effect: multi-task training also improves OV-KWS itself—on the TC-ResNet backbone, OV-KWS AUC rises from 98.94% to 99.16% (EER drops from 4.83% to 4.12%), and on the TDResNeXt backbone, AUC rises from 99.77% to 99.85% (EER drops from 1.85% to 1.56%). Speaker supervision acts as auxiliary task regularization.

**Finding 2: Shared encoder saves parameters without performance loss compared to dual networks (Table II, rows 4 and 5)**. Vanilla+SV (training a separate TDResNeXt for KWS and SV, then using them separately) has 396k parameters, SV EER 4.09%, OV-KWS EER 1.85%, PCOV-KWS EER 4.25%; whereas the multi-task shared version (w/o CIB) requires only 211k parameters, with the three EERs being 4.16%, 1.56%, and 3.98% respectively—saving nearly half the parameters, while OV-KWS and PCOV-KWS are actually better, and SV is basically on par. This is direct evidence of the dividends of hard parameter sharing.

**Finding 3: Grid search for CIB is superior to manual fixed values (Table II, rows 5 and 6)**. When manually fixing $\alpha$ (PCOV takes 0.5, OV-KWS takes 1, SV takes 0), SV EER is 4.16% and PCOV-KWS EER is 3.98%; after CIB grid search, these two optimize to 3.85% and 3.85%, and PCOV-KWS AUC also rises from 99.14% to 99.34%.

**Finding 4: The 17-step evolution chain of the TDResNeXt encoder (Table I, MSWC Accuracy / FLOPs, means of five trials with standard deviation)**. Looking at the motivation and benefit of each step: Baseline TC-ResNet14-1.5 is 78.21%(0.04) / 6.86M. Changing stage ratios to {1,1,3,1} yields 78.25%(0.09) / 5.54M—redistributing stage widths first saves about 19% computation without dropping accuracy; switching to search results from NoisyDARTS (AutoKWS [28]) yields 79.48%(0.12) / 4.63M; ConvNeXt-style "patchify" stem yields 79.36%(0.07) / 4.65M; introducing temporal depthwise separable convolution (temporal dsconv) yields 78.11%(0.07) / 1.61M—one step cuts FLOPs to less than a quarter, with accuracy dropping by less than 0.4 points, representing the largest computational gain; inverting dimensions yields 81.45%(0.05) / 7.87M—accuracy surges but computation rises, indicating that channel layout is sensitive to accuracy; moving dsconv up yields 82.18%(0.09) / 0.92M—regaining the current optimal accuracy-computation balance point; scanning kernel sizes from default to 5/7/9/11 yields 81.91%(0.14) / 0.91M, 82.09%(0.06) / 0.92M, 82.01%(0.06) / 0.94M, 81.94%(0.06) / 0.95M respectively—7 is best, and the 5–11 interval is flat, indicating that kernel size is not a sensitive direction; changing activation function from ReLU to GELU yields 82.39%(0.09); reducing number of activation layers yields 83.07%(0.12); reducing number of normalization layers yields 83.32%(0.08); replacing BN with LN yields 83.39%(0.09)—these three steps are all part of ConvNeXt’s "less is more" recipe, collectively gaining about 1.3 points; adding GRN module (ConvNeXt V2) yields 85.43%(0.05)—the single step with the largest improvement (+2.04 points); finally, separating depthwise separable conv (separate d.s. conv, i.e., TDResNeXt formation) yields 85.87%(0.07) / 1.13M. The significance of this entire chain is to prove: modernizing KWS encoders does not require starting from scratch. Systematically transplanting engineering experiences from 2020s visual convolutional networks (flat large kernels, GELU, fewer activations and normalizations, LN, GRN, grouped convolutions) onto a temporal convolution skeleton yields both accuracy and efficiency.

**Finding 5: Two sweet spots for training hyperparameters (Fig. 2)**. The distribution adjustment intensity $t$ in Eq. 2 is optimal at $t=5$ (Fig. 2a); the positive/negative sample weight $\lambda$ in Eqs. 3–4 is optimal at $\lambda = 0.7$ (Fig. 2b), with weighting biased towards positive samples aligning with the intuition that "positive pair distributions have larger variance and need to be tightened more." The gain of PCGrad over EW is seen in Fig. 2c/2d, with PCGrad’s FNR-FPR curves being superior on both tasks.

## Main Contributions

The paper’s contributions can be summarized in four points. First, proposing the PCOV-KWS multi-task learning framework, which (within the scope of this paper’s comparisons) unifies the open-vocabulary customization capability of OV-KWS and the speaker discrimination capability of SV into a single lightweight network for the first time, filling the gap where "existing KWS+SV multi-task frameworks cannot detect arbitrarily user-defined keywords while distinguishing the target user." Second, introducing SphereFace2 binary metric learning in the training criterion, rewriting multi-class classification into K independent binary classifications to eliminate inter-class competition, making zero-shot detection of open-vocabulary keywords possible, and solving dual-task gradient conflicts with conflict-aware loss weighting (PCGrad plus weight adaptation). Third, proposing the CIB Confidence Integration Block, allowing the same model to serve three inference modes—OV-KWS, SV, and personalized KWS—by switching the fusion coefficient $\alpha$. Fourth, constructing the TDResNeXt encoder using NAS search plus a ConvNeXt modernization recipe. With Table I’s 17-step ablation as evidence, it raises accuracy from 78.21% to 85.87% while compressing FLOPs from 6.86M to 1.13M on MSWC; the final system, with 211k/376k parameters and 1.13M/1.8M FLOPs, surpasses baselines with larger parameters on most metrics (Table III, Table IV).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, phonetically hard negative samples are not completely resolved. On $LP_H$, PCOV-KWS-S’s EER (19.33%) is still slightly worse than PhonMatchNet’s (18.82%, Table III); only the parameter-increased PCOV-KWS-M achieves a reversal. The weakness pointed out in the introduction—"cross-modal text registration is difficult to handle phonetically similar words"—is only indirectly countered by the voiceprint threshold in this paper, without specific experiments or quantitative analysis on phonetically confusable word pairs. Second, CIB’s $\alpha$ relies on validation set grid search; changing datasets or deployment conditions requires re-tuning, and the expressive power of linear interpolation fusion is limited, unable to express interactive relationships between the two confidences (e.g., whether a very low voiceprint score should result in a veto). Third, the SV branch is text-dependent in style—$LP_P$’s positive pairs require "same word, same speaker," which is semantically consistent with real personalized wake-up, but the model is not validated on general text-independent speaker verification benchmarks, leaving the generalization ability of the voiceprint embedding unknown. Fourth, lightweighting evidence only reaches the FLOPs level; the paper does not report any measured latency, running memory, parameter storage occupancy, or streaming frame-level inference schemes, making it impossible to judge how much of the theoretical advantage of 1.13M FLOPs can be realized on real MCU/DSPs. Fifth, the details of SphereFace2’s margin scheduler (labeled in Fig. 1) and the specific construction method of new keyword registration prototypes at inference (how many audio samples to register, how to aggregate them into prototypes) are not expanded in the main text; reproduction requires tracing back to literature [24].

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

Training and main evaluation all fall on English (MSWC English subset for training, LibriSpeech series for evaluation), while MSWC itself covers 50 languages; multi-lingual and cross-lingual generalization are completely unverified. $LP_P$ is a synthetic protocol constructed from LibriSpeech, lacking closed-loop registration-usage experiments on real devices: real variables such as noise in the registration environment, far-field reverberation, and voiceprint drift due to months-long intervals between registration and usage are not investigated, nor is the relationship between the number of registration samples and performance reported. There is no direct numerical comparison with the most relevant previous KWS+SV multi-task works [21][22][23]—Table II only provides its own Vanilla+SV (396k) as a control for "two independent networks," lacking comparison with existing joint models under the same protocol. The argument that "previous works cannot freely customize keywords" remains at a qualitative level. The length analysis in Fig. 3 provides only curves without numerical tables, and does not cover longer phrases of more than 4 words. Regarding hyperparameter sensitivity, $t$ and $\lambda$ only have curve observations in Fig. 2a/2b, without variance and significance analysis (although Table I–IV report standard deviations for five trials, statistical tests are not performed for baseline comparisons). Additionally, Google Speech Commands v1 is already an older benchmark; it has not been re-verified on v2 or more recent multi-lingual open-vocabulary benchmarks.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the gaps in the paper, natural directions for advancement include: extending training to MSWC’s multi-lingual subsets or even cross-language mixed training to verify the language-agnostic nature of open-vocabulary capabilities and speaker discrimination; performing hard negative sample mining or contrastive learning enhancement on phonetically confusable protocols like $LP_H$ to positively address the false-trigger weakness of the text-registration route; replacing CIB’s linear interpolation with learnable gating or attention fusion, and studying the impact of non-linear interactions such as "voiceprint veto" on security; completing the edge-side deployment evidence chain—streaming implementation, real latency, memory occupancy, quantization compatibility, and regression testing on real far-field registration-usage data; plotting registration sample count-performance curves to provide actionable registration cost recommendations for deployment; and conducting systematic comparisons with previous KWS+SV multi-task works and subsequent personalized KWS works under a unified protocol to clarify the true gain boundaries of the "open-vocabulary + voiceprint" route relative to pure multi-task schemes.
