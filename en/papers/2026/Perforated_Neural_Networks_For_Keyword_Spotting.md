## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Edge machine learning faces a set of constraints that are not encountered in large-scale cloud deployments: strict memory budgets, limited compute power, and non-negotiable accuracy thresholds, all of which must be satisfied simultaneously (Abstract). If a model is too large, it cannot run on the target hardware regardless of its accuracy; if it is too small, even if it fits in memory, it may fail to meet the minimum accuracy line required for reliable product operation (Section 1). Keyword Spotting (KWS) is one of the most typical workloads under such multiple constraints: always-on voice interfaces, smart home devices, and industrial monitoring systems require models to reliably detect a small vocabulary of trigger words under real-world noise conditions while running continuously on battery-powered devices (Section 1, citations [26,30]). Therefore, any technology claiming to improve both accuracy and model size finds KWS to be its natural touchstone.

This paper has a deeper motivation stemming from modeling itself, rather than just from the engineering perspective of compression. The artificial neuron as a computational unit has remained largely unchanged since the mathematical formalization by McCulloch and Pitts in 1943 [18] and Rosenblatt’s perceptron in 1958 [22]: presynaptic neurons connect directly to the cell body, and a scalar connection weight is the only parameter in this model. This reflects the neuroscientific understanding of 1943, not modern understanding (Section 2.1, citation [28]). After the proposal of backpropagation by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams in 1986 [23], activation functions, optimizers, learning rate schedulers, and large-scale architectures have undergone drastic evolution, but the core neuron unit and the gradient descent learning rule itself have never been touched (Section 1). Meanwhile, modern neuroscience has clearly confirmed that biological neurons are computationally much richer than their artificial counterparts, with the most prominent example being the active, nonlinear computation performed by dendrites [17]. The paper’s basic stance can thus be summarized in one sentence: upgrading the single-weight neuron of the 1943 version to a version with dendritic computation is itself a long-ignored source of performance, and this source is independent of architecture design and compression techniques.

From the neuroscience perspective, biological dendrites are not merely conduits that passively transmit presynaptic activation to the soma; they perform complex nonlinear computations as signals propagate along dendritic branches (Section 2.1). The specific mechanism is as follows: dendritic branches can generate localized, NMDA receptor-mediated spikes. NMDA receptors are the primary excitatory neurotransmitter receptors in the brain, and their current-voltage relationship exhibits a characteristic N-shaped curve dependent on glutamate concentration: under moderate glutamate concentrations, an unstable voltage threshold appears, and once the membrane potential crosses this threshold, it rapidly depolarizes to a high-voltage stable state (Section 2.1, citation [16]). This constitutes a thresholded, all-or-nothing response, and the threshold voltage itself varies with glutamate concentration. As a result, a single dendritic branch can implement nonlinear coincidence detection and feature gating, giving a single biological neuron the computational power that traditional artificial neurons would need multiple layers to replicate. Some researchers even argue that dendrites, rather than the entire neuron, should be considered the basic functional unit of the nervous system [2]. This viewpoint directly constitutes the motivation for adding dendritic modules to artificial networks.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper outlines gaps in existing work along three lines.

First, mainstream compression and optimization techniques can only trade off between resources and rarely improve both accuracy and model size simultaneously (Abstract). Pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation, and neural architecture search are widely used to reduce the model size for edge deployment [10]; MobileNet [11] and its successors are paradigmatic architectures designed from scratch for resource-constrained targets. However, the common pattern of this entire class of methods is "accepting an accuracy penalty in exchange for compression" (Section 3, original text: *accepting an accuracy penalty in exchange for compression*)—engineers can only select a point on a fixed accuracy-size trade-off curve, and no technology promises to shift the entire curve to the upper left.

Second, existing dendritic machine learning models have not fulfilled their potential. The idea of introducing dendritic structures into artificial neurons dates back at least to the morphological perceptron in 2003 [21]; early morphological perceptron work demonstrated that dendritic structures could perfectly classify any training dataset via hypercube partitioning [25]; a hardware-oriented dendritic architecture demonstrated high-energy-efficiency inference in *Nature Nanotechnology* [15]. However, a 2021 review [6] concluded that none of the surveyed dendritic models achieved state-of-the-art accuracy at the time, although they consistently outperformed traditional networks under equal parameter counts—indicating that dendritic structures do carry effective signals, but existing implementations failed to convert these signals into optimal results. Furthermore, these methods are replacement architectures in engineering form: they require modifying model definitions, training hyperparameters, or datasets, which is costly; only a few have introduced dendritic nodes into multi-layer architectures; and none have configured different learning rules for neuron nodes and dendritic nodes (Section 3).

Third, from a workflow perspective, edge KWS engineers have already completed training, evaluation, and deployment to embedded targets on hosted platforms like Edge Impulse [7], where standard NN blocks can export CNNs trained on audio, sensor, and image data to a wide variety of MCUs and SoCs (Section 4.1). Any new method that requires changing the toolchain, data format, or rewriting model code is unlikely to be truly adopted by engineering practice. Therefore, the paper must demonstrate that the dendritic method can enter the real deployment pipeline in a "plugin" form and outperform there, rather than being effective only in offline script environments.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Synthesizing the above background, the challenges to be solved by the paper can be broken down into three layers:

1.  **Mechanism Layer:** How to graft dendritic computation onto standard convolutional networks without replacing existing architectures—i.e., how to define the learning objective and learning rule for dendritic nodes so that they specifically fit the residual errors of the main network, without disrupting the main network’s own gradient descent process. This is essentially a question of "how two learning rules can coexist in one network."
2.  **Engineering Layer:** How to implement the above mechanism within the Edge Impulse KWS tutorial pipeline (MFCC feature input + configurable CNN + fully connected head) while maintaining full compatibility from training and evaluation to export deployment, ensuring that dendritic models can still land on any MCU/SoC.
3.  **Evidence Layer:** How to prove that the gain is not a lucky draw of hyperparameters, but a universal dominance across the entire accuracy-parameter trade-off surface. This is particularly critical for KWS, because its performance requirements are essentially binary: a model either fits on the target hardware or it doesn’t, or it meets the accuracy threshold or it doesn’t (Section 5.4)—engineers constrained by any one dimension should benefit from this method, which requires the advantage to hold simultaneously at all intersections of the two dimensions.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The base network is not redesigned but directly adopts the standard Neural Network Impulse Block from the Edge Impulse platform: a configurable convolutional neural network plus a fully connected classification head, trainable on audio, sensor, and image data, and exportable to a large number of MCUs and SoCs (Section 4.1). The keyword detection tutorial pipeline uses MFCC features extracted from short audio windows as network input, with the goal of detecting a small vocabulary of trigger words (Section 4.1). Choosing this "not starting from scratch" starting point is a deliberate design: the paper’s core claim is that PB belongs to an enhancement that can be applied to any existing pipeline, and using the platform’s native baseline as a control is the cleanest way to test this claim, while also excluding confounding variables brought by "manually designing networks specifically for dendrites."

On top of this, the authors implemented a custom Dendritic NN Impulse Block (Section 4.2): it extends the existing NN block, retaining full compatibility with Edge Impulse training and deployment pipelines, while introducing the alternating neuron/dendrite training schedule described in Section 2; users can access all configuration items provided by the original block, with dendritic options provided as new items side-by-side. All model code is open-sourced in the Perforated AI GitHub repository (Section 4.2, citation [20]). The triple motivation for this design is: (1) experimental fairness—the dendritic models and traditional models follow the exact same training and export paths, so performance differences can only be attributed to the dendritic mechanism itself; (2) reproducibility and generalizability—the community can directly try this method in real product workflows, and the paper’s conclusions can thus be independently verified; (3) scan feasibility—only by exposing dendritic hyperparameters as block-level configuration items can hundreds of automated scans be performed using external tools.

There is an easily overlooked but crucial detail in the deployment form: dendritic nodes are frozen and merged into the forward propagation at the end of training (Section 2.3), so the final deployed model is still a standard forward computation graph, and the deployment side does not require any special runtime or dedicated operator support—this is the technical premise for the claim that it "can be exported to any MCU/SoC," and it also means that the dendritic method is naturally compatible with the platform’s deployment backend (quantization, C++ export, etc.).

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

When standard backpropagation [23] passes through a multi-layer perceptron, the error term for neuron $j$ is (Section 2.3, Equation (1)):

$$\Delta_j = g'(in_j) \sum_i W_{i,j} \Delta_i$$

where $g'(in_j)$ is the derivative of the activation function applied to the total input of neuron $j$, and $W_{i,j}$ is the connection weight from postsynaptic node $i$ to presynaptic node $j$ (according to the paper’s notation direction). PB modifies this equation by splitting the postsynaptic nodes into two categories: neuron nodes (subscript $i$) and dendritic nodes (subscript $k$), and explicitly setting the error term of dendritic nodes to zero during backpropagation (Equation (2)):

$$\Delta_j = g'(in_j) \left( \sum_i W_{i,j} \Delta_i + 0 \cdot \sum_k W_{k,j} \Delta_k \right)$$

The term "perforation" comes from this: the backpropagation graph is perforated, and gradient information cannot pass back through dendritic nodes; dendritic nodes are kept outside the gradient descent graph architecturally—this property forms a clear analogy with the role played by biological dendrites outside of soma action potential generation (Section 2.3).

The error $\Delta_k$ of dendritic nodes does not go through backpropagation but is calculated separately using a modified version of the Cascade Correlation rule. Within each mini-batch (Equation (3)):

$$\Delta_k = \left( g(in_k) - \bar{g}(in_k) \right) \left( \Delta_i - \bar{\Delta}_i \right)$$

where $g(in_k)$ is the activation value of the dendritic node, $\bar{g}(in_k)$ is the sliding average of this activation value, and $\bar{\Delta}_i$ is the sliding average of the error of the associated neuron node. This expression is essentially a mean-centered correlation term: only when the output fluctuations of the dendritic node and the error fluctuations of the associated neuron are systematically aligned or anti-aligned statistically will the product remain persistently non-zero; random uncorrelated fluctuations are canceled out by the sliding average structure. The weight update rule for the input connections of dendritic nodes is (Equation (4)):

$$\frac{\delta \Delta_k}{\delta w_j} = \sigma \left( \Delta_i - \bar{\Delta}_i \right) g'(in_k)$$

where $\sigma$ is the sign function of the average correlation between the dendritic node output and the error of the associated neuron. In other words, the update direction of dendritic weights is determined by the global sign of "whether this node should be positively or negatively correlated with the residual," and the update magnitude is gated by the local activation derivative of the dendritic node itself.

The training process alternates between two phases (Section 2.3). Neuron phase: the entire network is trained with standard gradient descent until validation performance plateaus. Dendrite phase: all neuron weights are frozen, dendritic nodes are added to each neuron, and training proceeds using Equations (3) and (4) until correlation plateaus; then, for each neuron, only the best-performing dendritic node is retained, frozen, and merged into the forward propagation; subsequently, the neuron phase resumes training. This cycle repeats continuously until adding more dendritic nodes no longer improves validation performance.

Why must this dual-rule design be adopted, rather than letting dendritic nodes perform gradient descent together with the main network? The reason given by the paper is architectural: if error gradients pass through dendritic nodes, they degenerate into ordinary additional layers, losing their functional division of labor to "specifically fit residuals," and their error signals would contaminate each other with the main network; keeping dendrites outside the gradient graph preserves the correspondence with biological dendrites and also allows PB to be implemented as a PyTorch wrapper that makes minimal changes to existing model code (Section 2.3, citation [19]). From an optimization perspective, there is also an implicit benefit: the optimal solution of the main network is not disturbed by the gradients of dendritic nodes, and the two sets of parameters are updated in a time-shifted manner, with alternating training naturally possessing the nature of coordinate descent.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Generalizing Cascade Correlation to "Per-Neuron Residual Targets"

The original form of Cascade Correlation [9] was designed for single-layer perceptron networks: candidate nodes are trained to maximize correlation with the network’s output error while existing weights are frozen; the optimal candidate is then frozen and added to the network, and output nodes resume standard learning with the new node as an additional input; subsequent cascaded nodes also receive inputs from all previously cascaded nodes (Section 2.2). The key generalization of PB is: replacing the network-level target of "perceptron output error" with the backpropagation error gradient of every neuron in the deep network (Section 2.2). Why is this step the hub of the entire method: every neuron in a deep network has its own residual; hanging dendrites per neuron is equivalent to equipping every computational unit in the network with a dedicated error corrector, rather than just adding a global correction node at the output end; this is also the reason why PB can be applied as a plugin to any deep architecture (linear layers, convolutional layers) without rewriting the architecture.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Perforated Alternating Training Schedule and Frozen Deployment Form

The schedule design of "first train the main network to convergence, then perforate and add dendrites, freeze the best one dendrite per neuron, resume main network training, and loop until validation performance saturates" can be understood from three aspects. First, the main network must first form stable representations before dendritic nodes have stable residuals to fit; if trained jointly from scratch, the residuals themselves drift drastically, and correlation learning based on sliding averages cannot converge. Second, retaining only the best one dendrite per neuron and freezing it in each round controls the speed of volume expansion in the deployed model, and also allows the screening of "optimal" to be completed on the validation set, consistent with model selection discipline. Third, the termination condition of the loop is bound to validation performance rather than a fixed number of epochs, avoiding unbounded node increases that lead to loss of control over deployment volume. Coupled with the frozen form of "dendrites merged into the forward graph," the entire mechanism behaves on the deployment side as an ordinary, standard-toolchain-exportable forward network.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Platform-Level Open-Source Block Dendritic NN Impulse Block

Encapsulating the entire training mechanism as a custom block within the Edge Impulse ecosystem and open-sourcing it (Section 4.2 [20]) makes dendritic hyperparameters regular scannable configurations alongside dropout and learning rate. This seemingly engineering-oriented contribution is actually the direct prerequisite for the paper to complete 800 hyperparameter scans and win the Best Model award at the Edge Impulse 2025 Hackathon (December 2025) (Abstract, citation [8]): without platform-level integration, there would be no fair control and large-scale scanning.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

-   Compared to the dendritic model lineage (morphological perceptrons [21,25], hardware dendritic architectures [15], models surveyed in the 2021 review [6]): PB is not a replacement architecture but a plugin wrapped around existing deep architectures, requiring no modification of model definitions, training hyperparameters, or datasets; it is one of the few methods to introduce dendritic nodes into multi-layer architectures; and it is the only method to configure separate learning rules for neuron nodes and dendritic nodes (Section 3).
-   Compared to the compression lineage (pruning, quantization, distillation, NAS, MobileNet [10,11]): these techniques select points on a fixed accuracy-size frontier, whereas the difference with PB is that it can move the frontier itself—either exchanging fixed volume for higher accuracy, or fixed accuracy for smaller volume, depending on the integration choice with the original architecture (Section 3). The paper also implies that the two types of methods are orthogonal and can be used in combination, but this paper does not perform combined experiments.
-   Compared to native Cascade Correlation: the training target changes from global output error to per-neuron error gradients; the training flow changes from one-time recursive cascading to a multi-round loop alternating with gradient descent; nodes change from cascaded interconnection to local structures around each neuron.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The experimental data comes from the data used in the Edge Impulse keyword detection tutorial pipeline (Section 4.1), with the original data divided into training, test, and validation subsets (Section 4.3). The input features are explicitly MFCC coefficients extracted from short audio windows (Section 4.1). However, all key specifications regarding the data itself—vocabulary size, number of classes, number of samples, total duration, audio sampling rate, MFCC dimensions, frame length and shift, and noise conditions—are not reported in the paper. Therefore, its absolute accuracy cannot be directly compared with published numbers on public benchmarks such as Speech Commands [26]; this must be kept in mind when evaluating the external validity of the conclusions.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper uses only two metrics: test accuracy (and the error rate derived from it, e.g., the baseline accuracy of 0.921 in Table 1 corresponds to an error rate of 0.079) and the total number of parameters in the final deployed model (the definition of the x-axis in the scatter plot in Section 5.2). The rationale is clearly discussed in Sections 5.3 and 5.4: for edge deployment, the most important KPIs are model size and accuracy; and the performance requirements of KWS are essentially binary—a model either fits on the target hardware or it doesn’t, or it meets the accuracy threshold or it doesn’t—so dominating at all intersections formed by these two dimensions means practical value for any engineer constrained by one dimension. Furthermore, the paper executes strict model selection discipline: test scores are recorded at the epoch with the highest validation performance, not the epoch where the test peak occurs (Section 4.3), consistent with previous PB evaluation practices—this is to avoid optimistic bias caused by using the test set to select early stopping timing, which is experimental hygiene that should be commended.

Metrics not reported: inference latency, RAM/Flash usage, MACs or FLOPs per inference, false wake-up rate and miss rate, DET/ROC curves, variance under multiple random seeds, and any statistical significance tests. For a paper highlighting edge deployment, the lack of deployment-side hardware metrics is a clear gap (see Limitations section).

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Baseline (Section 5.1): The Edge Impulse standard KWS model achieves a test accuracy of 0.921 with 3,859 parameters.

Panoramic Scan (Section 5.2, Figure 1): All 800 trials are plotted on a plane of "total parameters (logarithmic x-axis, covering 100 to 2,000,000) × test accuracy"; blue points are traditional (non-dendritic) models, pink points are GD dendritic models, and yellow points are CC dendritic models; Figure 1 shows a focused view with accuracy higher than 0.920. Two global patterns are clearly visible (Section 5.2): first, on each horizontal accuracy threshold line, the leftmost point (reaching that accuracy with the fewest parameters) is a dendritic model; second, on each vertical parameter budget line, the point with the highest accuracy is also a dendritic model.

Key Model Comparisons (Section 5.3 and Table 1):

-   The smallest model exceeding baseline accuracy is a dendritic model, with approximately 1,556 parameters and a test accuracy of 0.933: a 16% reduction in error rate relative to the baseline (from 0.079 to 0.067), and a 60% reduction in parameters (from 3,859 to 1,556). The "1,500 parameters vs. ~4,000 parameters" in the abstract is a rounded expression of these numbers.
-   The model with the highest accuracy in the entire field is also a dendritic model: test accuracy 0.958, 11,421 parameters; the traditional model with the closest parameter count has 10,595 parameters, with an error rate 29% higher than it (Section 5.3; corresponding to a dendritic model error rate of 0.042 in Table 1).
-   The "optimal change" row in Table 1 gives parameters −60% and error rate −46%. Checking the numbers: −60% corresponds to the smallest model (3,859→1,556), and −46% corresponds to the most accurate model (0.079→0.042, a reduction of approximately 46.8%); that is, this row combines the optimal changes of two different models into one row; the paper text does not explicitly explain this口径 (this judgment is derived directly from Table 1 numbers).
-   Conclusions for two types of practitioners (Section 5.3): For those who prioritize maximum accuracy as the primary KPI, they will find that dendritic models require fewer parameters to reach every accuracy level than any traditional model; for those who prioritize minimum volume as the primary KPI, they will find that dendritic models always have higher accuracy at every volume tier.

Comparison with SOTA methods in the KWS field: Not reported. The entire text lacks any direct numerical comparison with DS-CNN, MatchboxNet, BC-ResNet, or the MLPerf Tiny benchmark [1]; the related work section only cites them as background (Section 3). All quantitative conclusions are internal controls within the same pipeline ("dendritic vs. traditional"). Previous PB works in other fields (Section 3, for reference only, not part of this experiment): drug toxicity prediction (TrimNet on Tox21) reduced average error rate by 13.6%; stock trend prediction (HIST on CSI300) improved by 8.6% on average; ICU mortality prediction (mTAN on PhysioNet); and compression results achieving accuracy parity with less than one-tenth of the parameters; subsequent research [4] further reported lossless compression of up to 90%, accuracy improvements of up to 16%, and inference cost reductions of up to 38x on GPU cloud hardware.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have traditional item-by-item ablation tables; the 800 hyperparameter scans (Section 4.3) themselves serve the role of ablation. The scanned dimensions include four groups:

-   Network architecture: total number of convolutional layers, total number of linear layers, network width, width growth pattern (uniform growth across layers such as [8,8,8,8], or doubling growth such as [8,16,32,64]);
-   Regularization and training: dropout rate, Gaussian noise standard deviation, learning rate, early stopping patience;
-   Dendritic-specific parameters: maximum number of dendrites per neuron, dendrite switching threshold, dendrite initialization magnitude, dendrite forward function, which modules to perforate (linear layers only vs. linear layers plus convolutional layers);
-   Model format: traditional neural network, GD dendritic (dendritic weights trained with standard gradient descent), CC dendritic (trained with modified cascade correlation).

The scanned architecture sizes range from far smaller than the baseline parameter count to significantly larger (Section 4.3). Three ablation-level findings can be extracted from this. First, format ablation: traditional, GD, and CC formats compete in the same field, and the entire best positions are occupied by dendritic models; however, the paper does not report the group statistical differences between the GD and CC dendritic variants (which is better, and in which parameter segment). Second, perforation location ablation: the two schemes of linear layers only and linear plus convolutional layers exist as scanning dimensions, but their independent conclusions are also not reported by the paper. Third, and the one most emphasized by the authors: universality itself is the core finding (Section 5.4)—the dendritic advantage holds across all parameter magnitudes, which is more persuasive than finding a single high-performance configuration, because it shows that the dendritic mechanism provides consistent signal improvement, rather than an偶然 effect that only appears under specific architectural conditions; the authors also point out that the KWS field is particularly suitable for demonstrating this universality, because the binary nature of its performance requirements makes "dominating at all intersections of the two axes" have direct engineering implications.

## Main Contributions

1.  First introduction of Perforated Backpropagation and artificial dendritic nodes into audio keyword detection and real edge deployment platforms, winning the Best Model award at the Edge Impulse 2025 Hackathon (December 2025) (Abstract, citation [8]).
2.  Established the evidence form of "full trade-off surface dominance" with 800 hyperparameter scans: dendritic models have the highest accuracy at every parameter budget, and the fewest parameters at every accuracy threshold—this is a dominance conclusion, not a single-point conclusion (Sections 5.2, 5.3).
3.  The best single model simultaneously achieves a 16% reduction in error rate and a 60% reduction in parameters (1,556 parameters, accuracy 0.933, Section 5.3, Table 1), breaking the default exchange relationship of "compression must pay an accuracy tax," proving that accuracy and volume can be improved simultaneously.
4.  Platform engineering contribution: the open-source Dendritic NN Impulse Block [20] allows any Edge Impulse user to directly enable dendritic training on top of all original configuration items, allowing the method to enter real product workflows immediately.
5.  Extended the cross-domain validation record of PB to the audio and edge inference domains; previously, PB has reported gains in chemical graph networks (drug toxicity), financial time series (stock prediction), medical time series (ICU mortality), NLP, protein biology, and image recognition (Sections 5.4, 3); cross-domain consistency strengthens the claim that "dendritic structures capture the universal properties of neural network learning."

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

-   Increased training process complexity: the alternating neuron/dendrite two-phase process plus multiple rounds of "freeze-add node-retrain" cycles until validation performance saturates (Section 2.3) inevitably leads to higher total training time and tuning costs than single-phase training; specific time overhead is not reported in the paper.
-   Expanded hyperparameter space: five categories of dendritic hyperparameters are added to the regular hyperparameters (maximum number of dendrites per neuron, switching threshold, initialization magnitude, forward function, perforation module scope), constituting a new search burden for users encountering this method for the first time; the paper also does not provide recommended values or sensitivity analysis for these hyperparameters.
-   Dendritic nodes widen the forward computation graph: each neuron can hang multiple dendritic nodes, and the additional computational cost, memory access, and potential serialization costs during the inference phase need to be measured with latency and memory data; the paper only reports parameter count as a volume proxy, and actual deployment overhead is not reported.
-   "Retaining only the best one dendrite per neuron per round" is a heuristic rule: whether the discarded sub-optimal dendrites carry useful signals, and what the cost-benefit curve is for retaining multiple dendrites, is not discussed in the paper.
-   Implementation details such as the sliding average window length in Equations (3) and (4) and the dendritic learning rate are not reported in the paper; exact reproduction relies on the open-source code [19,20].

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

-   Insufficient dataset transparency: vocabulary size, number of classes, sample size, noise conditions, and MFCC configuration are all unreported; third parties cannot precisely reproduce the experiments nor align the absolute accuracy values of 0.921/0.933/0.958 with any public benchmark.
-   Lack of direct comparison with domain SOTA methods: no numbers for DS-CNN, MatchboxNet, BC-ResNet, or MLPerf Tiny; all conclusions are pipeline-internal controls. The absolute level of the baseline 0.921 is determined by the Edge Impulse tutorial pipeline and data, and cannot represent competitiveness on public datasets; the correct reading of the conclusion is "dendritic relative improvement on the same architecture," not "dendritic models reach SOTA."
-   Lack of statistical tests: the "dominance at each intersection" conclusion of 800 points lacks significance analysis, and variance under random seeds is not reported; furthermore, Figure 1 only presents a focused view with accuracy higher than 0.920, and conclusions for the full range (100 to 2,000,000 parameters) rely on text descriptions in the main body, preventing readers from independently verifying the dominance relationship at the low-accuracy, large-model end from the figure.
-   Lack of deployment-side evidence: although the paper uses edge deployment as its title scenario, it does not report latency, RAM/Flash usage, or power consumption on real MCU/SoC hardware—these are precisely the direct measures of the constraints of "strict memory budget, limited compute" mentioned in the abstract, and parameter count is only one indirect proxy.
-   Systematic comparison results between the two variants, GD dendritic and CC dendritic, are not reported, preventing readers from evaluating the net contribution of the modified cascade correlation relative to naive gradient descent training of dendrites; similarly missing are the group results for the perforation module scope (linear only vs. linear plus convolutional).
-   The "optimal change" row in Table 1 presents optimal changes from two different models side-by-side (parameters −60% from the smallest model, error rate −46% from the most accurate model) without explaining the口径, which can easily be misread as a single model achieving both numbers simultaneously.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Directions stated by the paper (Section 6): apply to more audio architectures; conduct systematic evaluation on embedded deployment benchmarks; integrate with hardware-aware neural architecture search, allowing NAS to optimize directly in a search space containing dendrites.

Extensions from the reader’s perspective: perform combined experiments with compression techniques such as quantization and pruning to verify the orthogonal claim implied by the paper; reproduce on public datasets (such as Speech Commands [26]) and with larger vocabularies to补齐 external comparability; report the false wake-up/miss wake-up trade-off in streaming always-on scenarios, which is the true threshold metric for KWS productization; provide a theoretical characterization of the residual correlation learning in Equations (3) and (4) to explain why per-neuron fitting of residuals translates into global accuracy improvement; publish group statistics for GD vs. CC and perforation locations in the scanned data to complete the ablation dimensions that have been scanned but not reported; finally, measure the latency and energy consumption of dendritic models relative to traditional models on real MCUs, translating "fewer parameters" into "less deployment cost," which is the last necessary piece of evidence for this direction to move from an award-winning demonstration to an engineering standard.
