# MATE: Matryoshka Audio-Text Embeddings for Open-Vocabulary Keyword Spotting

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Youngmoon Jung, Myunghun Jung, Joon-Young Yang, Yong-Hyeok Lee, Jaeyoung Roh, Hoon-Young Cho (Samsung Research AI Solution Team, Seoul, South Korea)
- **Date**: January 2026 (arXiv:2601.14012v1, submitted 2026-01-20, eess.AS)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14012
- **Keywords**: open-vocabulary keyword spotting, text enrollment, matryoshka embedding, audio-text dual encoder, deep metric learning, PCA-guided prefix alignment, ECAPA-TDNN

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) enables devices to detect specific trigger words within continuous speech streams. Traditional solutions focus on a small set of fixed keywords (examples cited in the paper include "Alexa", "OK Google", "Hi Bixby"). Once trained, the model's vocabulary is locked. Recently, the industry has shifted significantly toward open-vocabulary KWS, allowing users to define arbitrary wake words. Among these, text enrollment has gained particular popularity—users do not need to record demonstration audio for the device but can simply type the desired trigger word. The system determines whether the word is present in the audio by comparing the similarity between audio embeddings and text embeddings. This approach brings two engineering benefits: a lightweight enrollment experience (typing replaces recording) and scalable deployment (new keywords require encoding the text only once, without collecting voice samples).

The technical foundation of text-enrollment KWS is a shared audio-text representation space: acoustic and text encoders are trained via Deep Metric Learning (DML) to pull embeddings of the same keyword closer and push those of different keywords apart, thereby supporting open-set retrieval. The paper identifies two mainstream matching paradigms in this context: (i) utterance-level matching, where audio and text of a whole word or phrase are pooled into single vectors for comparison; and (ii) phoneme-level matching, which compares acoustic and text sequences at a finer temporal granularity using attention, dynamic programming, or CTC-style alignment. This paper selects utterance-level matching as the primary focus for three reasons: it eliminates the need for sequence alignment modules (avoiding complex streaming implementations like dynamic programming or CTC decoding); a single pooled vector per modality suffices for open-set retrieval; and it is more friendly to low-latency edge-side KWS. Phoneme-level extensions are explicitly reserved for future work.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

From the perspective of embedding learning, the paper’s critique of existing utterance-level methods is highly focused: **they all learn embeddings at a single, fixed dimension**. A 256-dimensional vector is just a 256-dimensional vector; the training objective is defined only on this complete dimension, with no mechanism requiring "information to be placed into this vector in order of importance." This leads to two specific defects:

First, there is a lack of multi-granularity representation capability. A single granularity means all discriminative information is unstructuredly spread across the entire vector, making it impossible to answer questions like "how much information remains if only the first 64 dimensions are taken." In contrast, Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) in general representation domains has proven that nested sub-embeddings (prefixes) allow a single vector to serve multiple precision-cost tiers—low-dimensional prefixes carry condensed clues, while high-dimensional prefixes supplement details. MRL-style works such as M-Vec and DSM-GS have appeared in the speaker verification domain, demonstrating the feasibility of this approach on speech vectors. However, the paper explicitly states that there are no prior precedents in KWS ("to our knowledge the first application of matryoshka-style representation learning to KWS").

Second, naive multi-scale supervision suffers from cross-scale interference. Related works (such as ESE in the sentence embedding domain) have observed that directly applying the main loss to all prefixes creates competition between prefixes of different sizes—each prefix is forced to become a fully discriminative vector, and optimization objectives conflict with each other. Conversely, compression-driven feature ordering can stabilize multi-embedding training. The KWS domain has neither verified this phenomenon nor designed a solution for it. Furthermore, in proxy-based DML training, text embeddings play the role of class proxies/centroids for each keyword; how this structural prior is compatible with multi-granularity requirements remains an open question.

Third, even looking solely at the line of utterance-level objective functions, the evolution of prior work reveals another untapped ceiling. Early multi-view acoustic word embeddings relied on triplet loss, where training signals were sparse and sensitive to sample mining. Subsequently, proxy-based objectives became mainstream—asymmetric proxy loss (AsyP) and its variants with adaptive margins and scales (AdaMS) treat each word's text embedding as a learnable class proxy. RPL added cross-modal structural alignment on top of the proxy structure, while ADML used modality adversarial training to reduce cross-modal gaps. This entire evolutionary line answers "how to pull the space further apart," but no work has asked "how is information arranged in this space"—whether discriminative clues are uniformly smeared across the 256 dimensions or layered according to significance. MATE addresses this latter question, and does so in a manner orthogonal to all the aforementioned objective functions, which is why it emphasizes being loss-agnostic.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

The core challenges can be broken down into three: (1) How to introduce matryoshka-style nested sub-embeddings into audio-text dual-encoder KWS **without increasing any inference overhead**—inference still uses only the full-dimensional embeddings for cosine similarity, consistent with prior work; (2) How to supervise these prefixes to avoid cross-scale interference—directly applying the main loss to each prefix (the naive MRL approach) yields limited benefits or even conflicts, requiring a supervision signal that allows low-dimensional prefixes to focus on high-significance clues while high-dimensional prefixes supplement details; (3) The method must be **loss-agnostic**—not bound to a single DML objective function, but capable of stable performance gains across a family of utterance-level objectives such as Proxy-BD, Proxy-MS, CLAT, AsyP, AdaMS, and RPL. Otherwise, it would be merely an adjunct technique for a specific loss rather than a general framework.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

MATE is a dual-encoder framework (Fig. 1). On the acoustic side: 40-dimensional log-Mel filter bank features (25 ms window, 10 ms frame shift, utterance-level mean normalization) are input into a 256-channel ECAPA-TDNN (approx. 1.8M parameters) to output a phoneme-level embedding sequence, which is then aggregated into a 256-dimensional utterance embedding using Channel and Context-dependent Statistics Pooling (CCSP, the native pooling of ECAPA-TDNN). On the text side: a Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) frontend first converts the enrolled text into a phoneme sequence, which passes through a trainable 256-dimensional lookup table embedding, then through 2 layers of bidirectional LSTM (256 hidden units), followed by global average pooling and a linear layer, resulting in a 256-dimensional text embedding. All embeddings are ℓ2-normalized. This encoder configuration is not invented in this paper but follows the standard setup of recent KWS works like RPL—the contribution is deliberately placed at the training objective layer, with the encoder fully inherited, ensuring that ablation studies can cleanly attribute results to the supervision method.

Nested sub-embeddings (prefixes) are defined on top of this: Let the prefix dimension set be $D = \{d_1, \dots, d_K\}$, with $d_1 < \dots < d_K = D$. Following the halving schedule of MRL, $d_k = D \cdot 2^{-(K-k)}$, i.e., $D = \{D/2^{(K-1)}, \dots, D/2, D\}$. The prefix is simply the slice of the first $d_k$ dimensions of the vector: $u^{d_k}_a = u_a[1:d_k]$, $u^{d_k}_t = u_t[1:d_k]$. Experiments fix $D = 256$, with $K$ varying in $\{2, 3, 4, 5\}$; the main comparative experiments use $K = 5$, i.e., $\{16, 32, 64, 128, 256\}$. Inference uses only the cosine similarity of the full-dimensional $u_a$ and $u_t$—this is repeatedly emphasized as the foundation of the "zero inference overhead" promise.

The asymmetry of the pooling operators on both sides is also worth noting: the acoustic side uses CCSP (pooling that statistics first and second moments with channel and context weighting), while the text side uses only global average pooling. The paper does not specifically argue for this asymmetry; it inherits the convention from prior works like RPL. However, its inferable rationality lies in the fact that phoneme sequences on the audio side are affected by speech rate, accent, and noise, with frame-level contributions being highly uneven, requiring attention-based statistical pooling to highlight keyword frames. In contrast, phoneme sequences on the text side are deterministic, short, and regular, with each phoneme contributing roughly uniformly to the word identity; average pooling is sufficient, and adding attention would introduce unnecessary degrees of freedom. The halving prefix schedule also follows MRL conventions rather than being search-derived—the paper did not attempt non-halving dimension combinations; this choice is more of an engineering convention, and the $K$ ablation in Table 3 is also conducted within the halving grid.

In terms of design motivation, the paper provides an **information squeeze** perspective: the shared audio-text space should arrange keyword evidence in order of significance, with low-dimensional prefixes carrying condensed high-significance clues and progressively larger prefixes supplementing fine-grained details. In proxy-based DML, text embeddings serve as representative vectors for each keyword. Therefore, injecting "compressed text information" into the leading dimensions of acoustic embeddings is equivalent to allowing the most important dimensions of the acoustic vector to prioritize absorbing the core discriminative information from the text proxy.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The core of MATE is **PCA-guided prefix alignment**: using the PCA-compressed version of the full-dimensional text embedding as a teacher target to supervise both audio and text prefixes. Mathematically, this involves four steps (Equations 1–4):

Step 1: Corpus-level text intra-dependency matrix. Let $\mu^D_t$ be the mean of full-dimensional text embeddings over the training corpus, and $\bar{u}_t = u_t - \mu^D_t$ be the centered text embedding. Inspired by self-attention mechanisms, an intra-dependency matrix is constructed using scaled dot products followed by row-wise softmax, and estimated once per epoch using the **current text encoder** on the entire training set:

$$\bar{A}^D_t = \mathrm{Softmax}_{row}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} \frac{\bar{u}^{(j)}_t \bar{u}^{(j)\top}_t}{\sqrt{D}}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D} \tag{1}$$

Step 2: Perform SVD on this matrix: $\bar{A}^D_t = U\Sigma V^\top$, where $U, V$ are orthogonal, and $\Sigma$ is diagonal with singular values descending along the diagonal. Step 3: For a target prefix dimension $d_k$ ($1 \le d_k \le D$), take the first $d_k$ principal dependency components as the projection head:

$$A^{d_k}_t = U_{:,1:d_k}\,\Sigma_{1:d_k,1:d_k} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times d_k} \tag{3}$$

Step 4: Apply it to the centered full-dimensional text embedding to obtain the PCA-compressed text sub-embedding (teacher):

$$\tilde{u}^{d_k}_t = \left(A^{d_k}_t\right)^\top \bar{u}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k} \tag{4}$$

The paper explicitly notes that calling it "PCA compression" is metaphorical—it is an SVD projection that acts as the principal direction sorted by variance in practice. Intuitively, $\tilde{u}^{d_k}_t$ collects the $d_k$ most influential dependency directions of $u_t$. The reason for going through "softmax-normalized dependency matrix then SVD" rather than directly performing PCA on the text embedding covariance is clearly stated by the authors: this matrix construction mimics self-attention (scaled dot product + row softmax), capturing the correlation structure between dimensions, and the properties of row-stochastic matrices make the SVD decomposition numerically more stable.

The alignment loss (Equations 5–7) is defined for each $d_k \in D \setminus \{D\}$ (i.e., all nested prefixes except the full dimension). The teacher $\tilde{u}^{d_k}_t$ aligns with two students simultaneously—the audio prefix $u^{d_k}_a$ and the text prefix $u^{d_k}_t$. Let $\varphi_\tau(x) = \mathrm{Softmax}(x/\tau)$ be the temperature-softened distribution. Each alignment term is the sum of MSE and softened KL divergence (the combination motivation is drawn from comparative studies of KL and MSE in knowledge distillation [23]):

$$\mathcal{L}^k_{align,a} = \mathrm{MSE}(u^{d_k}_a, \tilde{u}^{d_k}_t) + \mathrm{KL}\left(\varphi_\tau(u^{d_k}_a)\,\|\,\varphi_\tau(\tilde{u}^{d_k}_t)\right) \tag{5}$$

$$\mathcal{L}^k_{align,t} = \mathrm{MSE}(u^{d_k}_t, \tilde{u}^{d_k}_t) + \mathrm{KL}\left(\varphi_\tau(u^{d_k}_t)\,\|\,\varphi_\tau(\tilde{u}^{d_k}_t)\right) \tag{6}$$

$$\mathcal{L}^k_{align} = \mathcal{L}^k_{align,a} + \mathcal{L}^k_{align,t}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{align} = \sum_{k=1}^{K-1} \mathcal{L}^k_{align} \tag{7-8}$$

The final objective (Equation 9) is the main loss plus a weighted alignment term: $\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{main} + \lambda_{align}\mathcal{L}_{align}$, where $\mathcal{L}_{main}$ is the utterance-level audio-text objective acting on the full-dimensional pair $(u_a, u_t)$ (instantiated as RPL in experiments, but the framework is loss-agnostic). $\lambda_{align}$ adopts a **delayed step schedule** (Equation 10): it is 0 for epochs 1 to 20, and becomes 0.5 starting from epoch 21. The rationale is to let the full-dimensional text embedding first become a reliable proxy/centroid under $\mathcal{L}_{main}$, after which the compressed alignment begins to guide the prefixes—the teacher must stand firm before teaching the students.

Training routine (epoch routine): At the beginning of each epoch, corpus statistics are recalculated using the current text encoder—corpus mean $\mu^D_t$, the dependency matrix of Eq. (1), and the projection heads of Eqs. (2)–(3); during training, the compressed target is generated on-the-fly for each mini-batch using Eq. (4) on the batch's centered text embeddings, and the alignment loss of Eqs. (5)–(7) is computed, weighted by Eq. (10). Implementation details: PyTorch, AdamW (initial learning rate 1e-4, weight decay 1e-5), trained for 100 epochs; each mini-batch contains 250 keywords, 2 utterances per keyword, totaling 500 utterances; alignment temperature $\tau = 1$; training completes in approximately one day on two H100 GPUs. Baseline objective functions (Proxy-BD, Proxy-MS, CLAT, AsyP, AdaMS, RPL) follow their respective original settings unless specified otherwise.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: First Matryoshka Representation in KWS

This introduces the nested prefix structure of MRL into KWS for the first time. The technical innovation lies not in the structure (prefixes are merely vector slices, with zero modifications to the encoder) but in the **division of responsibilities during training**: the main loss is attached only to the full-dimensional pair, while prefixes are entirely supervised by the compressed alignment. This forms an essential distinction from the original MRL approach (where the main loss is directly applied to each prefix). The value lies in: during training, prefixes act as a structured regularizer—forcing the network to arrange discriminative information in order of significance gradient into the leading dimensions of the vector—and this arrangement itself further improves the quality of the full-dimensional embedding (Table 2 shows the full-dimensional AP increased by an average of 2.59 percentage points). That is, the matryoshka structure here is primarily a **training-time information sorting tool**, and secondarily an optional multi-granularity output.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: PCA-Compressed Text Proxy as Prefix Teacher

The selection of the teacher signal is the most delicate part of this method. Why use "PCA compression of the full-dimensional text embedding" instead of something else (e.g., the output of another small encoder, or a proxy trained independently at low dimensions)? The paper's reasoning chain is: (1) PCA compression naturally carries **variance ordering**—the first $d_k$ principal directions are those with the most information, which perfectly matches the "low-dimensional prefixes hold high-significance clues" squeeze semantics; (2) The teacher is derived online from the text encoder during current training (recalculated every epoch), introducing no additional teacher network or two-stage distillation process, keeping the training pipeline single-stage; (3) Experience from related domains (ESE) indicates that compression-driven feature ordering stabilizes multi-embedding training and avoids the competition problem of naive per-prefix supervision. Aligning prefixes on both audio and text sides also serves two clear purposes: it allows acoustic leading coordinates to absorb compressed high-significance clues, and it keeps the compressed text proxy geometrically consistent with the original text prefix of the same dimension—the teacher itself is pulled to prevent deviation.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Compatibility of Loss-Agnosticism and Zero Inference Overhead

At the framework level, $\mathcal{L}_{main}$ is a slot, and RPL is merely the default filler in experiments. Table 2 verifies this point one by one across six utterance-level objectives, with average gains of +2.59 pp, minimum +1.81 pp, and maximum +3.19 pp, all positive. More importantly from an engineering perspective is the zero-overhead promise: the inference path is bit-for-bit identical to prior work (full-dimensional cosine similarity), and all gains occur during training—the deployment side requires no changes to inference code, no re-quantization, and no extra prefix computation. This draws a clear line from methods that trade performance for inference-time ensembling or test-time augmentation.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to **MRL**: MRL applies the main loss to each prefix (per-prefix supervision), while MATE leaves the main loss to the full dimension and entrusts prefixes to the compressed teacher. Table 1 directly compares the consequences of these two philosophies in KWS. Compared to **ESE**: ESE uses a two-stage recipe of learn-to-express and learn-to-compress, while MATE is single-stage end-to-end. Compared to **per-prefix RPL** (an MRL-style variant implemented by the paper itself): Table 1 shows per-prefix RPL yields only +0.83 pp, while MATE yields +2.28 pp, proving that the difference is not "whether there are prefixes" but "how prefixes are supervised." Compared to **KWS baselines like RPL/ADML**: MATE is an orthogonal training technique that can be stacked on top of these objectives (each baseline in Table 2 has a corresponding MATE-enhanced version). Compared to **phoneme-level methods** (CMCD, PhonMatchNet, CTC alignment): MATE requires no sequence alignment module; Table 5 shows that even against these heavier phoneme-level pipelines, utterance-level MATE still dominates comprehensively on LibriPhrase.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

**Training Set**: King-ASR-066 (DataOceanAI), completely following the setup of the RPL paper including the same data augmentation recipe, yielding approximately 4.6k hours of word-level speech segments, covering about 210k (210,000) word classes. This scale is noteworthy: the number of word classes is far larger than typical KWS training sets, representing a standard configuration for open-vocabulary tasks. **Evaluation Set 1**: WSJ (Wall Street Journal), constructed according to the RPL protocol: 3.2k different words, 18k word segments, plus reverberation (RIR) and MUSAN noise pollution; the test set contains 18k positive pairs and negative pairs 50 times the number of positives, simulating the extreme imbalance of the real world. **Evaluation Set 2**: LibriPhrase, using official splits and official metrics, divided into easy (LPE) and hard (LPH) subsets; all systems are trained on King-ASR-066 and do not use the LibriPhrase training set, specifically testing cross-corpus generalization. For fair comparison, all systems use the same acoustic and text encoders as MATE; ADML has its original SphereFace2 auxiliary loss removed (denoted ADML w/o SF2) to isolate the effect of the DML objective itself.

The batch organization is a detail but key profile for understanding such methods: each mini-batch contains 250 keywords, with exactly 2 utterances per keyword. This design serves proxy-based metric learning—there must be enough **different keywords** within the batch to participate in comparison, so that the signal for pushing classes apart is dense; fixing 2 utterances per keyword ensures a minimal pulling signal within the class. The compressed teacher for the alignment target is generated on-the-fly per batch (Eq. (4) acts on the batch's centered text embeddings), so the calculation of the alignment loss does not depend on cross-batch state. In distributed training, two H100s can each compute their own batches, which is one reason the single-stage end-to-end process remains concise.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

**AP (Average Precision)** is reported on WSJ, with the rationale directly written in the protocol design: the test set has 50 times more negatives than positives, making accuracy meaningless under such extreme imbalance; AP is more sensitive to retrieval ranking quality. On LibriPhrase, the official metrics are followed: **EER (Equal Error Rate)** and **AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve)**, reported on LPE/LPH respectively, where lower EER and higher AUC are better. Deployment metrics such as inference latency, model size, and prefix-dimension inference precision are **not reported** in the paper (the encoder parameter count is given only once for the acoustic side, approx. 1.8M).

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**WSJ Main Results (Table 2, K = 5, prefixes {16, 32, 64, 128, 256})**: Comparison of baselines and MATE-enhanced versions across six utterance-level objectives—Proxy-BD 69.76 → 72.95 (+3.19 pp); Proxy-MS 70.18 → 73.12 (+2.94 pp); CLAT 70.52 → 73.53 (+3.01 pp); AsyP 71.66 → 73.98 (+2.32 pp); AdaMS 73.87 → 75.68 (+1.81 pp); RPL 78.66 → 80.94 (+2.28 pp). Average improvement +2.59 pp, relative improvement 2.4%–4.6%. Two points worth circling: the strongest baseline (RPL, itself the predecessor of this series of works) still gains 2.28 pp, indicating this is not a technique that only works on weak baselines; the weakest baseline Proxy-BD gains the most (+3.19 pp), suggesting that the worse the embedding quality, the larger the repair space for information sorting regularization.

**LibriPhrase Cross-Corpus Results (Table 5, MATE uses RPL main loss, K = 3, consistent with the optimal WSJ configuration)**: On EER, MATE achieves 1.38% on LPE and 20.06% on LPH; compared to ADML (w/o SF2) 1.43/21.04, RPL 1.54/22.45, PhonMatchNet 2.33/24.11, CMCD 4.20/25.94. On AUC, MATE achieves 99.86% on LPE and 88.70% on LPH; compared to ADML 99.84/86.91, RPL 99.84/85.31, PhonMatchNet 99.70/83.39, CMCD 99.12/81.14. Relative to the strongest opponent ADML (w/o SF2), MATE further reduces LPE EER by 0.05 pp and LPH EER by 0.98 pp, and improves LPH AUC by +1.79 pp (99.86 vs 99.84, 88.70 vs 86.91). Gains on the hard subset are significantly larger than on the easy subset—generalization improvement under cross-corpus + hard conditions is more convincing than small gains in-distribution, and the trend is consistent with WSJ.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

**Ablation 1: Multi-scale Supervision Strategy (Table 1, four strategies under RPL main loss)**—This is the most informative table in the paper, containing an important **negative result**. RPL (full-dimension only, no prefixes) 78.66%; Per-prefix RPL (main loss applied to each nested prefix, MRL-style) 79.49% (+0.83 pp); Per-prefix RPL + PCA-guided alignment (two supervision signals pressing the same batch of prefixes simultaneously) 78.01%, which not only drops 1.48 pp compared to per-prefix RPL but is even 0.65 pp lower than the no-prefix baseline; MATE (main loss only on full dimension + PCA-guided alignment only for prefixes) 80.94% (+2.28 pp, 1.45 pp higher than per-prefix RPL). The paper's explanation for the negative result: per-prefix RPL requires each prefix to be fully discriminative, while PCA alignment pulls prefixes toward the compression proxy emphasizing variance ordering; the two objectives conflict in direction on the same batch of prefixes, increasing optimization difficulty. MATE's division of labor—full dimension for main loss, prefixes for compression regularization—happens to avoid conflict. This table essentially validates the conjecture from related domains (ESE) that naive per-prefix supervision causes cross-scale competition, and provides the first solution in KWS.

**Ablation 2: Number of Prefixes K (Table 3, RPL main loss, D = 256)**—K = 2 ({128, 256}) 79.02% (+0.36 pp); K = 3 ({64, 128, 256}) 81.03% (+2.37 pp, global optimum); K = 4 ({32, 64, 128, 256}) 79.38% (+0.72 pp); K = 5 ({16, 32, 64, 128, 256}) 80.94% (+2.28 pp). Non-monotonic: K = 4 drops 1.65 pp compared to K = 3, which the paper attributes to the discriminative capacity of the 32-dimension prefix being too small (citing similar observations from the speaker domain DSM-GS); adding another 16-dimension prefix (K = 5) causes AP to rise again, and the authors speculate that this extra coarse-grained prefix disperses the alignment pressure, alleviating over-regularization on the 32-dimension prefix—note that both explanations are labeled as conjectures by the paper and not further verified. The paper also reports that K ≥ 6 yields no additional benefits. All multi-scale configurations outperform the no-prefix baseline, indicating the direction is robust, with the sweet spot at K = 3.

**Ablation 3: Alignment Loss Composition (Table 4, K = 3, τ = 1)**—Pure MSE 79.96%; Pure KL 80.04%; MSE:KL = 1:1 (default) 81.03%, which is 1.07 pp higher than pure MSE and 0.99 pp higher than pure KL. The mechanistic explanation for the complementary nature of the two signals: MSE constrains proximity in Euclidean space (matching of vector values themselves), while softened KL aligns the relative importance of each dimension (distribution structure between dimensions); the combination is more stable than using either alone.

## Main Contributions

1. **First introduction of matryoshka representation learning to KWS**: Achieving multi-granularity embeddings via nested prefixes within the audio-text dual encoder, obtaining multiple embedding granularities in a single forward pass, with inference using only full-dimensional embeddings, bit-for-bit consistent with prior work, and zero inference overhead (emphasized in both abstract and conclusion).
2. **Proposed PCA-guided prefix alignment**: Using the compressed version of the full-dimensional text embedding obtained via SVD of the corpus-level dependency matrix as a teacher, aligning audio and text prefixes simultaneously with MSE + softened KL, combined with a delayed weight schedule (disabled for the first 20 epochs, then 0.5), pressing discriminative information into leading dimensions in order of significance.
3. **Identified and solved cross-scale interference**: The negative result in Table 1 (per-prefix main supervision + compressed alignment pressing the same batch of prefixes drops below baseline) and the positive solution (main loss exclusively on full dimension, prefixes supervised only by compression regularization) provide direct evidence in KWS for the division of responsibilities in multi-scale supervision.
4. **Loss-agnostic generality and dual-benchmark SOTA**: Average +2.59 pp across six DML objectives (Table 2); WSJ AP 80.94% (K = 5) / 81.03% (K = 3), LibriPhrase LPH EER 20.06% / AUC 88.70% (Table 5), all optimal under the same encoder and data.

Viewing these four points together, the true asset of this paper is a clean chain of controlled experiments: Table 1 proves that "having prefixes" is worth only 0.83 pp, while "who manages the prefixes" is worth 2.28 pp; Table 2 proves this conclusion does not depend on the main loss; Tables 3 and 4 map the hyperparameter space (number of prefixes, alignment composition). All gains come from the reconstruction of the training objective; the encoder, data, and inference pipeline remain untouched, which in engineering terms means it can be directly stacked onto any existing utterance-level audio-text KWS training framework without needing to re-pass the deployment pipeline.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First and most glaring: **the multi-granularity benefits of the matryoshka structure are not realized on the inference side**. Inference explicitly uses only full-dimensional embeddings, and the paper **does not report** any precision-overhead curves for "truncating to $d_k$ dimensions for inference" (e.g., how much AP drops when using the 64-dimension prefix directly as a lightweight embedding, or how much compute is saved for enrolled text comparison). This means MATE is currently essentially a training-time regularization technique, and the deployment value of the matryoshka structure ("one forward pass, multi-granularity output"—such as selecting dimensions based on battery level on the edge side, or a two-stage pipeline of cheap pre-screening followed by precise matching) remains at the potential level, unsupported by experiments. Second, PCA is **variance ordering, not discriminative ordering**: compression retains directions with the highest dependency matrix energy, which does not guarantee they are the most useful directions for class discrimination—the paper itself acknowledges this in the conclusion and lists LDA and other more discriminative compressions as future directions. Third, the **per-epoch recalculation overhead of corpus-level statistics**: recalculating the mean, dependency matrix, and SVD (256×256 scale) for the training corpus of approx. 210k word classes incurs additional training costs that are not separately quantified by the paper, only the total training time is given (approx. one day on two H100s). Fourth, the delay schedule is a **coarse-grained step function** (0 → 0.5, switching point set at epoch 20); there is no sensitivity analysis for the weight 0.5 or the switching point 20, and the rationale for tuning is not disclosed. Fifth, the method only covers **utterance-level matching**: long phrases, words with strong homophone interference, and scenarios requiring temporal alignment are outside its capability; phoneme-level extension is a clear future work.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

First, **no variance or significance reporting**: all tables are results of single runs (the paper does not mention multiple seed repetitions); the 1.56 pp difference between K = 4 and K = 5 in Table 3, and the 0.08 pp difference between pure MSE and pure KL in Table 4, are difficult to judge for robustness without confidence intervals. Second, **single training corpus**: only King-ASR-066 is used as the training source (although evaluation includes cross-corpus tests), with no verification on multi-language, multi-accent, or real far-field device data; there is a clear gap between the Samsung scenario motivation (edge-side, lineage of Hi Bixby) and the experimental data. Third, **lack of deployment dimension metrics**: as a work repeatedly emphasizing edge-side low latency, there are no system-level numbers for latency, memory, or energy per second (not reported in the paper); it is impossible to judge whether the combination of 1.8M parameter ECAPA-TDNN + bidirectional LSTM text encoder is feasible on MCU-level hardware. Fourth, some attributions remain at the **conjecture level**: the explanations for the drop at K = 4 and the rise at K = 5 are both labeled conjecture, lacking controlled variable experiments (e.g., training with the 32-dimension prefix removed alone) to support them. Fifth, the WSJ protocol follows RPL's custom experimental construction, making direct horizontal comparison with numbers from external papers impossible (the paper only compares with reimplementations using the same encoder and protocol, which is rigorous in itself, but also means the SOTA context in the tables is limited).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper lists two itself: extension to **phoneme-level matching** (combining matryoshka structure with sequence alignment to cover longer phrases and finer temporal granularity); and replacing PCA with **more discriminative compression** (such as LDA) so that the teacher signal sorts by class discriminability rather than variance. Beyond this, directions naturally extending from experimental gaps include: truly using prefixes for **elastic dimension deployment** on the inference side (a two-stage retrieval pipeline where low-dimensional prefixes do cheap pre-screening and full-dimension does precise matching—this is the classic use of MRL in retrieval systems, with considerable benefits when the KWS enrolled text library is large); replacing the delay step schedule with **smooth warm-up or curriculum-based weights**, and systematically scanning $\lambda_{align}$ and the switching point; verifying generalization on multi-language/far-field/accent corpora; and changing the per-epoch corpus statistic recalculation to sliding updates or exponential decay estimation to reduce training overhead. For engineering readers, the most transferable judgment from this paper is: **the key to multi-scale supervision is not "adding prefixes," but "assigning prefixes a dedicated teacher that does not conflict with the main objective"**—this principle should hold for any edge-side task requiring elastic granularity embeddings (speaker, voiceprint, event detection).
