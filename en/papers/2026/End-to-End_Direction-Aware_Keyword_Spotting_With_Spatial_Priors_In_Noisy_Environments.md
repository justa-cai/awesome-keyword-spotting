# End-to-End Direction-Aware Keyword Spotting with Spatial Priors in Noisy Environments

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Rui Wang, Zhifei Zhang, Yu Gao (Corresponding Author), Xiaofeng Mou, Yi Xu —— AI Research Center, Midea Group
- **Date**: March 2026 (arXiv:2603.09505v1 [eess.AS], submitted 2026-03-10)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09505
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, spatial prior, end-to-end multi-channel, DOA discretization, noise robustness, streaming MDTC backbone, microphone array

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS, also known as Wake Word Detection, WWD) is the task of detecting predefined wake words from continuous audio streams. It serves as the entry point for voice interaction and is widely deployed in commercial assistants such as Amazon Alexa, Apple Siri, Google Assistant, and Microsoft Cortana (Introduction, citations [1][2]). The authors are from the AI Research Center of Midea Group, and their motivation clearly stems from the deployment pressures of home appliance voice interfaces in real-world acoustic environments. Although specific product lines are not named, the phrase "reliable deployment in complex acoustic scenarios" runs throughout the paper.

The paper highlights three layers of real-world constraints at the outset: First, real applications often require responding only to commands from a **specific target speaker**, rather than triggering on any wake word shouted by anyone. Second, the acoustic environment in real scenarios is far more complex than in laboratories; background noise, reverberation, and overlapping speech all erode detection performance. Third, device-side KWS must run continuously in a streaming, low-power, and low-parameter manner. Any robustness solution that increases computational burden must be weighed against its cost.

The most common remedial measure in the industry is a **cascaded pipeline**: inserting a front-end enhancement or beamforming stage before the acoustic model (citations [3]–[9] cover a spectrum including neural network mask estimation, MVDR beamforming, and LSTM speech enhancement). With the proliferation of microphone arrays on devices, multi-channel processing has become increasingly important. Existing studies have shown that leveraging spatial information of the target source can significantly improve separation and extraction under multi-channel conditions (citations [10]–[12], including geometric source separation, geometrically constrained independent vector analysis, and directional target speaker extraction). However, the paper sharply points out two structural defects in traditional cascaded approaches: First, the **decoupling** between the front-end and the detector hinders joint optimization. Second, there is a risk of **objective mismatch**—the optimization goal of the front-end is enhancement quality or signal-to-noise ratio, not the accuracy of downstream wake-word detection. Processing artifacts introduced by the front-end for noise reduction can only be passively accepted by the detector. Even with large-scale data augmentation (additive noise, RIR convolution), robustness under noise and interference remains difficult (citation [16] is Amazon's dynamic curriculum learning work, indicating this is still an unsolved problem in the industry).

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper summarizes the problems with existing end-to-end KWS methods into two clear gaps (last paragraph of the Introduction):

First, **insufficient utilization of multi-channel information**. Deep learning has pushed KWS towards end-to-end modeling, with joint learning of feature extraction and detection (citations [17][18]). Production-grade toolchains like WeKws provide standardized pipelines and practical streaming recipes (citation [19], which is the source of the single-channel baseline in this paper). However, many end-to-end systems still assume single-channel input or only use simple **channel stacking** to process multi-channel signals—concatenating spectral features from multiple channels along the feature dimension and feeding them into a standard backbone. The result is insufficient utilization of spatial cues and a lack of a **learnable spatial module** (citation [14]). The deeper problem with channel stacking is that the network must rely entirely on data to learn to disentangle spatial cues such as inter-channel phase differences and amplitude differences from the stacked features, without any structural prior guidance or an explicit mechanism to inject "where the target is." The hardware cost of multiple microphones is paid, but the spatial information is only implicitly utilized.

Second, **directional knowledge is rarely explicitly modeled**. Direction (where the target speaker is located) is one of the strongest cues for distinguishing the target from interference under multi-speaker conditions, yet existing end-to-end KWS almost never models it. This limits target speaker perception under multi-speaker conditions and bottlenecks noise robustness and interference suppression in real-world deployments.

Combining these two gaps, the paper's judgment is that an end-to-end framework is needed that places spatial modeling **inside the recognizer** and allows for the **injection of direction-aware priors**.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved

The core problem to be solved in this paper can be broken down into four interrelated sub-problems:

1. How to make multi-channel spatial information **learnably** utilized within KWS, rather than relying on hand-designed beamforming or simple channel stacking—while avoiding the objective mismatch of cascaded schemes;
2. How to inject the external knowledge of "target direction" into the network in the form of **discrete labels**, so that it biases towards the target direction under noise, without dragging down the model when directional information is unreliable;
3. How to maintain a **strictly streaming** inference form (causal convolution, caching left context, no future frames) and a small parameter budget after injecting the spatial module and prior embeddings;
4. What is the relationship between the **granularity** of the prior (number of discrete intervals K) and noise intensity—this is the engineering decision basis for "how many prior zones to select" during deployment.

The fourth point is the most informative finding in the experimental section of this paper and is the value point that distinguishes it from "another multi-channel KWS paper."

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall framework (Paper Fig. 1) consists of three jointly trained modules: **(i) Spatial Encoder** learns inter-channel features from multi-channel signals; **(ii) Spatial Embedding** injects direction-aware priors into the network; **(iii) Streaming KWS Model** (MDTC backbone + multi-keyword classification heads). The first two provide **complementary conditional information**—audio-based spatial acoustic features and label-based directional cues—and the entire network is optimized end-to-end under the standard KWS loss. This "dual-path conditioning" design is worth expanding: the audio path (spatial encoder) implicitly learns spatial structure from the signal, while the label path (DOA embedding) explicitly tells the network where the target is. These two paths of information are fused at the feature dimension and passed to the shared backbone. When the DOA prior is unavailable or unreliable, the label path can degrade to a "no prior" token (label 0), and the system as a whole degrades to implicit spatial modeling via the audio path alone—this ensures that the same architecture can cover both "with prior" and "without prior" operating modes.

Design motivation for input representation: Multi-channel waveforms are converted to **complex spectral features** $X_m(f, t)$ in the time-frequency domain. The insistence on the complex domain rather than the common Fbank (the single-channel baseline uses Fbank) is because complex spectra preserve the **phase and amplitude relationships** between channels, thereby naturally carrying spatial cues—the inter-channel phase difference (IPD) encodes the time delay difference of sound sources arriving at each microphone, and the inter-channel level difference (ILD) encodes direction-dependent amplitude differences. These two quantities are the physical basis for spatial localization; taking the modulus or Mel energy would lose phase information, breaking the spatial cues.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Spatial Encoder** (Eq. 1):

$H \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T' \times d} = \text{Encoder}_{sp}(X_m(f, t))$

where $B$ is the batch size, $T'$ is the time step after subsampling, and $d$ is the feature dimension. Structurally, it is a two-stage Conv2D subsampler: the first stage is a **complex 2D convolution** (stride along both time and frequency dimensions) followed by ReLU, and the second stage is a **lightweight real-valued Conv2D** with stride, reducing the time resolution from $T$ to $T'$. The paper provides limited motivation for this configuration; my structural interpretation is that complex convolution performs joint linear transformation on the real and imaginary parts of the input (i.e., magnitude and phase), which is equivalent to a combination sensitive to phase rotation, making it the most natural layer for extracting IPD-like cues, hence placed in the first stage; complex convolution has significantly higher parameter and computational overhead than real-valued convolution, so the second stage reverts to real-valued convolution to control the cost of the spatial module. A point explicitly emphasized by the paper is: the output of the spatial encoder **retains inter-channel cues—IPD/ILD-like features—without performing explicit beamforming**, and is trained end-to-end jointly with the KWS objective. This sentence is the key to the methodology of the entire paper: no beamforming synthesis means there is no intermediate product of "enhanced single-channel signal"; spatial information enters the recognizer directly in feature form, and gradients can backpropagate all the way from the wake-word detection loss to the first layer of spatial feature extraction.

**Spatial Embedding** (Eq. 2):

$e_\theta = \text{Emb}(\theta), \quad e_\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$

where $\theta \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, and $K$ is the partition index of the acoustic space. The target Direction of Arrival (DOA) is discretized into $K$ angle zones covering the array's Field of View (FOV). $K$ controls the spatial resolution of the prior, with approximate zone width of $\text{FOV}/K$. The embedding network is very lightweight: two-layer MLP with ReLU non-linearity, followed by dropout and LayerNorm—the reason given by the paper is **stabilizing conditioning**, i.e., preventing scale drift of the prior embedding from interfering with backbone features. DOA labels are assumed known during training and evaluation; at deployment, the same interface can accept the output of any DOA estimation method, which is a very pragmatic decoupling design.

**Feature Fusion** (Eq. 3):

$\tilde{H} = H + e_\theta$

i.e., the encoder output and the prior embedding undergo **linear additive fusion**. The paper's explanation for this is: this fusion **biases the model towards the target direction while preserving acoustic evidence**. The benefits of additive fusion can be understood from three perspectives: First, $e_\theta$ and $H$ have the same dimension $d$, broadcast-added per time step, with zero additional parameters and no change to sequence shape, making it completely transparent to the streaming backbone; Second, addition is a "soft bias" rather than a "hard gate"—the prior only pushes the features towards the target direction, and the final decision is still dominated by acoustic evidence; if the direction is wrong, the model still has the opportunity to correct itself based on features; Third, in conjunction with the no-prior token of label 0, $e_0$ can learn to be a near-zero-vector "non-intervention" embedding, achieving plug-and-play for the prior.

**KWS Module**: The backbone adopts Multi-scale Depthwise Temporal Convolution (MDTC, citation [22], a two-stage streaming keyword detection and localization work by Hou/Xie/Zhang published in Neural Networks 2022). MDTC stacks **causal depthwise separable temporal convolution (DTC) blocks** with varying dilation rates, capturing multi-scale temporal context under streaming constraints—effectively expanding the receptive field without increasing parameter overhead. The "why" for choosing MDTC is clear: it is a backbone designed for streaming, small-footprint KWS. Causal convolution naturally satisfies the "no future frames" constraint, and the depthwise separable design ensures that parameters do not inflate as the receptive field expands, aligning with this paper's experimental claim of "comparing under similar parameter budgets." Inference runs in a streaming manner using causal convolution plus **caching left context**. To support multiple wake words, **independent binary classifiers—each keyword has a single-node sigmoid head** are attached after the shared encoder, with encoder parameters shared across all keywords. This design follows standard practices in previous KWS systems (citation [19]), with three benefits: the backbone remains compact; each keyword can be scored and triggered independently; adding or removing keywords only requires training the corresponding small head and reusing the shared encoder—very friendly to the operational needs of home appliances that frequently adjust wake words. At runtime, each head outputs the posterior for its respective keyword, which undergoes simple posterior smoothing and **per-keyword independent thresholds** to issue stable triggers.

**Training Objective**: C-class cross-entropy ($C = \text{number of keywords} + 1 \text{ non-keyword class}$); the loss is averaged over **valid frames** after flattening the time axis; per-frame accuracy is reported as a training diagnostic metric. This is the meaning of "standard loss"—the innovation of this paper lies not in the loss function, but in the structural dual-path spatial conditioning.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Learnable Spatial Encoder Replaces Explicit Beamforming Front-end

Traditional cascaded systems use beamformers like GSC/MVDR to "compress" multi-channel signals into an enhanced single channel before recognition, completely decoupling spatial processing from recognition. The spatial encoder in this paper acts directly on multi-channel complex spectra, outputting intermediate features that retain IPD/ILD-like inter-channel cues, without this explicit beamforming step, and is trained jointly with the KWS objective. The "why" chain for this design is: the biggest pathology of cascaded systems is that enhancement artifacts cannot be jointly optimized by the recognizer (Section 4.1 experiments confirm that cascaded systems lag behind due to this); the solution is to **cancel the intermediate product**, making spatial feature extraction itself the first few layers of the recognizer, shaped directly by the gradients of wake-word detection. In Table 1, this design (2ch E2E without prior, 76.92% @0dB) is 4.73 percentage points higher than the cascaded baseline (72.19%) (calculated by the author), accounting for the bulk of the total gain.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Discrete DOA Label Embedding and Additive Prior Injection

Making external directional knowledge into a "vocabulary-style" discrete embedding ($\theta \in \{1..K\}$) rather than continuous angle regression is a robust engineering choice: discrete embeddings avoid the dimensional and periodicity issues of angle regression (0° and 360° are adjacent), each zone can learn an independent bias vector, and it naturally supports the extension of a "no prior" token (label 0). Additive fusion (Eq. 3) combined with LayerNorm-stabilized embeddings makes prior injection a low-risk, pluggable operation. Note that prior information at test time comes from **metadata of rendered RIRs** (i.e., oracle ground-truth DOA). This is a choice for experimental clarity, but also one of the biggest experimental weaknesses of this paper (see Limitations).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Three-Channel Extension and Prior Vocabulary Scaling

The framework is scalable to both the number of channels and prior granularity: when extending from two channels (3 cm spacing linear array, front hemisphere 180°, 6 zones of 30°) to three channels (full 360° azimuth, 12 zones of 30°), only the input channel dimension and spatial embedding vocabulary size (6 → 12) need to be modified; all other network hyperparameters and training criteria remain exactly the same. This scalability is itself a design benefit: prior granularity $K$ becomes a deployment knob that can be adjusted independently, rather than a constant bound to the architecture—the paper relies on this property to complete the ablation of "prior granularity vs. noise intensity."

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to three categories of existing methods, the differences are clear: **For single-channel end-to-end KWS (WeKws [19])**, this paper changes the input from single-channel Fbank to multi-channel complex spectra, adding a learnable spatial module and prior injection, while keeping the rest of the streaming backbone and multi-keyword head design consistent—this is an intentional fair comparison design, concentrating the variable on "spatial." **For cascaded systems (GSC beam + WeKws)**, spatial information changes from an "external enhancer" to a "jointly optimized object inside the recognizer," eliminating objective mismatch. **For simple channel-stacking multi-channel schemes**, this paper additionally provides an explicit directional injection path on the label side, and this path can be turned off at any time using label 0. Compared to the lineage of directional target speaker extraction in citations [10]–[12], this paper does not pursue explicit separation or extraction intermediate signals; everything serves the final wake trigger, avoiding the indirectness of "good extraction quality ≠ accurate wake-up."

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

- **Clean Speech Sources**: Google Speech Commands v1 (GSC v1, citation [23]), consisting of 64,721 one-second recordings, 30 words, and 1,881 speakers. The train/val/test split follows the setup of the WeKws paper [19], with 10 of the 30 words set as keywords.
- **Spatialization Simulation**: Multi-channel recordings are synthesized using gpuRIR (citation [24]). Since GSC v1 is single-channel, each speech clip is treated as a **point source** and convolved with room impulse responses (RIRs) generated for specific array geometries to achieve spatialization. Acoustic environment parameters: room dimensions randomly sampled from length 3–8 m, width 3–5 m, height 2.5–4 m; reverberation time RT60 between 0.05–0.8 s; source-to-array distance 0.5–5 m.
- **Array and DOA Labels**: The two-channel configuration is a 3 cm spacing linear array, with target DOA limited to the front hemisphere (0°–180°), discretized into 6 zones of 30° (labels 1–6); the three-channel configuration extends to full 360° azimuth, discretized into 12 uniform 30° zones (labels 1–12). Both configurations additionally retain label 0 to represent "no spatial prior," used for baseline training and testing. Each rendered speech clip carries multi-channel waveforms and discrete zone labels $\theta$.
- **Noise**: Taken from 17 classes of noise in the DEMAND dataset (citation [25]), with 12 classes used for training and 5 classes for testing (class-level isolation to prevent leakage). Noise is also spatialized using independently sampled multi-channel RIRs (same simulator and room pool) and mixed with target speech; SNR is uniformly sampled in [0, 10] dB during training.
- **Test Set**: Uses the same simulator, array, and DOA discretization as training, but SNR is fixed at three levels: 0, 5, and 10 dB, with 6,835 independent test samples rendered per level. Unless otherwise specified, the spatial information used for priors comes from the metadata of rendered RIRs (oracle DOA).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The primary metric is the **average accuracy (%) on the test set at each fixed SNR**, listed alongside **parameter count (parameters)** in Table 1; per-frame accuracy is also reported as a diagnostic during training (Section 3.1.4). The rationale for choosing accuracy stems from the task format: GSC v1 is a fixed-vocabulary, one-second clip classification task, and utterance-level accuracy is the natural metric aligned with the original WeKws setup. However, it must be pointed out that the core metrics for production KWS are typically **miss rate/false alarm rate at a given false alarm rate (e.g., FRR@N FA/h), AUC, DET curves, and trigger latency**—none of which are reported here. Aside from parameter count, there is no data on FLOPs, inference latency, or memory usage, which is a significant evidence gap for the claim of "streaming deployment."

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Six comparison systems are included (Table 1, parameter count and accuracy at three SNR levels):

| Method | Parameters | 0 dB | 5 dB | 10 dB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-channel Baseline (WeKws [19], using only Channel 1, Fbank features) | 164k | 69.86% | 75.89% | 81.68% |
| Enhanced Cascaded Baseline (GSC Beamforming [26] + WeKws) | 164k | 72.19% | 78.19% | 82.87% |
| Two-channel E2E No Prior ($\theta=0$) | 279k | 76.92% | 84.26% | 88.57% |
| Three-channel E2E No Prior ($\theta=0$) | 279k | 80.23% | 85.85% | 89.40% |
| Two-channel Proposed Method (6-zone prior) | 279k | 77.67% | 85.25% | 89.42% |
| Three-channel Proposed Method (12-zone prior) | 279k | 79.39% | 85.22% | **89.61%** |

The core conclusion of Section 4.1 of the paper: **Two-channel end-to-end + directional prior ranks first in the 2-channel group across all SNR levels**; it achieves 77.67% accuracy at 0 dB, a relative improvement of 11.18% compared to the single-channel WeKws baseline, and is absolutely 5.48 percentage points higher than the beamforming cascade (77.67 − 72.19, verified by the author). Under similar parameter budgets (164k vs 279k, E2E is approx. +70%, calculated by the author), the end-to-end system consistently outperforms non-end-to-end baselines in all nine (system × SNR) combinations; although the cascade is better than the single channel (0 dB +2.33 percentage points, calculated by the author), it is limited by the separation of front and back ends—the processing artifacts introduced in the enhancement stage cannot be jointly optimized with the downstream recognizer, which is the mechanistic explanation given in Section 4.1.

Breaking down the gain structure (calculated by the author): At 0 dB, the increase from single-channel 69.86% to two-channel E2E no prior 76.92% (+7.06 percentage points) is contributed solely by **multi-channel complex spectra + learnable spatial encoder**, which already exceeds the 72.19% of the cascade with beamforming; adding the 6-zone directional prior to 77.67% (+0.75 percentage points) is the net gain of the **explicit prior**. That is, the bulk of the gain in this paper comes from the structural change of "moving spatial modeling into the recognizer," and the prior is just icing on the cake—the paper itself admits this in Section 4.2. The increase from two-channel to three-channel no prior (76.92% → 80.23%, +3.31 percentage points) shows that denser spatial sampling (one more microphone) itself yields considerable gains.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The ablation dimensions of this paper are **with/without prior × two/three channels × three SNR levels** (embedded in Table 1), with three findings:

**Finding 1: The overall marginal gain of the prior is small, because there are no interfering speakers in the evaluation.** Section 4.2 of the paper directly admits: under both two-channel and three-channel configurations, the performance gap between the model with prior and the no-prior baseline is relatively small, mainly because the current evaluation lacks different interfering speakers. Without strong directional interference, multi-channel acoustic features themselves are highly informative for robust keyword detection, and the no-prior model, relying on learned spatial representations and dynamically adapting to spatial cues, can achieve strong performance independently. This honest disclosure is important—it defines the boundary of applicability for this paper's conclusions: **the value of directional priors can only be fully realized in scenarios with directional interference (competing speakers), and this paper has not yet tested that scenario.**

**Finding 2: Coarse-grained priors provide stable, small gains across SNR levels.** Dividing 180° into 6 zones for the two-channel configuration is a relatively coarse prior, which is naturally insensitive to DOA mismatch; this broad clue provides small but consistent gains under all SNR conditions (0 dB +0.75, 5 dB +0.99, 10 dB +0.85 percentage points, calculated by the author), without failing even when noise degrades acoustic cues.

**Finding 3: High-resolution priors exhibit feature-prior mismatch at low SNR, realizing gains only at high SNR.** Expanding the prior to 12 zones and full 360° azimuth for the three-channel configuration doubles the spatial vocabulary. At low SNR (0 and 5 dB), conditioning such strict high-resolution priors on features "smeared" by noise increases the risk of **feature-prior mismatch**, causing the no-prior baseline to surpass the proposed method凭借 its data-driven flexibility (0 dB: 80.23% vs 79.39%; 5 dB: 85.85% vs 85.22%). At 10 dB, clean acoustic features can reliably align with the complex 12-zone prior, and the prior becomes a stronger directional bias, allowing the three-channel proposed method to achieve the highest score in the entire set, 89.61%. From this, the paper distills a deployment guideline: **prior granularity should match the reliability of spatial cues under noise intensity**—high-resolution priors act as precise spatial filters in clean conditions, while low-complexity priors provide better fault tolerance in heavy noise. The authors announce that they will further analyze this phenomenon using DOA error perturbations in more complex acoustic scenarios (not expanded in this paper).

## Main Contributions

1. **Unified Framework**: Proposes unifying a learnable spatial encoder, direction-aware embedding, and streaming shared backbone into a single end-to-end multi-channel KWS framework, all trained jointly under standard cross-entropy loss, canceling the intermediate enhancement products of cascaded systems.
2. **Dual-Path Complementary Conditioning**: Audio-side implicit spatial acoustic features + label-side explicit discrete directional prior (including the degradation mode of "no prior" token 0), forming a pluggable direction-aware mechanism.
3. **Empirical Advantages**: Under similar parameter budgets (279k vs 164k), the end-to-end multi-channel scheme exceeds single-channel WeKws and GSC beam cascades in all nine combinations of 0/5/10 dB; at 0 dB, the two-channel proposed method shows a relative improvement of 11.18% over the single channel and is absolutely 5.48 percentage points higher than the cascade (Table 1, Section 4.1).
4. **Deployment Insights on Granularity-Noise Trade-off**: Through controlled comparisons of two/three channels and 6/12-zone priors, provides actionable conclusions that "high-resolution priors suit clean conditions, coarse priors suit heavy noise" (Section 4.2), which is a methodological contribution beyond single-point performance numbers.
5. **Scalability and Operability**: Channel count and prior vocabulary can be scaled independently; multiple wake words are implemented via shared encoder + independent sigmoid heads, where adding or removing keywords only requires training small heads.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Prior is a single-point hard assumption**: The additive injected $e_\theta$ is a deterministic embedding of a single discrete zone, without modeling the uncertainty of DOA itself; how the model degrades when DOA estimation fails is not verified by the paper (test uses oracle DOA from rendered metadata). The high-resolution 12-zone prior is surpassed by the no-prior baseline at 0/5 dB (Table 1), indicating that mismatch penalties truly exist at low SNR, while the error perturbation experiment is pushed to future work.
- **Fusion method lacks comparison**: Additive fusion (Eq. 3) is the only conditioning method tested in the paper; comparisons with alternative schemes such as multiplicative modulation (e.g., FiLM-style scale-shift), concatenation, or cross-attention are not reported, so the claim that "addition is optimal" lacks evidence.
- **Motivation for spatial encoder configuration is opaque**: The specific design rationale for the two-stage subsampler (first stage complex 2D convolution, second stage real convolution) is not detailed in the paper; hyperparameters such as channel count, convolution kernels, and stride are not listed; the implementation cost of complex convolution is higher than real convolution (usually several times that of real convolution of the same size), and FLOPs and latency are not reported, making it impossible to verify if it is "lightweight."
- **Parameter budget is not free**: 279k vs 164k is approx. +70% (calculated by the author), and three channels require even more input dimensions; for extremely cost-sensitive MCU-level deployment, this increment needs justification.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Fully simulated data**: Training and testing are entirely from gpuRIR simulation + DEMAND noise, with no real far-field array recordings; the RT60 upper limit is 0.8 s, and factors such as strong reverberation in real homes, microphone mismatch, and inconsistent microphone gain in arrays are not covered. The sim-to-real gap is not quantified.
- **Lack of interfering speaker scenarios**: The evaluation has no overlapping speech or competing speakers, which is exactly the core scenario that the directional prior design targets. The paper itself admits this is the main reason for the small marginal gain of the prior (Section 4.2), effectively saying that **the motivating scenario is not directly tested by experiments**, and the actual benefit of "target speaker perception" remains inference rather than evidence.
- **Thin metric system**: Only utterance-level accuracy is provided, without production-standard KWS metrics like FRR@FA/AUC/DET, trigger latency, or streaming long-audio performance (GSC v1 is a 1-second clip, and sliding window trigger behavior on continuous streams is not tested).
- **No statistical significance**: Each configuration reports only single-run results, with no variance across multiple seeds; differences of 0.6 percentage points (5 dB three-channel 85.22% vs 85.85%) may fall within the range of random fluctuation, making the conclusion of "low SNR reversal" based on this weak evidence.
- **Narrow baseline coverage**: Multi-channel comparisons are limited to the WeKws family (single-channel, GSC cascade, variants of this paper), without incorporating existing multi-channel/auditory attention KWS works like citation [14] or other joint optimization schemes; the claim of "surpassing SOTA" can only be limited to these baselines.
- **Tension between Abstract and Table**: The abstract claims that spatial modeling and directional priors "combine to achieve the best results," but in Table 1, three-channel + prior is lower than three-channel no prior at 0 and 5 dB—"combination optimal" only holds at 10 dB (89.61%) and within the 2-channel group; the abstract statement is overly optimistic (my critical reading).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The routes listed by the paper in Section 5: **First**, integrate a trainable DOA estimator into a multi-task framework to dynamically provide zone-level or continuous directions to condition the spatial embedding, replacing external oracles; **Second**, place an enhancement front-end in parallel with the spatial encoder to form a localize–enhance–wake trinity joint pipeline, expecting further mitigation of feature-prior mismatch under high reverberation through joint optimization; **Third**, explore richer prior designs such as probabilistic spatial embeddings to improve robustness to DOA mismatch; **Fourth**, research on-device weakly supervised fine-tuning to deploy the unified system under strict streaming latency and memory constraints.

My supplementary judgment: For those aiming for productization, the three most urgent experiments to fill are—performance under real array data and real DOA estimation errors, verification of prior benefits in scenarios with competing speakers, and production metric evaluation using the FRR@FA metric; at the method level, the most worthwhile trials are adaptive selection of prior granularity based on SNR (fine prior for clean, coarse prior or direct fallback to label 0 for noise), and replacing discrete zone embeddings with continuous probability distribution embeddings conditioned on DOA, to mechanistically absorb estimation uncertainty. Overall, this is an industrial paper with clear structure and honest ablation, but whose experimental radius is significantly smaller than its claim radius: it proves the structural judgment that "spatial modeling inside the recognizer is superior to cascaded" (sufficient evidence), while the "value of directional priors in multi-speaker noise scenarios" remains a check yet to be cashed.
