## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Traditional Keyword Spotting (KWS) systems are trained on "predefined keywords," such as "Ok Google" and "Hey Siri." These systems rely on large-scale annotated corpora. The paper provides two specific magnitudes in the related work section: Xiaomi's "Xiao Ai Tongxue" wake-word data reaches 1.7 thousand hours (citing [4]), and Amazon Alexa's wake-word data reaches 470 hours (citing [39]), accompanied by a large amount of negative samples to form a binary classification (BCE) task. In other words, the cost of launching a new wake word = re-collecting thousands of hours of positive examples + tens of thousands of hours of negative examples + retraining + redeployment, with a cycle measured in months. With the popularity of smart devices and conversational terminals (the paper specifically mentions products like Xiaomi Xiao Ai), users wish to change their wake word at any time, just like changing a nickname. This directly drives the direction of User-Defined Keyword Spotting (UDKWS): detecting a new keyword never seen during training, relying on only a small number (or even zero) of enrollment samples.

There is a systematic performance gap between UDKWS and predefined KWS (paper introduction [7]–[9]), and the abstract converges the pain points into three: (1) insufficient discriminative power between confusable words; (2) unstable performance across speakers with different pronunciations/accent; (3) high data cost required to ensure reliable wake-word activation. These three points correspond to the three modules in the subsequent paper: dual-stage matching (solving discriminative power), multi-modal enrollment (solving speaker differences), and continual adaptation (solving data and adaptation costs). The entire methodology section is designed to dismantle these three pain points one by one.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing UDKWS solutions into four types according to technical routes and points out their defects one by one (Related Work Section II):

- **LVCSR Retrieval Schemes**: Based on lattice retrieval of large-vocabulary continuous speech recognition, they have high accuracy on predefined words but perform very poorly on out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words ([10]–[12]). User-defined keywords are naturally OOV, so this path is inappropriate from the task definition perspective.
- **Seq2seq ASR Schemes** (e.g., Whisper): KWS can be performed via WFST decoding graphs, greedy search, beam search, etc., but the model size and computational overhead are large, making them "impractical for always-on edge-side KWS." This is a fundamental conflict between accuracy and resident power consumption.
- **Lightweight Phoneme-level ASR Schemes**: Phoneme detection + short-block streaming decoding results in compact models that can consume large-scale ASR pre-training data, yielding good results after full fine-tuning—but "full fine-tuning" precisely means that the new keyword customization stage still requires considerable target word data and fine-tuning costs ([20], [27], [37], [38]), failing to satisfy "rapid customization."
- **QbyE Schemes** (Query-by-Example): Subdivided into QbyA (Query-by-Audio, speaker-dependent, early methods using bottleneck features BNF + DTW [7], phoneme posterior probabilities PPP [43], acoustic word embeddings AWE [22][24][44][45]), QbyT (Query-by-Text, speaker-independent, representative works include CMCD [5], EMKWS [26], PhonMatchNet [8], CED [28], MM-KWS [9], etc.), and QbyAT (Audio+Text multi-modal, such as MM-KWS and PLCL based on Whisper/XLSR-53 alignment). The paper points out two structural defects of QbyE: First, the inference stage relies on pre-segmented pairs and sliding window detection, which mismatches the training stage (usually trained on full sentences/words); Second, if QbyT relies on large pre-trained text encoders (e.g., DistilBERT), the computational and memory overhead is large, making it unfriendly for edge devices.

In addition to architectural deficiencies, the paper specifically reviews the line of "synthetic data for KWS" (Section II-C): some works use 7.5 million synthetic positive samples to improve performance ([52]), some mix TTS and real data ratios and use interpolation analysis to estimate the required amount of real data ([50]), and some use pseudo-TTS + wav2vec2.0 pseudo-phoneme sequences for completely unsupervised few-shot training ([51]). These works prove that synthetic data is effective for rapid optimization, but none combine it with "parameter-efficient fine-tuning" into a complete customization process that can be deployed on edge devices—this is exactly the entry point for this paper's continual adaptation module.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

The paper's introduction summarizes the key challenges before deployment into two points. Combined with the three pain points from the abstract, they can be merged into one sentence: **How to use a lightweight framework with 4.1M parameters to simultaneously achieve (a) zero-shot detection of any new word with text-only enrollment, (b) fine-grained discriminative power for phoneme-level confusable words, (c) utilization of registered user accents to improve accuracy in speaker-dependent scenarios, (d) new word launch requiring only 187k trainable parameters and minute-level fine-tuning, (e) inference overhead almost not increasing relative to a single-stage CTC pipeline.** These five points constrain each other—for example, using a large encoder (PLCL's Whisper 40M, MM-KWS's XLSR-53 331.8M) buys discriminative power but destroys (d) and (e); using only CTC streaming decoding preserves (a) and (e) but loses (b). The core contribution of this paper is to use the combination of "coarse-to-fine dual-stage + multi-modal enrollment + LoRA continual adaptation" to guard these five constraints simultaneously.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

DMA-KWS is a combination of three branches overall (Figure 1): (a) Speaker-independent scenario, text-only enrollment (G2P converted to phoneme sequence), allowing any user to customize keywords without recording; (b) Speaker-dependent scenario, multi-modal enrollment, utilizing the registered user's reference audio; (c) Continual adaptation mechanism, iteratively updating the model using synthetic data and real user feedback.

**Audio Encoder**: 6-layer Conformer, 144-dimensional embedding, 576-unit feedforward module, 4 attention heads, kernel size 3, relative position self-attention, frontend conv2d downsampling, approximately 3.6M parameters, shared across all stages. The reason for choosing Conformer (citing precedents [9][26][28][33] in the paper): The convolution module captures local acoustic details (critical for phoneme-level discrimination), and self-attention captures long-range context (critical for whole-word modeling). The two are complementary and can maintain efficiency at small dimensions (144). The feature frontend follows the WeNet standard configuration: 80-dimensional log Mel FBank, 25ms window, 10ms frame shift, 16kHz sampling, 0.1 dither. Why use 80-dimensional FBank instead of MFCC: FBank retains more spectral details and is compatible with the pre-training process of the WeNet/Conformer ecosystem, facilitating direct consumption of large-scale ASR corpora.

**Why use CTC in the first stage instead of sliding window QbyE**: The CTC branch can be pre-trained directly on large-scale ASR corpora (100→460→1460 hours), learning generalizable phoneme-level representations; combined with parameter-free streaming decoding (complexity O(T×U), where T is the number of frames and U is the number of target phonemes), it avoids the structural mismatch of QbyE "training uses pre-segmented pairs, inference uses sliding windows." However, CTC matching is coarse-grained—it only looks at the "accumulation of posterior probabilities along the optimal path," being insensitive to phoneme-level differences, which is the root cause of insufficient discriminative power for confusable words (the paper empirically demonstrates this with the "six" counterexample in Figure 8: the CTC branch gives high false alarm scores for statements not containing "six").

**Why use QbyT phoneme matching in the second stage**: The matching head is very small—two layers of Transformer + GRU + fully connected layer. Text embeddings directly use nn.Embedding (the phoneme vocabulary has only 70 symbols, which is also the reason why DMA-KWS text enrollment parameters are only 9K while MM-KWS@T requires 66.8M: no need for large text encoders like DistilBERT). The entire QbyT branch has approximately 0.5M trainable parameters. It performs sentence-level + phoneme-level dual verification on candidate segments detected by CTC, specifically compensating for the fine-grained discriminative power lost in the first stage.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**First-stage CTC Modeling**. Input speech frame sequence $F_a=(f_1,\dots,f_L)$, encoder output $E_a=\text{Encoder}(F_a)=(e_1,\dots,e_T)$, $e_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$ (Equation 1); the CTC layer maps $E_a$ to a posterior distribution over the label set $\mathcal{A}$, where $\mathcal{A}$ consists of 70 phonemes plus blank, totaling 71 symbols (Equation 2). The keyword text is converted to 70 phonemes via G2P, then to a target label sequence $p$ via CharTokenizer (Equation 3). Training uses standard CTC loss (Equation 4):

$$\mathcal{L}_{CTC}(c,p)=-\log P(p|c)=-\log\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{B}^{-1}(p)}P(\pi|c)$$

where $\mathcal{B}^{-1}(p)$ is the set of all alignment paths of length T that fold into $p$. **Streaming Decoding (Algorithm 1)**: Insert blanks into the target sequence to form $\tilde{w}=[\phi,w_1,\phi,\dots,\phi,w_U,\phi]$ (length $\tilde{U}=2U+1$), initialize forward probabilities $\delta(1,1)=\delta(1,2)=1$, then recursively frame by frame—blank nodes take $\delta(t,\tilde{u})=p_t(\phi)\cdot\max\{\delta(t-1,\tilde{u}-1),\delta(t-1,\tilde{u})\}$, non-blank nodes take the maximum of three candidates (allowing repeated phoneme merging), outputting $\text{Score}[t]=\max\{\delta(t,\tilde{U}-1),\delta(t,\tilde{U})\}$ per frame. This dynamic programming completely reuses CTC forward pass, requiring no additional parameters or graph structures, hence called "parameter-free streaming decoding"—the design motivation is to make KWS detection a byproduct of ASR decoding, naturally streaming and naturally supporting any new word.

**Second-stage Matching Probability**. The phoneme matcher estimates the binary probability $P(y\,|\,E_a, E_{enr}^t)$ (Equation 5), where $y\in\{0,1\}$ indicates the presence or absence of the keyword. Its geometric interpretation (Equation 6): audio embeddings should be close to the target prototype and far from non-target prototypes, $d(e_x,p_{target})\ll d(e_x,p_{non\text{-}target})$, i.e., intra-class distance $d_{intra}$ is much smaller than inter-class distance $d_{inter}$—this is the standard formulation of metric learning, indicating that the second stage essentially learns a phoneme prototype metric space on top of CTC representations. The matcher concatenates audio embeddings and phoneme embeddings, passes them through a self-attention layer, then through a lightweight discriminator, outputting two similarities at the phoneme level and sentence level, $P_{phon}$ and $P_{utt}$. The sentence-level $P_{utt}$ is the second-stage score $\text{Score}_2$. Joint loss (Equations 7, 8): $\mathcal{L}_{matcher}=\mathcal{L}_{utt}+\mathcal{L}_{phon}$ (sentence-level BCE + phoneme-level sequence BCE), total objective $\mathcal{L}_{total}=\mathcal{L}_{CTC}+\mathcal{L}_{matcher}$, two-stage joint training.

**LoRA Update for Continual Adaptation** (Equation 10):

$$W_{attn}\leftarrow W_{attn}+\Delta W=W_{attn}+AB,\quad A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d},\ r\ll d$$

Only update the QKV matrices of the phoneme matcher's attention, with rank $r=16$. Why choose attention QKV instead of FFN: The attention projection is the most sensitive component in the matcher regarding "how phoneme-audio alignment is done"; changing it is equivalent to changing "how to compare," while keeping the encoder unchanged ensures "what is compared" (acoustic representation) does not drift, thereby balancing anti-forgetting and adaptation capability.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Coarse-to-Fine Dual-stage Matching

The first-stage CTC streaming phoneme search is responsible for "localization": finding candidate segments from frame-wise posteriors and providing a coarse score $\text{Score}_1$. Only if it exceeds threshold $\tau_1$ is the second stage activated. The second-stage QbyT phoneme matcher is responsible for "verification": performing sentence-level and phoneme-level dual verification on candidate segments. The two levels are cascaded via threshold $\tau_1$, restricting expensive fine-grained matching to a few candidates (on Hey-Snips, when optimal $\tau_1=0.04$, the second stage is activated only 1953 times, Figure 13). The second stage supports two input modes, corresponding to two performance-cost tiers:

- **Mode 1 Enc-Clips (Encoder Frozen)**: Crop audio features directly from the CTC branch output according to the time stamps predicted in the first stage, pass them through a lightweight linear layer into the matcher. The entire process reuses the same encoder forward pass, with almost zero additional encoding overhead (Table XII shows the second stage adds only 3–10 seconds).
- **Mode 2 Wav-Clips (Encoder Trainable)**: Crop the original waveform using the same time stamps, and re-encode using a fine-tuned audio encoder. Dual encoding brings more discriminative features (Table I shows LPH EER drops from 10.02 to 6.13), at the cost of increased inference time (Table XII: GPU 481s vs 466s).

**Why designed this way**: The coarse stage borrows the generalizability of ASR data to preserve recall, while the fine stage uses a small-capacity matching head to preserve accuracy. After decoupling, the capacity budget for each is controllable; the cascaded threshold makes the average overhead approach that of a single stage. This is a typical implementation of the detector–verifier cascading idea in UDKWS.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Multi-modal Enrollment and Multi-modal Alignment Module (MAM)

In speaker-dependent scenarios, enrollment information expands from text to "text + enrollment audio," and the matching probability becomes $P(y\,|\,E_a,(E_{enr}^t,E_{enr}^a))$ (Equation 9). MAM fuses the two modalities into a unified speaker-aware prototype $E_{enr}^{t\prime}$. The paper compares two fusion strategies:

- **Strategy 3 Concat (No additional parameters)**: Concatenate text and audio embeddings along the time dimension and add positional encoding, $E_{enr}^{t\prime}=[E_{enr}^t;E_{enr}^a]$, leaving alignment to the matcher's own self-attention. The advantage is zero new parameters and simplest implementation.
- **Strategy 4 Cross-Attention (No additional tokens)**: Add a layer of cross-attention, $E_{enr}^{t\prime}=\text{Cross-Attn}(E_{enr}^t,E_{enr}^a,E_{enr}^a)$, using the conditional information of the enrollment audio to modulate the phoneme prototype, equivalent to "re-calibrating the standard phoneme prototype according to this speaker's accent."

**Design Motivation**: The pronunciation differences of the same keyword among speakers with different accents are very large (citing [9][29] in the paper). Text enrollment can only provide a "standard pronunciation prototype," while the enrollment audio exactly carries information about "how this person actually pronounces this phoneme." Table IV shows that Strategy 4 outperforms Strategy 3 on LPH^SD with 97.70% AUROC / 6.58% EER compared to Strategy 3's 97.67% / 6.70%, and also dominates on LP^E_SD and QcommSD—cross-attention utilizes audio information more fully than simple concatenation. During training, only the phoneme matcher and MAM are updated, while all other parameters are frozen (20k steps, learning rate 4e-4) to prevent small-data enrollment fine-tuning from destroying pre-trained representations.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: LoRA Continual Adaptation (Sequential Fine-tuning from Synthetic Data to Real Feedback)

The complete process for customizing a new wake word is divided into two steps: First, use TTS (e.g., F5-TTS) to synthesize positive examples and confusable negative examples (optional), and fine-tune the LoRA adapter to enhance discriminative power; Second, continue fine-tuning using real user feedback or collected recordings to refine the specific performance of that word. Key numbers (Section IV-B): In the first stage, the encoder is fine-tuned with rank 16 LoRA, 114k trainable parameters, 5k steps, learning rate 4e-4, taking approximately 30 minutes; in the second stage, the matcher introduces 73k parameters with the same rank LoRA; totaling 187k trainable parameters, taking approximately 3 minutes, which can be completed on a single RTX 4090 (the main training uses 4 RTX 4090s). To mitigate catastrophic forgetting, the fine-tuning set is balanced with "equal amounts of general ASR data + phrase data"—Table VIII proves that general tasks (LSclean P-WER, LPH/LPE detection) hardly drop after fine-tuning (LoRA 187k: LSclean 4.68%, LSother 12.71%, LPH 95.50%/10.27%, compared to Base's 4.45%/11.80%/95.77%/10.02%).

**Why parameter-efficient fine-tuning is necessary instead of full fine-tuning**: First, edge-side storage and bandwidth cannot accommodate multiple full-weight versions; having a full model for each keyword in a multi-keyword scenario is infeasible; second, full fine-tuning on small data easily leads to overfitting and destroys general capabilities; third, 187k parameters mean the fine-tuned product can be distributed like a "plugin" per word. The DET curve comparison in Figure 10 shows that LoRA (73k) and full fine-tuning (0.5M) have similar detection performance on "OK Google," saving nearly an order of magnitude in parameters.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Negative Decoding Strategy for Shared Prefixes

The paper analyzes two sub-cases of shared prefixes (Figure 12 and footnote 12): (a) **Phonetic Difference Type**, such as "alexa" (/AH0 L EH1 K S AH0/) and "Alexander" (/AE2 L AH0 G Z AE1 N D R IY0 AH0/), where spelling prefixes are the same but phoneme sequences differ; CTC decoding can recognize phoneme differences and thus avoid false triggers; (b) **Phonetic Inclusion Type**, such as "Rain" and "Rainbow," where the short word's phonemes are completely covered by the long word's phonemes, causing the model to trigger the short word in the prefix region of the long word. For the latter, the paper proposes a **negative decoding strategy**: when the confidence score of the longer word is higher, suppress the trigger of the short target word. This strategy avoids relying on ASR or semantic recognition. It should be noted that this strategy is only given qualitative heatmap analysis in Figure 12 in the main text, without separate quantitative ablation data (not reported in the paper).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to mainstream QbyT/QbyAT schemes, the differences of DMA-KWS are concentrated in four areas: First, **parameter-free streaming decoding vs. sliding window detection**—directly reusing CTC forward pass for frame-synchronous search, eliminating training/inference mismatch; second, **dual-stage cascading vs. single-stage matching**—MM-KWS, CED, etc., use hard example mining to force discriminative power in a single stage, while DMA-KWS separates "localization" and "verification," using each's own capacity; third, **9K enrollment parameters vs. 66.8M–398.6M** (Table IV)—the text enrollment side uses nn.Embedding for direct table lookup (phoneme vocabulary has only 70), whereas MM-KWS@T enrollment runs DistilBERT (66.8M), and MM-KWS@AT runs XLSR-53 (331.8M, 398.6M on the enrollment side), a difference of 3–4 orders of magnitude in enrollment overhead; fourth, **built-in continual adaptation closed-loop vs. only pre-training**—existing works focus on pre-training (PLCL uses 680k hours, PhonMatchNet uses 110k hours), while DMA-KWS covers post-deployment customization and iteration with 1460 hours of pre-training + 187k parameter fine-tuning process. Compared to CaTT-KWS (transducer-transformer cascading), which is also cascaded, DMA-KWS's verification level explicitly performs phoneme-level supervision ($L_{phon}$), making it more robust to phoneme-level substitution errors (Table XI).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

**ASR Pre-training** (First stage): LibriSpeech clean subsets train-clean-100 (LS-100, 100 hours) and train-clean-100/360 (LS-460, 460 hours); 1000-hour subset of GigaSpeech (GS-1000, taken from the Middle part, approx. 910,140 audio clips); merged into LS-GS-1460 (1460 hours). **Phrase Training** (Second stage): LibriPhrase anchor limit 0.5–2 seconds, LP-100 approx. 12k classes, LP-460 approx. 78k classes, plus two subsets sampled from LP-460: 20k/40k; GigaPhrase (GP-1000) derived from GigaSpeech using the same MFA segmentation process; LP–GP–1460 totals approx. 155k phrase classes. **Evaluation Sets**: LibriPhrase test set (taken from train-others-500), negative examples divided into Easy (LPE)/Hard (LPH) by Levenshtein distance; Cross-domain GSC (1-second command word recordings, 10 English command words, 64,720 evaluation samples, 237k anchor pairs); Qcomm (4,270 sentences, 4 keywords "Hey Android / Hey Snapdragon / Hi Galaxy / Hi Lumina", max 50 speakers); Speaker-dependent re-segmented LibriPhrase (positive pairs approx. 35k/35k/10k/1k by keyword length 1–4, negative examples divided into LPESD / LPHSD); AudioMNIST (approx. 30k clips, 9.5 hours, 60 people speaking English digits 0–9); Hey-Snips wake-word dataset (negative examples aggregated from train/dev/test totaling approx. 97 hours for false alarm set); DeepMine Persian accent dataset (select 5 words for first-stage evaluation and fine-tuning, second-stage specialized evaluation for "OK Google"). **Training Configuration**: CTC stage uses speed perturbation (0.9/1.0/1.1) and SpecAugment (max time mask 50, max frequency mask 10); second stage 50k steps, increasing anchor class numbers, no augmentation; 4×RTX 4090 for training, 1×RTX 4090 for fine-tuning.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **AUROC and EER** (Main metrics for SI-KWS / SD-KWS): Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve and Equal Error Rate. Rationale: UDKWS is an open-set detection task, and the operating point (threshold) in actual deployment varies by device and scenario. Threshold-independent AUROC characterizes overall ranking quality, while EER provides the error level at the "miss=FA" balance point; the two are complementary; EER is particularly suitable for horizontal comparison of systems with different threshold strategies.
- **P-WER (Phoneme-level Word Error Rate)**: Calculated after converting all texts to the 70-phoneme set, used to track ASR pre-training quality (Equation 3). Rationale: The capability of the first stage is essentially phoneme recognition; P-WER directly measures "how good the representation is," establishing a causal chain with downstream KWS performance (Table II shows that as P-WER drops, LPH AUROC rises).
- **Recall@FAR (Recall at Fixed False Alarm Rate)**: Main metric for WUW task, reporting FAR = 0.05/0.5/1.0 per hour (Hey-Snips) and FAR = 0.05/h (custom wake words). Rationale: The engineering constraint for wake-word products is that the false alarm budget (number of false wake-ups per hour) is fixed first, and recall is maximized under this constraint. Recall@FAR aligns with real product acceptance criteria and is more meaningful than raw accuracy.
- **Inference Time** (Table XII): Total inference time for 10.75 hours of LibriSpeech test set, single process, covering three hardware tiers: RTX 4090D GPU / Intel i9-14900K CPU / Mac mini M2 CPU. Rationale: To verify whether the overhead claims of the cascading architecture truly hold.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

**Dual Data Scaling (Table II)**: ASR corpus 100h→460h, LSclean/LSother P-WER drops from 6.98%/18.79% to 4.44%/13.39%; adding GS-1000 to make 1460h, LSother further drops to 11.80% (LSclean stays flat at 4.45%, indicating the clean domain is saturated, gains mainly come from accent/channel diversity). When phrase data is expanded synchronously in the second stage, DMA(1)'s LPH AUROC monotonically rises 91.78%→95.33%→95.77%, EER 15.34%→10.78%→10.02%; DMA(2) is steeper: 93.10%→97.03%→97.85%, EER 13.71%→7.97%→6.13%. **Finding 1: Data for both stages must be scaled in the same direction; the representation gains on the ASR side must be realized by matching training on the phrase side; expanding only one side yields limited gains.**

**Anchor Class Number Scaling (Table III, Encoder frozen on LS-460)**: Anchor class numbers 12k→20k→40k→78k→155k, LPH AUROC 93.22%→93.95%→94.75%→95.33%→95.45%, EER 13.38%→12.50%→11.62%→10.78%→10.65%. **Finding 2: The ability to discriminate confusable words is mainly driven by "how many different words have been seen," rather than how many samples per word; but gains show diminishing returns (+0.12 AUROC only from 78k→155k), indicating that anchor diversity gains also have a saturation point.**

**Representation Visualization (Figure 5–9)**: Figure 5's t-SNE shows phoneme-level representations clearly clustered, proving that ASR pre-training produces discriminative acoustic representations; Figure 6 shows that for six pairs of hard example words (e.g., "sex" vs "six"), DMA(1) has limited separation, while DMA(2) separates significantly after phrase fine-tuning, indicating the value of encoder fine-tuning for confusable words; Figure 7 heatmap gives quantitative comparison—positive example "himself" audio enrollment similarity 93.47, text enrollment 95.52, hard negative "itself" audio enrollment still as high as 82.37 while text enrollment is only 9e-4, easy negative "sometimes" audio 42.78, text 3e-6—**text enrollment has far better separation for hard examples than audio enrollment**, which is direct evidence that "text prototypes are precise to the phoneme, while audio prototypes are polluted by acoustic variations," and also explains why the second stage chooses QbyT instead of QbyA. Figure 9 shows that under SD settings, phoneme samples of the target speaker (black dots) form tighter, more separable clusters, providing a representation-level explanation for the gains of multi-modal enrollment.

**Fine-tuning Strategy Ablation (Table VII, Figures 10–11, DeepMine Persian accent)**: Zero-shot P-WER on five target words is 49.56/36.67/30.82/27.06/26.77%, showing significant accent domain gaps. Full fine-tuning: 100 real samples (FT-1) pulls the five words to 0.45/0.54/0.99/0.42/0.77%, 7k real samples (FT-2) to 0.39/0.26/0.59/0.32/0.40%; pure synthetic 7k (FT-3) only reaches 14.59/18.64/15.17/7.77/10.99%; 10 real + 7k synthetic (FT-4) recovers to 2.79/5.56/5.17/2.00/4.67%. LoRA version trends are consistent (FT-5–8 correspond to 1.76/1.10/13.62/7.52% average). **Finding 3: When representations are strong enough, 100 real samples ≈ 7k samples effect (FT-1 average 0.77% vs FT-2's 0.40%, FT-5's 1.76% vs FT-6's 1.10%); synthetic data is dragged down by TTS and target accent mismatch; pure synthetic is far inferior to a small amount of real data; "synthetic base + real finish" is the correct usage.** Figure 10's DET curves also show that under the same data, second-stage fine-tuning generally outperforms first-stage-only fine-tuning; Figure 11 shows that Stage-2-only model recall rises monotonically with the amount of real enrollment data but is overall lower than the two-stage strategy.

**Robustness Ablation (Tables IX–XI)**: First, posterior perturbation (interpolated with uniform distribution, strength α): CTC-Streaming's Recall@FAR=0.05 drops from 98.06% to 96.48% at α=20%, while DMA(1) only drops from 98.66% to 97.31%, DMA(2) from 99.45% to 98.26%—the second stage substantially compensates for the first stage's posterior uncertainty. Second, timestamp perturbation (Table X): Performance remains basically stable under 5–10% offset (DMA(2) LPH 97.85/6.13 → 97.63/6.59 → 96.73/8.47), only degrading significantly at 20% (94.20/11.95)—the matcher has tolerance for medium-level time misalignment and extra phoneme interference. Third, error type decomposition (Table XI, Filtered LibriPhrase Hard): Shared prefix errors Stage 1 only 54.87 AUROC / 47.26 EER, Stage 2 pulls it to 96.63/9.19 (largest improvement); single phoneme substitution 79.23/26.90→96.90/7.85; deletion errors 97.74/5.57→98.95/4.26. **Finding 4: The gains of the second stage are highly concentrated on "prefix sharing" such structural blind spots of CTC, validating the dual-stage division of labor hypothesis.**

**Threshold-Efficiency Trade-off (Figure 13)**: Fixed FAR=0.05/h, when $\tau_1=0.04$, recall peaks at 99.45%, with the second stage activated only 1953 times; continuing to lower $\tau_1$ does not significantly improve recall while redundant computation grows exponentially. There is a clear sweet spot for the cascaded threshold.

**General Capability Preservation (Table VIII)**: After target word adaptation, LoRA (187k) on LSclean/LSother/LPH/LPE is 4.68/12.71/95.50/10.27 and 99.97/0.59, full fine-tuning (4.1M) is 4.59/12.65/95.22/10.76 and 99.98/0.52, showing no significant degradation compared to Base (4.45/11.80/95.77/10.02 and 99.98/0.52)—the "equal amount of general data balancing" anti-forgetting design is effective.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed DMA-KWS Unified Framework**: First to integrate dual-stage matching, multi-modal enrollment, and continual adaptation into a single 4.1M parameter UDKWS system,兼顾ing zero-shot customization, confusable word discrimination, speaker adaptation, and rapid new word adaptation (Abstract and Introduction contribution list).
2. **Multi-modal Alignment Module (MAM)**: Fuses keyword text and registered user reference audio to achieve speaker-dependent KWS, reaching LPHSD 97.70% AUROC / 6.58% EER, with only 3.6M parameters on the enrollment side (compared to MM-KWS@AT's 398.6M).
3. **Parameter-Efficient Continual Adaptation Mechanism**: Based on LoRA, updating only 187k parameters, sequentially utilizing synthetic (F5-TTS) and real wake-word data, customizing new words in minute-level fine-tuning, and maintaining general capabilities through data balancing.
4. **Systematic Empirical Conclusions**: Achieves SOTA or near-SOTA on datasets such as LibriPhrase/GSC/Qcomm/AudioMNIST/Hey-Snips/DeepMine (LPH 97.85% AUROC / 6.13% EER), and provides transferable engineering judgments through ablations on dual data scaling, anchor scaling, and three types of robustness perturbations.
5. **Open Source**: Released DMA-KWS training and fine-tuning code (github.com/aizhiqi-work/DMA-KWS), having community value in the context of scarce UDKWS open-source resources (as stated in the paper).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Weak Adaptation Capability with Pure Synthetic Data**: In Table VII, pure synthetic fine-tuning (FT-3/FT-7) P-WER averages 10.99%/13.62%, far worse than 100 real samples (0.77%/1.76%), due to mismatch between TTS synthesized speech and DeepMine Persian accent real speech. Synthetic data can only serve as a base; completely self-service customization with "zero real data" has not yet been achieved.
- **Audio Single-modal Enrollment is a Weakness**: In Table IV, DMA-KWS(2)@A on LPHSD is 96.61/9.27, which is actually worse than pure text enrollment DMA-KWS(2)@T (97.31/7.21). Figure 7 also shows that audio prototypes have high similarity to hard negatives (82.37 vs text 9e-4). The gain from multi-modal comes from the combination of "precise text + accent-supplementing audio"; the audio modality alone is unreliable.
- **Still Dependent on First-stage Time Stamps**: Table X shows that under 20% timestamp perturbation, DMA(2) LPH degrades from 97.85/6.13 to 94.20/11.95. The cascaded structure propagates localization errors downstream, requiring a fallback mechanism in extreme misalignment scenarios.
- **Phonetic Inclusion-type False Triggers Rely on Manual Strategies**: The "Rain"/"Rainbow" problem in Figure 12 is mitigated by the negative decoding strategy (suppressing short words when long word confidence is high), but this strategy lacks quantitative ablation (not reported separately in the paper), and the scalability of rule-based design to multi-word coexistence scenarios is questionable.
- **Cascaded Threshold Requires Data-dependent Tuning**: The $\tau_1$ sweet spot on Hey-Snips is 0.04 (Figure 13); whether this holds for different corpora/SNR is not analyzed in the paper (cross-dataset $\tau_1$ generalization experiments not reported).
- **Non-monotonicity in Some Tiers**: In Table I, DMA(2) at 460h tier has Qcomm EER 3.74%, which is worse than DMA(1)'s 2.17% (only surpasses at 1460h tier, 1.52% vs 1.62%), indicating that trainable encoders have cross-domain fluctuation risks with small data.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Narrow Language Coverage**: Training and main evaluation are all in English (LibriSpeech/GigaSpeech/LibriPhrase). The only out-of-accent domain is DeepMine (Persian accent), and zero-shot P-WER is as high as 26.77%–49.56% (Table VII), exposing accent generalization weaknesses; there is no Chinese or multi-lingual evaluation, while the authors' own MM-KWS is inherently a multi-lingual framework. This absence means the conclusion of "multi-lingal transferability" can only be extrapolated.
- **No Real Edge-side Hardware Validation**: Table XII only tests three "server/desktop" platforms: RTX 4090D GPU, i9-14900K, Mac mini M2. There is no latency, peak memory, or power consumption data on MCU/DSP (not reported in the paper). While the paper repeatedly emphasizes suitability for on-device deployment, the current evidence only reaches the CPU level.
- **Absence of Noise/Far-field Conditions**: All evaluations are based on read or close-talk data. There is no added noise, reverberation, or far-field SNR scanning (not reported in the paper), insufficiently covering real working conditions for always-on wake-up.
- **SD Evaluation is Re-segmented, Not Native Collection**: Speaker-dependent LibriPhrase is constructed by re-pairing from train-others-500 by same-speaker same-word (positive pairs for keyword length 4 are only approx. 1k). Enrollment audio and query audio are in-domain, and the distribution difference of "user recording 3 clips themselves" is not modeled.
- **Incomplete Alignment in Comparison with Strongest Baselines**: PLCL uses 680k hours of pre-training, PhonMatchNet uses 110k hours, while DMA-KWS uses only 1460 hours—this highlights efficiency, but also means the lack of fair comparison in data volume dimensions (no upper-bound experiment for "DMA-KWS + large-scale pre-training," not reported in the paper).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Accent/Style Matching Synthetic Data**: Use target-domain adaptive TTS or voice cloning to first fit the registered user's timbre before synthesizing, compensating for the synthetic-real domain gap exposed in Table VII, approaching "zero real sample" customization.
- **Reliability Weighting of Enrollment Audio**: Addressing the phenomenon where @A mode is weaker than @T, dynamically weight the audio branch in MAM based on enrollment quality (duration, SNR, consistency with text), automatically degrading to pure text mode for bad enrollments.
- **Edge-side Deployment Closed-loop**: Add INT8 quantization, structured pruning, and MCU-level latency/power measurement, pushing Table XII's CPU results to true always-on hardware tiers; LoRA adapters (187k parameters) are naturally suitable for per-word OTA distribution, exploring multi-word adapter coexistence and routing.
- **Multi-lingual and Cross-domain Expansion**: Connect the framework to multi-lingual G2P and multi-lingual pre-training corpora, reuse MM-KWS's multi-lingual priors, and verify whether dual-stage matching holds equally in Chinese/mixed-language scenarios.
- **Adaptive Cascaded Threshold**: Use the first stage's confidence distribution to online estimate $\tau_1$ (or learn a lightweight threshold strategy network), replacing the fixed 0.04, solving the cross-scenario tuning problem.
- **Learning the Negative Decoding Strategy**: Transform shared prefix/inclusion-type suppression into a trainable contrastive objective (joint modeling of long word—short word), and supplement quantitative ablation, replacing manual rules.
