## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Query-by-Example Spoken Term Detection (QbE-STD) refers to the task of retrieving all audio segments containing a specific spoken term from a large audio database, given a spoken query audio, without requiring text transcription. This is a core technology for applications such as voice search, content retrieval for massive podcasts and conference recordings, and media monitoring. Unlike conventional Keyword Spotting (KWS), STD does not face a pre-defined small vocabulary but rather an open vocabulary—the query term may be unseen during training (OOV, Out-of-Vocabulary), requiring the system to possess vocabulary-agnostic generalization capabilities.

The paper categorizes existing technical approaches into three types and identifies pain points for each. The first type is ASR-based methods (References [1,2,3]), which use subword lattices to represent speech to handle OOV words. However, these rely on high-accuracy ASR models, whereas STD queries are typically short and lack context, making them the most error-prone inputs for ASR. The second type is direct audio template matching methods (References [4,5]), which use Segmented Dynamic Time Warping (SDTW, Reference [6]) to bypass transcription and directly compare audio segments. While retrieval accuracy is vocabulary-independent, the computational cost of pairwise DTW comparisons grows linearly with database size, preventing scalability to large-scale databases. The third type is discriminative acoustic word embedding methods (References [7,8,9]), which map audio segments to a vector space for distance-based retrieval. However, both training and inference require precise word boundary annotations, which are either unavailable or extremely costly in real-world scenarios. Furthermore, most existing STD systems are designed for clean acoustic environments, suffering severe performance degradation under noise and reverberation—conditions that characterize real-world voice search usage scenarios (streets, public spaces, offices).

The authors' previous work, BEST-STD (ICASSP 2025, Reference [10]), pioneered the tokenization route: discretizing speech into sequences of "subword-like" tokens, allowing the direct application of mature text retrieval algorithms (e.g., inverted index) for fast retrieval. Inference requires no boundary annotations and naturally handles OOV words. However, the first-generation system left two fatal legacy issues, which are the direct motivation for the second generation: First, tokens are sensitive to acoustic conditions—Table 1 shows that the first-generation BEST-STD token Jaccard consistency drops from 0.72 under clean conditions to 0.21 under -5dB noise, and further to 0.19 under noise plus reverberation (t60=0.7s), rendering it practically unusable. Second, token entropy is low (uneven codebook utilization), limiting discriminability and discriminative power. Additionally, semantic speech tokenizers (HuBERT[11], WavLM[12], SpeechTokenizer[13]) generate tokens that remain entangled with speaker characteristics and other acoustic cues, rather than providing "content-pure" representations.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

- **Cascading Errors in ASR Posterior Tokens**: Using posterior tokens extracted from HuBERT/WavLM for retrieval essentially bets the STD task on ASR recognition accuracy. Table 2 shows that the ASR posterior route collapses most severely under noise and reverberation—HuBERT-Large achieves an MTWV of only 0.02 for In-Vocabulary (IV) terms at -5dB on LibriSpeech (lower part of Table 2), because short queries lack context leading to transcription errors, which directly propagate into retrieval results. Even WavLM, trained on massive data for noise robustness, achieves only 0.11 for IV under the same conditions.
- **Template Matching Does Not Scale**: SDTW requires pairwise warping between each candidate segment in the database and the query, incurring high computational costs. The paper explicitly states in the introduction that this "hinders scalability," so SDTW-like methods were not included in the experimental comparisons.
- **Acoustic Word Embeddings Require Boundaries**: Both training and inference rely on precise word boundary annotations, making them "impractical for most real-world scenarios."
- **Poor Token Quality from Semantic Tokenizers**: SpeechTokenizer's semantic tokens (taking the output of the first quantizer in the RVQ stack) achieve a Jaccard consistency of only 0.45 for the same word across different speakers under clean conditions, dropping to 0.09 under -5dB noise, and only 0.03 under noise plus reverberation at -5dB (Table 1). WavLM Layer 23 features clustered via KMeans (SpeechBrain pre-trained model, 1000 codewords) achieve only 0.40 under clean conditions (Table 1). This indicates that such tokens are neither speaker-invariant nor noise-robust.
- **Codebook Collapse in Learnable Codebooks Cannot Be Fixed by KL Regularization**: The codebook in Vector Quantization (VQ) suffers from "index collapse" (Reference [15]) during end-to-end training—most codewords are unused, and the distribution $p(z|c_k)$ is severely skewed. The paper attempted learnable codebooks with KL divergence regularization (as in wav2vec 2.0, Reference [27]), but despite "extensive optimization efforts," collapse still occurred. Table 3 shows normalized entropy values of only 0.63/0.50/0.38 for codebook sizes 1024/2048/4096, with larger codebooks collapsing more severely.
- **Dual Deficiencies of First-Generation BEST-STD**: The first generation used post-hoc KMeans clustering on embeddings to derive tokens (labeled as Kmeans in Table 3), with normalized entropy dropping from 0.76 (1024 codewords) to 0.43 (4096 codewords)—insufficient token diversity directly leads to high false alarm rates (Section 4.3 explicitly attributes this: "lack of token diversity leads to higher false positive rates"). Additionally, no noise/reverberation augmentation was applied during training, causing tokens to drift with acoustic conditions.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

In one sentence: How to make the "search speech as text" route viable under real-world acoustic conditions—simultaneously achieving four goals: (1) Tokens are robust to noise and reverberation and speaker-invariant; the same word spoken by anyone in any environment maps to the same token sequence; (2) Codebook utilization is balanced; large codebooks (up to 4096) do not collapse during training, ensuring token discriminability and information content; (3) Retrieval efficiency is high enough to support large-scale databases (the paper reports ~3x speedup over the first generation); (4) Generalization is maintained for unseen words (OOV) and unseen noise levels (training on 0-10dB, evaluation down to -5dB). These four goals are mutually constraining: training techniques for robustness (noise augmentation) may make the embedding space harder to learn; constraints enforcing codebook balance may harm alignment with retrieval semantics; quantization errors from discretization may amplify in cascaded retrieval. The core contribution of this paper is a self-supervised framework that simultaneously suppresses these contradictions.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall pipeline (Fig. 1) consists of three stages: Front-end Features → Encoding & Quantization → Indexing & Retrieval.

**Front-end and Encoder**: Input audio extracts 16-dimensional MFCCs along with their first and second-order deltas (25ms window, 10ms frame shift), fed into an encoder composed of 8 layers of Bidirectional Mamba (BiMamba) blocks. The output of the last layer is projected into a 128-dimensional embedding space and L2-normalized to unit norm, totaling 8.1M trainable parameters (Section 3.4). The choice of BiMamba over Transformer is backed by experiments: the paper trained a Transformer encoder of comparable scale within the same framework as a baseline, and BiMamba outperformed it under all conditions (see Experimental Results). The authors attribute this gap to the Transformer's rotary position embeddings capturing "unnecessary temporal variations," whereas BiMamba processes temporal dependencies linearly, resulting in more effective temporal modeling. L2 normalization ensures that dot products are equivalent to cosine similarity, maintaining metric consistency with the subsequent cosine nearest-neighbor assignment in VQ.

**Quantization**: The encoder output is discretized via Vector Quantization (VQ, Reference [14]). Unlike the first generation's post-hoc KMeans, the second generation's codebook is end-to-end trainable and equipped with an Optimal Transport (OT) regularization to enforce balanced usage (this is the core innovation of the second generation, see below).

**Retrieval** (Section 2.3, following the authors' Interspeech 2025 work[17] fast progressive search strategy): Each audio in the database is cut into 1-second overlapping segments with a sliding hop. Each segment is encoded and quantized into a token sequence, and a TF-IDF representation is constructed for each token sequence. An index is built using IVF-PQ (Reference [18]). Upon query arrival, a three-stage progressive filtering is applied: Stage 1 uses the index to coarse-retrieve candidate set P1; Stage 2 filters P1 using Jaccard similarity between query and candidates to obtain P2; Stage 3 applies edit-distance-based filtering on P2 to yield the final result P3. Why this combination? Jaccard is a bag-of-words comparison, fast but losing temporal information; edit distance operates on token sequences, recovering temporal structure, so it is placed in the later stage with a smaller candidate set. The paper stops at Stage 3 for efficiency but notes that higher precision can be achieved by further refining with DTW on both discrete and continuous representations.

**Self-Supervised Training Framework** (Section 2.2, Fig. 1): Training requires no boundary annotations; supervision signals emerge from data pairing. For each spoken word $w$, two pronunciations $(u, \tilde{u})$ from different speakers are taken (without loss of generality, assume $\tilde{u}$ is longer). Context padding is added to both audio clips to make them fixed-length inputs. Noise and reverberation are randomly added to $\tilde{u}$ to generate a distorted version $\tilde{u}_n$. Among the three MFCC sequences $X, \tilde{X}, \tilde{X}_n$, DTW alignment is performed on the clean-clean pair $(X, \tilde{X})$ (excluding padding frames during alignment, yielding effective sequences of length $T$ and $\tilde{T}$, Eq. 1). The alignment set $\mathcal{A}$ provides frame-level correspondences $(t, \tilde{t})$. Then $X$ and $\tilde{X}_n$ pass through the encoder to get $Z$ and $\tilde{Z}_n$, forming frame-level anchor-positive pairs $p_t = (z_t, \tilde{z}_{n\tilde{t}})$ (Eq. 2). There are three carefully designed "whys": **Why add padding?**—to simulate real retrieval scenarios where words are embedded in continuous audio streams, forcing the encoder to learn to locate content frames of keywords amidst contextual interference; **Why add noise/reverberation only to the positive side?**—asymmetric augmentation forces the distorted representation to align with the clean one, equivalent to explicitly optimizing "distortion invariance," while keeping the anchor clean avoids polluting the supervision signal if both sides were distorted; **Why perform DTW alignment in the clean domain?**—alignment on distorted audio itself introduces errors; aligning on clean-clean pairs and mapping to the distorted side yields higher-quality supervision.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Contrastive Loss (Eq. 3)**: For the $i$-th training pair, the frame-level InfoNCE loss is:

$$L_{contrast}^{(i)} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} -\log \frac{e^{(z_t \cdot \tilde{z}_{n\tilde{t}}/\tau)}}{e^{(z_t \cdot \tilde{z}_{n\tilde{t}}/\tau)} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} e^{(z_t \cdot z_k/\tau)}}$$

The numerator is the cosine similarity between the anchor and the distorted positive ($\tau=0.1$ temperature). Negative examples $z_k$ are randomly sampled from other training pairs (word $w' \neq w$) in the same batch. This term pulls representations of the same word across speakers and distortions together, while pushing different words apart in the continuous embedding space.

**Vector Quantization (Eq. 4)**: Each $z_t$ is mapped to the codeword with the highest cosine similarity:

$$\hat{z}_t = \frac{c_{k^*}}{\|c_{k^*}\|_2}, \quad k^* = \arg\max_{c_k \in C} z_t \cdot \frac{c_k}{\|c_k\|_2}$$

**Commitment Loss (Eq. 5)**: $L_{commit}^{(i)} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_t z_t \cdot \hat{z}_t$, which maximizes the dot product between the embedding and its quantized representation, "pinning" the continuous embedding near the codeword to prevent the encoder output from drifting away from the codebook.

**Balanced Clustering Objective (Eq. 8)**—the mathematical core of the paper. Token learning is reformulated as a balanced clustering problem with marginal constraints:

$$\max_{\{c_k\}} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbb{E}_z^p \left[ p(z|c_k) s_k(z) \right], \quad s_k(z) = z \cdot \frac{c_k}{\|c_k\|_2} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbb{E}_z[p(z|c_k)] = \frac{1}{K} \ \forall k$$

Intuitive explanation: The objective is to maximize the sum of cosine similarities between frames and their assigned codewords, under the constraint that "each codeword is used uniformly" (the marginal constraint forces each codeword to be assigned 1/K of the frames). The authors point out that this problem can be interpreted as an Optimal Transport (OT) problem: the cost of assigning sample $z_t$ to $c_k$ is taken as $-s_k(z_t)$. The Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm (Reference [16]) is used to solve this in approximately linear time. The resulting soft assignment probabilities $p(z_t|c_k)$ are directly used in Eq. 7. Why OT form cures collapse: KL regularization is a "soft" penalty for imbalance (gradients can be offset by high-similarity assignments that are sub-optimized), whereas OT's marginal constraint is a hard constraint—the solution must lie within the transport polytope satisfying uniform usage. It is mathematically infeasible for a codeword to "lie flat and unused."

**Robust Consistency Loss (Eq. 6, 7)**—the third loss acts directly on the token assignment distribution:

$$L_{robust}^{(i)} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{(t,\tilde{t})\in\mathcal{A}} \left[ L(z_t, \tilde{z}_{n\tilde{t}}) + L(\tilde{z}_{n\tilde{t}}, z_t) \right]$$

$$L(z_t, \tilde{z}_{n\tilde{t}}) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p(z_t|c_k) \log \frac{\exp((\tilde{z}_{n\tilde{t}} \cdot c_k)/\tau')}{\sum_{k'=1}^{K} \exp((\tilde{z}_{n\tilde{t}} \cdot c_{k'})/\tau')}$$

The OT assignment distribution $p(z_t|c_k)$ of the clean-side embedding serves as the "target label." The similarity between the distorted-side embedding and codewords is softened into a distribution via temperature $\tau'$ (=0.1) and compared via cross-entropy. Calculated bidirectionally and symmetrically. The key lies in the alignment level: it is not aligning continuous embedding vectors, nor hard token indices, but aligning the **soft assignment distribution**—this provides smoother gradients than hard token alignment (similarity structure between codewords allows gradient propagation) and is closer to the discrete quantities actually used in retrieval than embedding-level alignment.

**Total Loss (Eq. 9)**: $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} \left( L_{contrast}^{(i)} + \lambda_1 L_{robust}^{(i)} + \lambda_2 L_{commit}^{(i)} \right)$, batch size $B=96$, $\lambda_1=1, \lambda_2=10$ (Section 3.4). The paper does not report the selection process for these weights.

**Codebook Balance Metric (Eq. 10)**: Normalized entropy $H(C) = -\frac{1}{\log K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log p_k$, where $p_k$ is the usage frequency of codeword $k$. Closer to 1 indicates better balance.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Self-Supervised Paired Training with Noise and Reverberation Augmentation

This is the first contribution listed in the abstract. During training, clean speech is mixed with noise at SNRs uniformly sampled from 0-10dB, and reverberation augmentation is applied using room impulse responses from the MUSAN corpus (Reference [21]) (Sections 3.1, 3.4). Combined with asymmetric augmentation (anchor clean, positive distorted) and DTW frame alignment, the system learns distortion-invariant, speaker-invariant tokens directly from "two different speakers pronouncing the same word" without boundary or noise annotations. The improvement over the first generation (Table 1: clean 0.72, noise -5dB 0.21) is magnitude-based: the second generation maintains a Jaccard of 0.72 under -5dB noise, exactly equal to the first generation's performance under clean conditions—effectively transplanting the first generation's "clean condition performance" to -5dB noise.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Learnable Balanced Codebook with Optimal Transport Regularization

This is the second contribution in the abstract and the source of "Balanced" in the title. The first generation's KMeans was post-hoc offline clustering, where codebook utilization deteriorated rapidly with increasing codeword count (Table 3: 1024→0.76, 2048→0.65, 4096→0.43); directly switching to an end-to-end learnable codebook leads to collapse, which KL regularization cannot fix (Table 3: 0.63/0.50/0.38, and the paper admits "despite extensive optimization efforts," collapse still occurred). The OT-regularized learnable codebook achieves normalized entropy of 0.98/0.97/0.96 (Table 3)—at 4096 codewords, entropy drops only from 0.98 to 0.96, showing almost no decay with codebook size. The benefit of a balanced codebook for retrieval is twofold: high token diversity leads to greater distinction between token sequences of different words, reducing false alarms (the first generation's high false alarm rate was explicitly attributed to "lack of token diversity" in Section 4.3); simultaneously, each codeword carries a uniform sample volume, stabilizing semantic estimation.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Distribution-Level Robust Consistency Loss

The third contribution listed separately in the abstract ("dedicated noise-robustness loss"). As described in the Mathematical Principles section, it uses the OT soft assignment of the clean side as the target distribution and the softened similarity distribution of the distorted side as the prediction, performing bidirectional cross-entropy (Eq. 6, 7). Its division of labor with the contrastive loss: the contrastive loss shapes the global geometry of the continuous embedding space (aggregating same-word, separating different-word), while the robust consistency loss specifically pins down the property that "distorted frames and clean frames must fall into the same/same group of codewords"—this is precisely the property relied upon by token retrieval. The combination ensures that "noise robustness" is not just a vague training technique, but a loss term with a clear optimization objective.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: TF-IDF Based Three-Stage Progressive Retrieval

Treating speech tokens as text words: constructing TF-IDF vectors for token sequences of each audio segment, building IVF-PQ quantized indices, and applying three-stage filtering (index recall → Jaccard → edit distance). Efficiency gains measured: on Intel Xeon Platinum 8268, memory-based retrieval for top-10 results averages ~1.2 seconds, compared to ~3.4 seconds for the first generation relying on inverted index, representing approximately 3x speedup (Section 4.3), with no drop in retrieval accuracy. Why edit distance is placed in the last stage: it introduces temporal information missing from Jaccard, but has higher computational cost than bag-of-words, so it is applied only to the small candidate set filtered by the first two stages.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Compared to the first-generation BEST-STD (Reference [10]), the second generation's improvements can be precisely decomposed into four points: (1) Training includes noise/reverberation augmentation (first generation was clean training); (2) Codebook changed from post-hoc KMeans to OT-regularized end-to-end learnable codebook (Table 3 token type column explicitly labels first generation as Kmeans, second as Learnable); (3) Addition of distribution-level robust consistency loss; (4) Retrieval changed from inverted index to TF-IDF + IVF-PQ three-stage progressive filtering (~3x speedup). Compared to ASR posterior token methods, this paper does not rely on transcription correctness; from Table 2, MujBERT-like methods collapse under reverberation while this method maintains an advantage. Compared to semantic tokenizers (SpeechTokenizer, etc.), this paper's tokens explicitly strip speaker information via cross-speaker contrastive learning (Table 1 Jaccard gap: clean 0.86 vs 0.45). Compared to acoustic word embedding methods, no word boundary annotations are needed. Compared to template matching, indexed retrieval reduces online retrieval cost from "DTW proportional to database size" to approximate vector retrieval.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

- **Training**: LibriSpeech train-clean-360 subset; validation uses test-clean subset (Section 3.1).
- **Retrieval Database and Queries**: The train-clean-100 subset (~100 hours of spoken content) is used to build the speech archive database and query set—this ensures that the evaluation speakers never appeared in training. Word boundaries are obtained using Montreal Forced Aligner (Reference [20]).
- **Cross-Database Generalization**: TIMIT (Reference [19]).
- **Training Augmentation**: Noise samples and room impulse responses are taken from the MUSAN corpus (Reference [21]); evaluation noise is taken from the ETSI background noise database (Reference [23], containing real recordings of offices, public spaces, streets, etc.), and reverberation uses the Aachen Impulse Response database (Reference [22]) RIR—noise sources and RIR sources for training and evaluation are completely different, avoiding source contamination, which is a commendable aspect of the experimental design.
- **Training/Evaluation SNR**: Training mixed-noise SNR is uniformly sampled from 0-10dB; evaluation is fixed at -5 to 20dB, intentionally including -5dB outside the training range to test generalization to unseen noise levels (Section 3.4). Reverberation evaluation condition is fixed at t60=0.7s.
- **Implementation Details** (Section 3.4): Audio segment length is 1 second, covering ~93% of spoken terms in the database to ensure sufficient term coverage during training; 16-dim MFCCs plus first and second-order deltas, 25ms window, 10ms frame shift; 8-layer BiMamba plus projection layer totaling 8.1M parameters, 128-dim embeddings; $\tau=\tau'=0.1$, $\lambda_1=1, \lambda_2=10$; 740k steps, Adam optimizer with learning rate $5 \times 10^{-4}$, batch size 96.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **MTWV (Mean Term Weighted Value, Reference [24])**: The standard primary metric in the STD field, comprehensively measuring detection performance weighted by query terms at a selected operating threshold, combining miss rate and false alarm rate. Rationale: STD is a retrieval task; single accuracy cannot reflect the trade-off between recall and precision; MTWV is the de facto standard for this task. The paper does not provide its definition formula (citing previous literature).
- **Jaccard Similarity (Reference [25])**: Set overlap between tokenized representations of different pronunciations of the same word (intersection divided by union). Rationale: It directly measures "token consistency"—whether the token sequence remains stable for the same word regardless of speaker or addition of noise/reverberation. This is a diagnostic metric closer to the tokenizer itself than end-to-end MTWV, allowing attribution of retrieval errors to the token layer or the retrieval layer. Token consistency evaluation used 5000 pairs of cross-speaker term pairs (Section 4.1).
- **Codebook Normalized Entropy (Eq. 10)**: Measures the balance of codebook usage; closer to 1 is more balanced, directly testing "whether collapse occurred."
- **Retrieval Latency**: Average query time for top-10 results, testing the "efficiency" claim.

Query Set Design (Section 3.2): Two query sets, each containing 100 unique terms—the IV set (the text form of the word appeared in training data, but query pronunciation comes from unseen speakers) and the OOV set (neither the text form of the word nor the speaker appeared in training data), testing speaker generalization and vocabulary generalization respectively.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

**Token Consistency (Table 1, Jaccard, higher is better)**: BEST-STD 2.0 (1024 codewords) achieves 0.86 under clean conditions, 0.72/0.78/0.81/0.83/0.84 under noise at -5/0/5/10/15dB, and 0.61/0.69/0.74/0.77/0.79 under noise plus reverberation (t60=0.7s)—highest across all conditions. Key comparison numbers: First-generation BEST-STD clean 0.72, noise -5dB 0.21, reverberation -5dB 0.19; the second generation's 0.72 under -5dB noise exactly equals the first generation's clean condition level; the paper text states a "10% improvement" over the first generation under clean conditions (Table 1 actual values 0.86 vs 0.72). Baseline side: WavLM-Large ASR posterior (32 tokens) clean 0.72, reverberation -5dB 0.52; HuBERT-Large posterior clean 0.73, reverberation -5dB 0.24; SpeechTokenizer semantic token clean only 0.45, collapsing to 0.03 under reverberation -5dB; WavLM KMeans token (1000 codewords) clean 0.40. Notably, the second generation's tokens even surpass WavLM posteriors—the latter are models trained on massive data for noise robustness. The paper argues from this: the improvement comes not only from noise robustness but also from stronger speaker invariance and more efficient tokenization.

**Retrieval Performance (Table 2, MTWV)**: On LibriSpeech, BEST-STD 2.0 IV retrieval under pure noise at -5/0/5/10/15/20dB is 0.58/0.64/0.72/0.75/0.77/0.77, while the first generation corresponds to 0.27/0.35/0.43/0.50/0.57/0.62—an absolute improvement of 0.31 at -5dB, more than doubling; OOV is 0.51/0.62/0.65/0.67/0.68/0.68, first generation corresponds to 0.22/0.29/0.37/0.44/0.49/0.54, rising from 0.22 to 0.51 at -5dB. Advantages are maintained under noise plus reverberation: IV improves from first generation's 0.18/0.26/0.34/0.40/0.46/0.51 to 0.45/0.53/0.61/0.67/0.68/0.68; OOV improves from 0.13/0.20/.../0.43 to 0.40/0.50/0.56/0.58/0.61/0.62. The collapse magnitude of baselines under reverberation is more intuitive: HuBERT posterior IV -5dB only 0.02, SpeechTokenizer only 0.03, WavLM posterior 0.11, while this paper achieves 0.45. TIMIT cross-database results follow the same trend: pure noise IV from -5dB to 20dB is 0.60/0.67/0.78/0.80/0.81/0.82, outperforming baselines under all conditions (first generation TIMIT corresponds to 0.29/0.38/0.47/0.54/0.62/0.66). Strong performance on OOV is used by the paper to argue for the compositional nature of tokens: unseen words are composed of learned subword-like units, allowing generalization beyond the vocabulary.

**Retrieval Efficiency (Section 4.3)**: Intel Xeon Platinum 8268 CPU memory retrieval, top-10 matches average ~1.2 seconds, first-generation BEST-STD (inverted index) ~3.4 seconds, approximately 3x speedup, attributed to the TF-IDF retrieval strategy reducing search overhead while maintaining accuracy.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

It must be stated honestly: the paper body lacks a complete ablation table—the authors explicitly state "due to space limitations, additional results including ablation studies and qualitative analysis are placed on the project webpage" (end of Section 4.3). There are three embedded component-level comparisons in the body:

1. **Encoder Architecture Ablation (Embedded comparisons in Table 1, Table 2)**: A Transformer encoder of comparable scale within the same training framework (Ours-Transformer row) lags comprehensively behind BiMamba. In token consistency, clean 0.78 vs 0.86, noise -5dB 0.67 vs 0.72, reverberation -5dB 0.57 vs 0.61 (Table 1); in MTWV, LibriSpeech noise IV 20dB 0.67 vs 0.77, reverberation IV -5dB 0.41 vs 0.45, TIMIT reverberation IV 0.43 vs 0.47 (Table 2). The gap is more pronounced under noise/reverberation conditions, supporting the attribution that "BiMamba's linear temporal modeling is more suitable for this task," while also indicating that the second generation's gains do not come entirely from architecture (the Transformer version is also much stronger than the first generation, e.g., reverberation IV -5dB 0.41 vs first generation 0.18).
2. **Codebook Strategy Ablation (Table 3)**: Three codebook schemes compared within the same framework—KL divergence regularized learnable codebook (wav2vec 2.0 style) normalized entropy 1024/2048/4096 codewords is 0.63/0.50/0.38 and actually suffered collapse during training; first-generation KMeans is 0.76/0.65/0.43; second-generation OT regularization is 0.98/0.97/0.96. This verifies two things simultaneously: KL regularization is insufficient to cure collapse (worse with more codewords), while OT marginal constraints can (entropy hardly drops from 1024 to 4096 codewords), supporting the claim of "scalable learning of large codebooks."
3. **Out-of-Distribution Generalization in Training (Table 2 Design)**: Evaluation SNR covers -5dB (below the training sampling lower bound of 0dB) and 15/20dB (above the upper bound of 10dB). The second generation maintains an advantage over baselines at both ends (e.g., LibriSpeech noise OOV -5dB 0.51, 20dB 0.68), indicating that robustness is not just overfitting to noise levels seen during training.

Experiments removing individual loss terms ($L_{robust}, L_{commit}$), hyperparameter ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \tau$) sensitivity, and impact of codebook size on MTWV: Not reported by the paper (according to authors, available on the project webpage).

## Main Contributions

1. **Self-Supervised Training Framework with Noise/Reverberation Augmentation**: Using DTW frame alignment as free supervision and asymmetric augmentation (anchor clean/positive distorted) as the core, producing noise-robust and speaker-invariant speech tokens. Effect: Token consistency improved from first-generation clean 0.72/-5dB 0.21 (Table 1) to clean 0.86/-5dB 0.72, effectively transplanting clean condition performance to -5dB noise conditions.
2. **Curing Codebook Collapse with Optimal Transport**: For the first time (according to authors, novel application) reformulating token learning as a balanced clustering problem with uniform marginal constraints and solving it via Sinkhorn-Knopp, achieving normalized entropy of 0.98/0.97/0.96 (Table 3, 1024/2048/4096 codewords), standing firm where KL regularization failed (0.63/0.50/0.38 and actual collapse), supporting scalable training of large codebooks.
3. **Distribution-Level Noise Robust Loss**: Bidirectional cross-entropy using OT soft assignment as the target distribution (Eq. 6, 7), making "distorted frames and clean frames falling into the same codeword" an explicit optimization objective, complementing InfoNCE.
4. **TF-IDF Three-Stage Progressive Retrieval**: Cascaded filtering of index recall + Jaccard + edit distance, combined with IVF-PQ, reducing average retrieval time for top-10 from ~3.4 seconds in the first generation to ~1.2 seconds (~3x, Section 4.3), while outperforming baselines in MTWV under all conditions (Table 2).
5. **Paradigm Claim at the Methodological Level**: The paper concludes by positioning this work as a "paradigm shift towards tokenized speech representations," allowing spoken content retrieval to directly reuse text retrieval indexing and search technologies, bridging the methodological gap between spoken content retrieval and natural language processing.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Limited Training Noise Range**: Training mixed-noise SNR is only uniformly sampled from 0-10dB (Section 3.4), not covering below -5dB; although evaluation down to -5dB maintains an advantage, -5dB is only one step below the training lower bound, and performance under more extreme conditions (e.g., -10dB) is not reported. Reverberation is only evaluated at one t60=0.7s level, with heavier reverberation unverified.
- **Unreported Overhead of OT Solving**: Each training batch requires running Sinkhorn-Knopp to solve for soft assignments. The paper reports the algorithm's "approximately linear time" complexity conclusion, but does not provide the actual increase in training time relative to the non-OT version, nor report Sinkhorn iteration counts or entropy regularization coefficients; the balance constraint relies on batch statistics, and with batch size 96, the average sample volume per codeword is limited (for 4096 codewords, the average per batch per codeword is less than the order of magnitude of a pair of frame-level samples), and the statistical stability of marginal constraints is not discussed.
- **Traditional Handcrafted Feature Front-End**: Input uses 16-dim MFCCs plus deltas (Section 3.4), without exploring self-supervised pre-trained features (e.g., HuBERT/WavLM features) as encoder inputs—considering that baseline models' features perform poorly when KMeans-tokenized (Table 1), this choice is reasonable, but the combination of "SSL features + this framework" remains unexamined.
- **Truncation of Retrieval Precision and Efficiency**: Three-stage filtering stops at edit distance; DTW refinement (first on discrete representation, then on continuous representation) is explicitly cut for efficiency (Section 2.3); Jaccard and edit distance have no tolerance for token synonyms (legal variants of the same word's token sequence); residual errors where token consistency does not reach 1.0 (e.g., clean 0.86) will directly leak into retrieval errors.
- **Training-Inference Inconsistency in Soft Assignment Distribution**: During training, OT soft assignment $p(z_t|c_k)$ is used as the supervision target, while inference uses hard nearest neighbor for VQ (Eq. 4). The quantization gap between training and inference is not analyzed by the paper.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Ablations Not in Main Text**: Component contributions of the three losses, hyperparameter sensitivity, and other key ablations are all placed on the project webpage (explicitly stated in Section 4.3), making it impossible to verify "how much each component contributes" in the paper itself, which is the most vulnerable point for reviewers.
- **Small Query Set**: IV and OOV sets contain only 100 unique terms each (Section 3.2); confidence intervals or statistical significance tests are not reported, making it impossible to assess the fluctuation risk of MTWV under small query sets.
- **Single Language, Read Speech**: Training and evaluation are on English read speech (LibriSpeech, TIMIT); although this series of works claims language-agnostic routes (its Interspeech 2025 predecessor[17] titled Language-Agnostic), no cross-lingual experiments are presented in this paper; spontaneous speech, telephone channels, and multi-speaker overlap in real difficult scenarios are not covered.
- **Simulated Distortion Rather Than Real Recordings**: Noise and reverberation are post-hoc mixed/convoluted simulations (MUSAN noise, RIR convolution), without verification on real far-field recordings (microphone arrays, echo, compression codecs).
- **Scalability of Database Size and Latency**: The retrieval database is ~100 hours (train-clean-100); the 1.2-second top-10 latency is measured on an in-memory index of this scale; the performance of IVF-PQ when the database scales to thousands of hours and the index exceeds memory is not reported, while "scalability" is one of the core claims of this paper.
- **Gaps in Comparison Scope**: No end-to-end QbE-STD methods based on SDTW template matching are included as precision upper-bound references (excluded only for efficiency reasons), nor are comparisons made with the latest end-to-end word embedding retrieval methods.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Wider Distortion Coverage and Real Data**: Expand training noise lower bound below -5dB, train reverberation in t60 bins, and verify on real far-field and telephone data; this directly determines whether the system can move from "clean database retrieval" to the real voice search envisioned by the paper.
- **Retrieval Verification on Larger Database Scales**: Measure IVF-PQ index latency and recall curves on hundreds to thousands of hours of databases, testing the candidate pass-through rate of the three-stage filtering, which is the ultimate touchstone for the "text-like retrieval" claim.
- **Combination of Hierarchical Codebooks and OT**: This paper proves that a single-layer codebook with 4096 codewords does not collapse, but the combination of larger vocabulary expressions (e.g., residual VQ stacks) with OT balance constraints remains unexplored; SpeechTokenizer's RVQ structure (this paper only uses its first layer as a baseline) combined with layer-by-layer OT constraints is a natural extension.
- **Alignment with Text Modality**: Tokens currently have only "subword-like" structural similarity, without alignment to real subword/phoneme units; introducing text-side supervision (e.g., phoneme posterior distillation) could strengthen OOV compositionality and open channels for cross-lingual transfer.
- **Training-Inference Consistency**: Research using soft assignment or temperature-annealed quantization strategies during inference to bridge the gap between OT soft supervision and hard nearest-neighbor quantization; conduct systematic sensitivity analysis on Sinkhorn overhead and batch statistical stability.
- **Streaming and Edge Deployment**: BiMamba's linear complexity is friendly to streaming encoding; exploring incremental index update schemes with 1-second segment frame hops is worth investigating, which is also an inevitable path for this series of works to reach real-time retrieval scenarios in smart speakers and voice assistants.
