- **Authors/Affiliations**: Yutong Zhu (School of Integrated Circuits, Southeast University, Nanjing 211100)
- **Date**: March 2026 (Conference Short Paper, pages 742-747)
- **Link**: https://doi.org/10.54097/4btbhd09
- **Keywords**: FPGA Accelerator, Keyword Spotting (KWS), Data Reuse, Line Buffer, TinyML, Memory Optimization, Edge AI

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS, also known as wake-word detection) is the primary human-computer interaction entry point for smart speakers, mobile voice assistants, autonomous robots, and IoT edge devices. The paper immediately highlights two rigid constraints for such systems: First, it must be "always-on"—the device must continuously listen for predefined trigger words in a continuous audio stream without on-demand wake-up and calculation; Second, it must operate under strict energy budgets (citing [1]). The combination of these two constraints makes the hardware implementation of KWS essentially not a problem of "computing fast," but rather a problem of "how much energy and time are consumed per inference while working continuously."

The paper’s assessment of candidate hardware platforms is as follows: CPUs and GPUs offer computational flexibility, but their energy efficiency is not cost-effective for always-on KWS tasks; FPGAs, as reconfigurable devices whose logic can be customized even after manufacturing, provide a good balance among flexibility, power consumption, and performance. Their internal large-scale programmable logic blocks, Look-Up Tables (LUTs), flip-flops, and interconnect networks support arbitrary data paths, while DSP hard cores and memory blocks (BRAMs) can be customized for neural network workloads, making them naturally suitable for inference tasks that are computationally regular and highly parallel (Section 2.2, citing [2][8]).

However, there is a repeatedly verified bottleneck in neural network accelerators on FPGAs: memory access. The paper directly asserts in Section 2.3 that "memory bandwidth remains the primary bottleneck for FPGA accelerators." The sliding window calculation method of convolutional layers determines that input data for adjacent calculation windows overlaps significantly—each feature element is repeatedly used by multiple output positions. If the 9 elements in the window are re-read from off-chip DRAM every time the window moves, the number of memory accesses multiplies with the output resolution. Moreover, the energy cost of off-chip DRAM transactions is significantly higher than on-chip access (Section 3.2, the paper does not provide a specific multiplier). Frequent off-chip access drives up both energy consumption and latency. This is the core pain point this paper aims to solve: it is not that "convolution cannot be computed," but rather that "the data for convolution is not fed sufficiently or is too expensive to feed." DSPs can complete a multiply-accumulate (MAC) operation in one cycle, but memory ports may take several cycles to gather operands—the root cause of computational unit starvation lies in the memory access path.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper reviews three existing paths and points out the gaps they leave in the KWS scenario:

First, the energy efficiency of general-purpose processors is insufficient. The flexibility of CPUs/GPU comes at the cost of instruction pipelines, cache hierarchies, and general scheduling mechanisms. For a single, fixed-computation-pattern, always-on workload like KWS, a large number of transistor cycles are spent on tasks unrelated to inference. The paper only makes qualitative judgments in the introduction and does not provide specific power consumption comparison figures for CPUs/GPUs (not reported in the paper).

Second, naive FPGA implementations suffer from memory access redundancy. Taking the baseline implemented in this paper as an example: for a 3×3 convolution window, every step of movement requires accessing 9 feature elements continuously from memory (Section 3.1), of which up to 8 elements are re-read from the previous step, with unchanged values. The quantitative judgment given in Section 2.3 is that line buffer mechanisms allow the accelerator to "reuse up to 90% of feature data while maintaining computational integrity"—conversely, the redundancy read ratio of naive implementations is at this level.

Third, the granularity of existing memory optimization methods does not match the small models of KWS. The paper cites families of strategies such as data reuse, on-chip caching, and memory blocking (citing [3-5]). It specifically mentions that Shortcut Fusion-related studies show that on-chip storage allocation for intermediate data can significantly reduce DRAM access and improve the energy efficiency of FPGA accelerators (citing [4], i.e., the ROMANet lineage of reuse-driven off-chip memory management and data organization). It also notes that these optimization techniques are not only applicable to CNNs but are also key to various compute-intensive tasks such as sparse matrix multiplication on FPGAs (citing [5]). However, most of these works target general CNNs or sparse matrix multiplication, with complex optimization frameworks and significant scheduling overhead. In contrast, the typical KWS model is a small three-layer network of "Convolution + Activation + Fully Connected," requiring a lightweight memory optimization scheme that can be unified by a single state machine, rather than a large and comprehensive scheduling framework. This is the gap this paper enters: taking the most common line buffer reuse and integrating it into a small, complete KWS inference data path, and quantifying the benefits it can achieve.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Converging the above problems into one sentence: How to simultaneously reduce the number of off-chip reads and end-to-end latency for KWS inference on FPGAs solely through architectural optimization of the memory access path, without changing model accuracy (no quantization, no pruning, no change in network structure), while keeping the hardware structure simple enough (modular, parameterized, controlled by a single FSM) so that it can serve as a scalable framework for TinyML scenarios rather than a one-off engineering demo.

Specifically broken down into three sub-challenges:

1. Elimination of redundant reads in convolution sliding windows—which of the 9 reads for each 3×3 window movement can be kept on-chip, and with what structure;
2. Integration of three heterogeneous operators—the data flow of convolution (sliding window addressing, multiple reads one write), ReLU (element-wise, stateless), and FC (dense matrix-vector multiplication, weight-dense flow) must be pipelined under unified control, with intermediate data not falling to off-chip;
3. Scalability—modules must be parameterized to adapt to different model scales and bit-widths (Section 3); otherwise, if the parameters of the line buffer (row width, bit-width) are hard-coded, it is merely a single-point demo, not a framework.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall architecture (Fig. 1) is modular and hierarchical: a top-level module (Top Module) provides system interfaces for simulation and potential hardware deployment, coordinating and connecting three computing engines downward—the convolution module (conv2d.sv, optimized version conv2dreuse.sv), the activation module (relu.sv), and the fully connected module (fc.sv). These are uniformly scheduled by a central Finite State Machine (FSM), forming a pipelined data path: convolution results are continuously passed to ReLU, and then to FC to output the final classification scores. The entire system is written in Verilog/SystemVerilog and modeled/simulated in the Vivado 2017 simulator; each module is parameterized to test optimizations under different model scales and bit-widths (Section 3). The design motivation for each module is described below:

**Convolution Module**. This is the core carrying multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations, implemented using FPGA DSP slices to achieve high parallelism and arithmetic precision (Section 2.2). Why use DSP hard cores instead of LUT-based logic: DSP slices implement multiply-accumulate as fixed-function circuits, offering determined bit-widths, good timing convergence, high frequencies, and high area efficiency. This is the standard practice for FPGA neural network acceleration, and this paper follows this convention, leaving the innovation to the memory access side. In the baseline version (conv2d.sv), 9 elements are read from memory for each window movement; the optimized version (conv2dreuse.sv) adds a line buffer—storing intermediate feature data in FPGA on-chip registers. When windows overlap, data shifting within the buffer replaces re-reading from off-chip storage, and values in the buffer are reused rather than re-read from external storage. The paper explicitly states "the main innovation of this design is this optimization" (Section 3.1). It should be noted that the original paper describes the convolution scale as "carries out 3 MAC operations." Combined with the context of "nine feature elements" and "3×3 rotating window," the actual meaning should be 9 MACs for a 3×3 window. There is a transcription loss in the original text here.

**ReLU Activation Module**. Element-wise non-linearity that filters negative values in the convolution results to zero. The design key is to be "pipelined synchronously with the convolution module" (Section 3.1)—it has almost no computational load itself, but if not placed in the pipeline, it would form a stall bubble between convolution and FC, unnecessarily extending latency. Making it a stage in the pipeline, although straightforward in programming, ensures a smooth and continuous data flow.

**Fully Connected (FC) Module**. Performs dense matrix-vector multiplication on feature data to calculate classification logits for all candidate keywords. To ensure numerical integrity, this layer is implemented with custom arithmetic logic and nested loops, and is "controlled only by the system clock" (Section 3.1)—i.e., no asynchronous logic or multi-clock domains are introduced, trading throughput upper limits for timing determinism. Why is FC not parallelized? The paper does not report the trade-off between expansion degree and DSP usage (not reported in the paper). From an engineering perspective (author's note), since FC has only one layer and the paper's focus is on memory access, ensuring functional correctness and numerical integrity is a reasonable trade-off. However, this also foreshadows the later "latency only halved" issue.

**Control and Timing Logic**. Using start, output_valid, and internal status signals, the FSM manages the computation flow between modules, supporting handshake synchronization for multi-cycle execution to ensure correct operation order (Section 3.1). Why choose FSM instead of a more complex scheduler: KWS inference is a single-threaded deterministic data flow, and the execution order of conv→ReLU→FC never changes; a single state machine is sufficient. Moreover, a small number of states means timing is easy to verify and race conditions are easy to guard against—this is the premise for the waveform verification later showing "no signal overlap, no race conditions" (Section 3.3).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The "algorithm" core of this paper is not a new neural network structure, but the data reuse structure of sliding window convolution. Elaborating on it:

Definition of 3×3 Convolution: Input feature map X (specific dimensions and channel counts not reported in the paper), kernel K is 3×3. Output Y(i,j) equals the accumulation of products of 9 pairs of elements within the window—each output position requires 9 input elements.

Mathematical Structure of Sliding Window Overlap: When the window moves horizontally from position (i,j) to (i,j+1), 6 of the 9 elements in the new window (the first two columns, three rows) are exactly the same as the old window, with only the rightmost column of 3 elements being new. The same applies to vertical sliding. More generally (derived by the author, not expanded in the paper): with a stride of 1, each input element is used by up to 9 output windows of 3×3. This means that in naive implementations, each data point is on average re-read about 9 times in the total read volume. In an ideal line buffer scheme, each input element needs to be read from off-chip only once, and the remaining 8 uses all occur on-chip. In steady state, only 1 new pixel needs to be fetched per clock cycle, while the remaining 8 values are reused (Section 3.2)—this is exactly the source of the 88.9% figure:

Read Reduction Rate = (9 − 1) / 9 = 8/9 ≈ 88.89% (Table 1)

Section 2.3 states that the line buffer mechanism allows the accelerator to "reuse up to 90% of feature data," which is a rounded expression of 8/9 ≈ 88.9%. The two figures are consistent.

The paper's implementation is a "3×3 rotating window line buffer" (a 3×3 rotating window line buffer)—a set of registers that change data every clock cycle (Section 3.2). Why use a register file (flip-flops) instead of BRAM for the line buffer: The active data for a 3×3 window is only 9 words (plus row暂存 related to row width, specific row width parameters not reported in the paper). At this scale, implementing with registers reduces addressing logic to zero—shifting is addressing, and shifting in a new data point naturally matches the pipeline cycle. BRAM would incur overhead for read/write port timing and initialization, which is not cost-effective for such fine-grained shifting. The paper's corresponding statement is "this design reduces the LUT and flip-flop levels required for buffer storage, while not increasing hardware complexity" (Section 3.2).

Energy Principle: On-chip access energy is significantly lower than DRAM transactions (Section 3.2, the paper does not provide a quantified multiplier relationship). Therefore, moving 8 of the 9 reads from off-chip to on-chip will approximately linearly translate to a reduction in memory access energy. It must be emphasized: the paper only reports a read count reduction of 88.9%, not total energy numbers—"read count reduction of 88.9%" cannot be directly read as "energy saving of 88.9%," because computation energy, FC weight reading, clock tree, etc., have not changed.

Latency Model: The paper does not provide an analytical model for latency, only reporting simulation observations (latency halved). There is a notable tension here (author's note): If the serializing of 9→1 reads were the only bottleneck, the convolution segment latency should have reduced by about 9 times, while the end-to-end latency only dropped by 50%. This indicates that half of the latency is spent on segments unrelated to convolution read ports (FC nested loops, FSM state transitions, handshake overhead, etc.). The paper does not perform a latency decomposition, which is a gap in its experimental side and the most worthy point to question.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Convolution Data Reuse Based on Line Buffer (conv2dreuse.sv)

Innovation: Changing the repeated reading of convolution inputs from "9 off-chip reads per window" to "1 read per clock + 8 on-chip reuses." Why it is critical: Sliding window overlap is an inherent mathematical property of convolution; any implementation without buffering pays about 9 times the memory access cost for the same batch of data. The buffering cost for a 3×3 window is extremely low (9 words at the register level), making it a typical "asymmetric trade" of exchanging a tiny amount of on-chip resources for a large amount of off-chip bandwidth. Measured effects (Table 1, Section 3.3): Reads per cycle 9 → 1 (reduction of 88.89%), end-to-end latency 100,000 ns → 50,000 ns (reduction of 50.00%).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Integration of Three Operators Under Unified FSM

Innovation: The three heterogeneous operators—convolution, activation, and FC—are not three independent IPs acting on their own, but are hung under the same finite state machine, connected into a continuous pipeline using handshake signals (start / output_valid) (Section 3.1). Why it is critical: Intermediate data (convolution output, ReLU output) stays in the on-chip data path throughout, without going to DRAM—this effectively zeroes out the often-ignored memory access item of "off-chip round-trip of inter-layer intermediate data." The data path can be summarized in four stages (Section 3.2): Local registers load and record feature map and weight data → Calculation stage performs convolution MAC on buffered data → Activation and classification stage consumes convolution results via ReLU and FC modules → Output stage provides the final classification score and declares completion by asserting output_valid.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Parameterized and Scalable Design

Each module is parameterized, supporting testing and optimization under different model scales and bit-widths (Section 3); the conclusion further declares that the modular architecture makes it possible to extend to deeper models and more convolutional layers (Section 5). Why it is important: A line buffer that only supports a single fixed feature map shape and bit-width has no framework value—changing the row width would require rewriting the buffer depth; parameterization makes row width and bit-width adjustable quantities, making the term "framework" valid. The paper does not report specific supported bit-width levels, maximum feature map sizes, or any parameter scan results.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared with three categories of related work, the technical position of this paper is clear:

1. **Comparison with ROMANet / Shortcut Fusion type general memory management (citing [4])**: Those methods target off-chip memory scheduling and data organization for general DNNs, working at the scheduling layer with high complexity. This paper does only one thing—convolution line buffering for a three-layer small KWS model—directly embedding reuse into the RTL data path rather than the scheduling layer, verifying "how much benefit the smallest memory optimization can yield on the smallest model."
2. **Comparison with Kokkinis & Siozios' TinyML resource estimation methods (citing [13])**: They answer "how to accurately predict on-chip resources and power consumption in the early design stage," while this paper answers "how to write specific RTL and how many reads can be saved in simulation." One manages prediction, the other manages implementation; they are complementary, not competitive.
3. **Comparison with Bae et al.'s binary/ternary DNN hardware (citing [14]) and He et al.'s TENet configurable accelerator (citing [15])**: They approach energy efficiency from numerical precision (binary/ternary weights, low bits) and model structure (time-efficient networks). This paper does not touch numerical precision or model structure at all; the assumption of "not sacrificing accuracy" runs through the entire paper (no accuracy numbers are reported in the entire paper because the model was not changed). This belongs to pure architectural-level memory optimization. The upper limit of this route is constrained by the fact that memory access is only part of the energy consumption, but the benefit is that it is orthogonal to any model compression or quantization scheme and can be used in combination.

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

The paper does not report any speech dataset. There is no mention of training or test sets like Speech Commands or LibriSpeech throughout the text, nor are there recognition accuracy evaluations—verification is completed entirely in the Vivado 2017 simulation environment: the testbench (tb.sv) generates clock and reset signals, initializes feature data and weight data (source, dimensions, and numerical distribution of data are not reported). Two accelerator instances are simulated simultaneously under the same input (Section 3.3). In other words, this is a simulation paper on "hardware behavioral correctness + memory/latency counting," not a "model recognition performance" paper; its experimental object is the data path, not speech.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Two hard metrics (Table 1):

- **Storage Reads per Cycle (reads)**: Baseline is 9, reuse version is 1 (Section 3.3). Rationale: Off-chip reads are the main source of energy consumption and bandwidth pressure. The read count is the proxy metric closest to energy consumption and can be precisely counted in behavioral-level simulation—deterministic numbers can be obtained without synthesizing to the gate level.
- **End-to-End Latency (latency, unit ns)**: Baseline is 100,000 ns, reuse version is 50,000 ns (Section 3.3), defined as the interval between the start pulse and the output_valid pulse (Sections 3.1, 3.3, the testbench is responsible for capturing the time difference between this pair of pulses). Rationale: KWS is always-on, and the latency of a single inference determines whether the pipeline can keep up with a continuous audio stream, making it the most direct real-time metric.

Auxiliary waveform-level verification (Section 3.3): The start signal triggers convolution at 60,000 ns; output_valid appears at 160,000 ns in the baseline and at 110,000 ns in the reuse version—the pulse times of both examples minus the start time exactly equal 100,000 ns and 50,000 ns respectively, consistent with the latency numbers in Table 1; no signal overlap or competitive conditions (original text "competitive conditions," likely a transcription error for race conditions) were observed in the waveforms, confirming timing stability and data integrity.

Metrics Not Evaluated: Recognition accuracy, power consumption (watts or milliwatts figures), resource utilization (LUT/FF/BRAM/DSP counts), clock frequency, throughput (frames per second)—none of these are reported in the paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Strictly speaking, there is only one set of performance comparisons: two instances simulated in parallel under the same testbench and same input—baseline (instance name u_base, naive conv2d.sv) and reuse version (instance name u_reuse, conv2dreuse.sv). Results are summarized in Table 1:

| Metric | Baseline | Reuse | Reduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| reads (reads per cycle) | 9 | 1 | 88.89% |
| latency (end-to-end latency) | 100,000 ns | 50,000 ns | 50.00% |

This "dual-instance parallel simulation with same input" control design is commendable: both designs see bit-identical stimuli, and performance differences can be fully attributed to the single variable of the line buffer, eliminating confusion caused by different input distributions.

Comparison with SOTA: The paper does not perform SOTA comparisons under the same workload and platform. The four "typical application scenarios" in Section 4 (4.1-4.4) are essentially literature review-style scenario positioning, where the numbers given are all from other works: Bae et al. achieved real-time latency of less than 100 milliseconds in smart home scenarios using depthwise separable binary/ternary networks (Section 4.2, citing [14]); He et al.'s TENet configurable accelerator maintained recognition accuracy at about 95% through distributed on-chip buffering (Section 4.3, citing [15]); Reddi et al.'s MLPerf benchmark showed FPGA energy efficiency improvements of 3% to 5% relative to CPU implementations (Section 4.4, citing [9]); Kokkinis & Siozios focused on early resource estimation for multi-layer perceptron accelerators (Section 4.1, citing [13]). These numbers are completely different in caliber from this paper's 88.9%/50% (tasks, platforms, and metrics vary), and cannot be placed in the same table for direct comparison.

An interpretation worth highlighting (author's note): The ratio of read count reduction by 8/9 to latency reduction by 1/2 itself carries information—it indicates that under this simulation setup, the serialization of the convolution read port contributes only about half of the total latency, while the other half is consumed in unoptimized segments such as FC nested loops, FSM state transitions, and handshakes. If the authors had performed a phased latency decomposition (how much each of the convolution/activation/FC segments accounts for), it would have been more valuable for engineering guidance than a single 50% figure, but the paper does not report this decomposition.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not report ablation experiments. The only control variable throughout the text is the on/off state of the line buffer (difference between conv2d.sv and conv2dreuse.sv). There are no bit-width scans, line buffer depth scans, convolution kernel size scans (3×3 vs. other sizes), channel count scans, or comparisons with/without FC layer optimization—these usually constitute an ablation table in similar accelerator papers, all of which are missing here. Section 3 claims "each module is parameterized to test under different model scales and bit-widths," but the main text does not present any corresponding parameter scan results; the parameterized capability remains at the declaration level.

## Main Contributions

1. **A reproducible minimal verification**: On an FPGA accelerator for a three-layer small KWS model (Convolution + ReLU + FC), by using only 3×3 line buffer reuse, the number of off-chip reads per cycle was reduced from 9 to 1 (reduction of 88.89%), and end-to-end latency was reduced from 100,000 ns to 50,000 ns (reduction of 50.00%) (Table 1). The engineering implication of these figures is: memory-centric optimizations yield immediate benefits on TinyML-level workloads, and the cost paid is only a very small buffer at the register level—extremely high cost-performance ratio.
2. **Integration of three-operator pipelining under unified FSM control** (Section 3.1): Convolution, activation, and fully connected layers flow continuously under a single state machine and handshake signals, with intermediate data staying on-chip throughout, inter-layer memory access being zeroed out, while ensuring the correct order of multi-cycle execution and waveform-level timing stability.
3. **Parameterized, modular framework posture** (Section 3, Section 5): Modules can be reconfigured according to model scale and bit-width; the architecture declares scalability to deeper models and more convolutional layers, providing a storage-centric reference starting point for TinyML FPGA accelerators.
4. **Methodological decoupling contribution**: Using a control experiment of "no change in precision, only change in memory access," the paper decouples memory optimization from quantization/pruning-type optimizations in experimental design, demonstrating that they are orthogonal and can be stacked—this leaves a clear interface for subsequent combined optimizations of "line buffer + low-bit quantization."

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

1. **Only the convolution layer is optimized**. The premise of line buffer reuse is sliding window overlap, a structure that exists only in convolution operators; the dense matrix-vector multiplication of the FC layer (weight-dense flow, no overlap for reuse) is completely unoptimized (Section 3.1 only describes the correctness design for FC). The fact that end-to-end latency only halved implies that FC and control segments account for a considerable share of the latency.
2. **Verification at single-channel/shallow-layer scale**. The conclusion admits that recent work focuses on "three-and three-channel operations" (3×3 three-channel scale operations), while real KWS networks (DS-CNN, MatchboxNet types) are multi-channel, multi-layer structures; multi-channel convolution is explicitly listed as future work (Section 5). When extending the line buffer scheme to multi-channels, whether each channel requires a separate line buffer or time-division multiplexing is not discussed in the paper.
3. **No numerical format processing**. No quantization, normalized arithmetic, or adaptive precision (all listed as future work in Section 5), meaning the bit-width costs of weights and activations are retained as is. Compared to contemporary low-bit KWS hardware (e.g., Bae et al.'s binary/ternary schemes [14]), no benefits could have been gained on the storage and bandwidth sides.
4. **Results remain at behavioral-level simulation**. All numbers come from Vivado 2017 simulation (Section 3.3), with no post-synthesis timing reports, no post-routing resource and power reports; "reads" is a behavioral-level count, not a post-synthesis storage port transaction count. The performance of 88.9% under real DRAM controllers, bus arbitration, and clock constraints is unknown.
5. **Read count does not equal energy consumption**. The paper does not report power consumption, only read count reduction; extrapolating 88.9% to "energy reduction of 88.9%" is invalid—computation switching, FC weight搬运, and clock tree energy have not changed. The causal chain direction in the abstract of "reducing off-chip memory access to improve inference performance" is correct, but the magnitude can only be inferred indirectly from read counts.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

1. **No dataset, no accuracy**: The testbench synthesizes data, and the scale, bit-width, and numerical distribution of features and weights are not reported (Section 3.3), making it impossible to evaluate the functional boundaries of the accelerator running real KWS models (whether fixed-point bit-width is sufficient, whether there are overflow/saturation risks).
2. **Baseline is self-implemented**: The control object is the author's own naive version, not a third-party implemented accelerator for the same task; 88.9%/50% is the "optimization magnitude relative to its own naive implementation," not the "leading magnitude relative to industry baselines."
3. **Missing key engineering parameters**: Target device model (only mentioned in the conclusion as future work for Xilinx Zynq or Intel Cyclone), clock frequency, LUT/FF/BRAM/DSP utilization, power consumption and energy efficiency ratio (e.g., microjoules per inference) are not reported in the paper—these are exactly the necessary fields to place this paper in a KWS accelerator comparison table.
4. **Confusing citation annotations (author's verification)**: Section 2.4 states that "Chen et al." research shows that memory-centric optimization can improve energy efficiency by up to 3 times and cites [13], but reference [13] is actually the resource estimation paper by Kokkinis and Siozios; "Smith et al." in Section 4.3 corresponds to [15], which is actually the TENet paper by He, Chen, and Su; Section 4.1 correctly attributes [13] to Kokkinis. The same number has inconsistent attributions in two places. Additionally, the full text's English has obvious transcription traces (e.g., "Mell frequency inverse spectral coefficient" should be a misspelling of MFCC, "competitive conditions" should be race conditions, "fc.sv layer lies pipelined" has awkward word order). Readers should check the original literature before citing these secondary numbers (3x energy efficiency, 95% accuracy, 3-5% improvement).
5. **No ablation, no parameter scan**: Single simulation, single-variable control; although hardware simulation is deterministic and does not involve random seed issues, the "testing under different model scales and bit-widths" promised in Section 3 was not fulfilled with any scan data.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Directions listed by the paper (Section 5): Support for multi-channel convolution and deeper networks; introduction of normalized arithmetic and adaptive precision technologies; addition of dynamic voltage scaling (DVS) and partial reconfiguration to improve energy efficiency and flexibility; deployment of the design to Xilinx Zynq or Intel Cyclone platforms for real-time testing; inclusion of multi-language KWS models and adaptive noise filtering to enhance practicality in edge AI scenarios.

The author supplements three directions:

1. **Weight residency and FC block reuse**: The total amount of weights in small KWS models can usually fit entirely into on-chip BRAM—keeping FC weights resident on-chip and performing output block reuse (partial sum residency accumulation) for matrix-vector multiplication is expected to consume the remaining half of the latency and also optimize FC memory access;
2. **Joint operation with low-bit quantization**: The reuse benefits of line buffers are completely orthogonal to quantization. If parameterized bit-widths are implemented in 8bit/4bit fixed-point and combined with dual-multiply packing in DSP slices, storage and bandwidth can be reduced by another order of magnitude. This is also the specific path for the paper's "adaptive precision" prospect;
3. **Completing evaluation metrics**: End-to-end verification of recognition accuracy on public datasets like Google Speech Commands, and providing post-synthesis resource utilization and energy consumption per inference, converting "read count reduction of 88.9%" into energy efficiency metrics that can be directly compared with other KWS accelerators—this is a necessary prerequisite for this paper's results to enter mainstream comparison tables and a key step from "simulation verification" to "citable benchmark."
