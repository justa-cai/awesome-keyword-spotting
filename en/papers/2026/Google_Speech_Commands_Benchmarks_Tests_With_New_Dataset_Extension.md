## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword spotting (KWS) and limited-vocabulary speech recognition are core technologies for voice interfaces in smart speakers, mobile phones, in-car systems, and home appliances, with artificial neural networks being the most effective methodology. For algorithms to advance, comparison is necessary; for fair comparison, shared test data is required—this is the fundamental reason why the speech domain relies heavily on public datasets. Among these, the Google Speech Commands (GSC) dataset released by Pete Warden in 2018 is the most balanced and widely used, with 2,295 citations on Google Scholar at the time of writing (as stated in the paper's introduction). GSC has two main versions: the first version contains 30 common English words (digits 0–9, yes/no, up/down, cat, dog, and names like Marvin, Sheila, etc.), totaling 64,727 recordings; the second version expands on the first by adding 5 new words (backward, forward, follow, learn, visual), reaching 35 words and 105,829 recordings, with approximately 1,500 to 2,500 recordings per word, achieving overall approximate balance (Section III, Dataset Description). All recordings are uniformly one second in length (silence is padded at the beginning and end for shorter words), sampled at 16 kHz, and mono. Speakers are anonymized, and each speaks only a few words, making this dataset unsuitable for speaker identification.

However, the starting point of this paper is not to propose a new model, but to address a colder reality in the engineering community: **the accessibility, transparency, and reproducibility of benchmark results are collapsing**. The Papers with Code platform, which once served the triad function of "paper + code repository + verifiable result leaderboard," has ceased operations. Its successor, Hugging Face, has lost some previously uploaded results and is still under construction; only the Wayback Machine of the Internet Archive partially preserves old site snapshots, with varying quality. The authors admit in the conclusion that what was thought to be an easy task (running models from the leaderboard) ultimately proved much more difficult—among the 42 entries claiming results on GSC, only 12 were proven to be actually runnable (Section VII, Conclusion). This means that a significant portion of the SOTA figures reported publicly in recent years are now in an "unverifiable" state. The second motivation for the paper is the GSC extended dataset recorded by the authors' team at the Wrocław University of Science and Technology in autumn 2024: using newly recorded audio, guaranteed to have never participated in any model training, to test published models. This is the baseline requirement for testing generalization in neural network research (Section I, Introduction).

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The literature review in Section II of the paper reveals three specific deficiencies. First, **missing code and repository decay**: There were 42 entries related to speech recognition on Papers with Code, several of which could be attributed to the same paper, leaving only 32 truly independent original papers; 13 entries had no code repository at all (not in the paper, nor found via search engines); after removing those without code, 21 remained, and after successive eliminations due to "broken links, missing archives, repositories that cannot run, or irrelevance to the task," only 12 entries were runnable, converging to 4 frameworks. The authors cite two examples of "well-written papers without code": EdgeCRNN (an acoustic feature enhancement KWS model oriented towards edge computing) and the DenseNet+BiLSTM combination scheme. The Triplet Loss representation learning paper, which ranked first on the leaderboard, had an active repository, but could not be reused due to overly complex input pipelines and the necessity of full model retraining.

Second, **missing pre-trained artifacts**: A batch of schemes represented by the ResNet family (broadcasted residual learning, sub-spectral normalization, etc.) are efficient in training for image analysis and suitable for mobile deployment, but none of the ResNet-related entries provide pre-trained models. The "papers do not report" any directly evaluable artifacts, forcing third parties to retrain—yet retraining itself introduces randomness and hyperparameter choices different from the original authors, distorting the comparison.

Third, and most critically pointed out by the paper: **none of the analyzed works attempted to test the trained models on newly created samples** (as explicitly stated at the end of Section II). All studies evaluate within the training/validation/test splits of GSC itself, meaning the "test set" actually consists of in-domain samples from the training distribution. The rankings on the leaderboard therefore cannot answer a naive question: Can these models maintain the reported accuracy when faced with a different batch of recording conditions and speakers? Additionally, the literature review notes the emerging quantum hybrid methods (Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks, QCNN, simulated using the PennyLane framework, where classical convolutional layers reduce dimensions before connecting to a quantum circuit layer Ansatz), which achieved an accuracy of $95.12 \pm 0.18$ on a QPU simulator (citation [9]). Such new paradigms will also need reliable benchmarks for comparison with classical methods in the future—this constitutes the "forward-looking" wing of the paper's motivation.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The paper aims to solve three interlocking challenges simultaneously. First, **benchmark archaeology**: Under the premise that platforms are dead and links are decaying, rescue runnable repositories, accompanying papers, and pre-trained models from Wayback Machine archives, and establish a unified evaluation protocol that works for all models (same hardware, same preprocessing, same metrics), making "re-test numbers" comparable. Second, **construction of a homomorphic extended dataset**: The new dataset must be strictly homomorphic to GSC v2 in terms of vocabulary (all 35 words), duration (one second), sampling rate (16 kHz), and channels (mono), so that models can perform inference directly without any adaptation; meanwhile, recording conditions must be controlled to the ITU-T P.800 standard, thereby fixing the "recording condition variable" to specifically examine the impact of the "speaker variable" (all non-native speakers). Third, **auditing published artifacts without retraining**: Three of the four repositories use the original authors' pre-trained models directly, evaluating only without training, to test "what others actually get their hands on" rather than "what would be ideal after retraining"; the only exception is Howl (no pre-trained model provided), which must be trained from scratch by the authors and accompanied by a separate account of the training process. These three things combined essentially replace "unverifiable numbers on the leaderboard" with "re-runnable, cross-dataset three-column comparison numbers available to anyone."

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The "architecture" of this paper is not a specific network, but a **three-column comparative evaluation framework**: each model obtains an accuracy under three conditions—"document self-reported value" (Acc in doc, the number from the original author's paper or repository documentation), "Google official data re-test value" (Acc on Google, the number the authors ran themselves on GSC), and "extended dataset value" (Acc on our dataset, the number on newly recorded audio). Why three columns instead of two? Only with all three columns can the three effects be disentangled: the difference between doc and Google columns measures "reproduction fidelity" (whether numbers match), the difference between Google and ours columns measures "cross-domain generalization ability" (whether performance drops with new data), and the difference between doc and ours exposes "the credibility of leaderboard rankings on real new data." This is the core design of the paper's methodology, and all conclusions are read from the relative relationships of these three columns.

The objects under test are four public repositories (Table I): ARM Software Team's ML-KWS-for-MCU (with paper [13], Hello edge, the pioneering work of KWS on microcontrollers), ARM's keyword-transformer (with paper [14], KWT), Castorini's Howl open-source wake-word detection system (with paper [15]), and douglas125's SpeechCmdRecognition (with paper [16], neural attention model). The four frameworks cover the main architectural spectrum of KWS and all have active code, which is the hard condition for selection. The authors also assigned a fixed color to each repository throughout the text's charts, which is a pure layout usability design.

The internal composition of each framework is as follows. ML-KWS-for-MCU includes seven neural network architectures—Deep Neural Network (DNN), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) (including Basic LSTM, LSTM, GRU variants), Convolutional Recurrent Network (CRNN), and Depthwise Separable Convolution Network (DS-CNN)—each paired with three resource specifications: S class with approximately 80 KB storage / 6 MOPS compute, M class with approximately 200 KB / 20 MOPS, and L class with approximately 500 KB / 80 MOPS (Section IV-A). This "architecture × size" matrix design allows the evaluation to answer both "which structure is better" and "how much performance is lost with resource reduction" questions, forming the basis for the ablation studies later. The keyword-transformer repository provides pre-trained models, including three self-attention model variants KWT-1/2/3 (Table II gives parameters: KWT-1 has embedding dim 64, MLP dim 256, 1 head, 12 layers, 607K parameters; KWT-2 has 128/512/2 heads/12 layers/2394K; KWT-3 has 192/768/3 heads/12 layers/5261K), as well as the MHAtt-RNN (Multi-Head Attention RNN: using GRU or LSTM as backbone, stacking multi-head attention on the hidden state sequence, balancing long-term dependency modeling and compact structure, positioned between lightweight CNN/RNN and expensive Transformers). Howl adopts the res8 lightweight convolutional architecture—an eight-layer convolutional residual network from the ResNet family—recognizing ten core commands (yes, no, up, down, left, right, on, off, stop, go), with cross-entropy loss function and AdamW optimizer (adaptive learning rate plus weight decay regularization), initial learning rate 0.01, decaying by 0.8 per epoch, for 20 epochs, batch size 64; since the original project did not provide pre-trained models, the authors of this paper trained and evaluated it themselves (Section IV-C). SpeechCmdRecognition's Attention RNN is oriented towards low-compute devices: in the feature extraction phase, one-dimensional temporal convolution captures local patterns of spectral frames, and the acoustic representation is then fed into two layers of bidirectional LSTM to model forward and backward long-term dependencies; training uses the Adam optimizer, initial learning rate 0.001, decaying by 0.4 every 10 epochs, minibatch 64, up to 40 epochs, with early stopping if the validation set shows no improvement for 10 consecutive epochs (Section IV-D).

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

Since this paper is a benchmark re-test study, its mathematical core consists of two parts: the structural mathematics of the tested models, and the metric mathematics of the evaluation system itself.

On the model side, the KWT series is entirely based on self-attention mechanisms, with its core operation being the standard Transformer's scaled dot-product attention $\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k})V$, where queries, keys, and values are obtained via linear projection of mel-spectrogram patches, and the multi-head mechanism divides the $d_k$-dimensional space among $h$ heads for parallel computation before concatenation—this explains the configuration logic in Table II where heads increase from 1 to 3 as the model grows: larger embedding dimensions require more attention subspaces for division of labor. The difference with MHAtt-RNN is that the attention acts on the RNN's hidden state sequence rather than the input patch sequence. DS-CNN adopts depthwise separable convolution, decomposing the operation of standard convolution $K \times K \times C_{in} \times C_{out}$ into per-channel spatial convolution ($K \times K \times C_{in}$) and pointwise channel mixing ($1 \times 1 \times C_{in} \times C_{out}$), reducing the computational load to approximately $1/K + 1/C_{out}$ of the original, which is the arithmetic foundation for its entry into the 80 KB class microcontrollers. Bidirectional LSTM aggregates context from both directions $1..t$ and $t..T$ simultaneously at each time step $t$, concatenating the outputs before sending them to the classification layer.

On the evaluation side, the paper adopts a dual perspective of classification reports and confusion matrices (end of Section IV and Section V). For class $i$: Precision $\mathrm{Precision}_i = TP_i/(TP_i+FP_i)$, measuring what proportion of samples predicted as this class truly belong to it; Recall $\mathrm{Recall}_i = TP_i/(TP_i+FN_i)$, measuring the proportion of true samples of this class that are correctly detected; F1 is the harmonic mean of the two $\mathrm{F1}_i = 2 P_i R_i/(P_i+R_i)$, which is more stable than looking at precision or recall alone when classes are imbalanced; Support is the number of test samples for this class. Overall accuracy is the sum of the diagonal elements of the confusion matrix divided by the total number of samples. The off-diagonal elements of the confusion matrix expose pairwise "which two words are confused"—this is exactly the failure mode that aggregate metrics naturally erase. The paper's most important qualitative finding (the down/no confusion pair) is read from the matrix, not from the accuracy.

Regarding the inference pipeline, KWT evaluation uses a Python script to load the `non_stream.tflite` model via the TensorFlow Lite interpreter, reading class label mappings from a text file line by line to output indices; each audio file is loaded using the `soundfile` library and strictly verified for 16 kHz sampling rate, converted to mono by averaging channels if multi-channel, and then precisely aligned to one second via zero-padding or truncation. The ML-KWS-for-MCU models are stored in frozen TensorFlow computation graph format (.pd) and loaded/ran using a Python script based on TensorFlow. All experiments (including training and inference) were completed on an NVIDIA DGX Station A100: AMD EPYC 7742 processor (64 cores 128 threads, 1.5–2.25 GHz), 512 GB DDR4 ECC memory, four NVIDIA A100 SXM4 80 GB accelerators (CUDA 12.4, driver 550.xx), DGX version Ubuntu, four-node NUMA topology (Section V). Writing down the hardware configuration in such detail is motivated by reproducibility—anyone re-running in an equivalent environment should obtain the same sequence of numbers.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Extended Dataset Strictly Homomorphic to Original Dataset with Controlled Recording Conditions

The construction of the extended dataset is the paper's most substantial technical contribution (Section III). There are four interlocking design decisions. First, **full vocabulary coverage**: 106 students from the Faculty of Information and Communication Technology at the Wrocław University of Science and Technology each pronounced all 35 words of GSC v2 once, resulting in 3,710 new audio files (106 × 35 = 3,710, one utterance per person, full vocabulary, ensuring exactly 106 samples per word in the test, naturally balanced). Why full vocabulary instead of only recording the ten core commands? Because the judgment of unknown/silence classes and the coverage of other 25 words as negative samples all depend on the full vocabulary; without it, the 12-class protocol cannot be reproduced. Second, **controlled acoustic environment**: Recordings were conducted in a room compliant with the ITU-T P.800 standard, using an MXL770 condenser microphone paired with a Focusrite Scarlett Solo sound card. Why controlled? The original GSC dataset has huge variations in microphones, environments, and accents; if the new dataset is also diverse, it would be unclear whether "model performance drop" is due to accent or channel issues. By fixing the recording conditions, the performance difference across datasets can be almost entirely attributed to speaker group differences. Third, **signal specification alignment**: Originally sampled at 512 kHz (as stated in the paper), downsampled to 16 kHz and trimmed to one second during the study, completely consistent with GSC, allowing models to perform inference directly with zero adaptation. Fourth, **non-native speaker group**: Unlike the original dataset which is primarily native English speakers, none of the speakers in the extended set are native English speakers; most are Polish, with some Ukrainian, fluent in English but with varying degrees of accent. This makes the extended set a "cross-accent extrapolation" stress test—a dimension that truly exists in the global deployment of smart speakers but is never tested in internal GSC evaluations.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Pre-trained Model Audit Paradigm Without Retraining

The paper makes a clear choice in its evaluation stance: three of the four frameworks directly use the pre-trained models released by the original authors, and only Howl is trained by this paper because no pre-trained artifact was provided. Why is this important? Leaderboard verification tests "method potential," while what circulates in the ecosystem is "published artifacts"—weight files, TFLite models, label mappings. Testing new data directly with pre-trained artifacts yields the experience users will truly encounter; if one re-trains first and then tests, what is measured is only architectural capability, not delivery quality. Correspondingly, the paper unifies preprocessing into a narrow and rigid pipeline (16 kHz verification, channel averaging, one-second alignment) and runs all inference on a single piece of hardware, eliminating "engineering-induced bias from the re-tester" which is the most common source of irreproducibility. This paradigm rewrites "reproduction research" from "re-running training scripts" to "auditing published artifacts," offering methodological reference for subsequent benchmark maintenance work.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Per-Class Confusion Pair Diagnosis and Three-Column Reading Method

The paper does not just report a single total accuracy, but provides confusion matrices for representative models one by one (Tables VI, VII, IX–XIV, XVI–XVII), and extracts transferable qualitative judgments from the matrices: the confusion between down and no repeatedly appears in CRNN (Table VII: 12 samples in the down row predicted as no) and KWT-1 (Table IX), which the authors attribute to the shared temporal dynamic characteristics of the two words; DNN, on the other hand, makes massive bidirectional misjudgments on acoustically or temporally similar pairs such as yes/no, up/down, left/right (Table VI: 23 samples in the up row predicted as down, 28 in the down row predicted as no, 44 in the go row predicted as no, 22 in the yes row falling into unknown). Combined with the aforementioned "doc / Google / ours" three-column reading method, the paper essentially provides a small benchmark audit syntax: first check if the three columns align, then check if the failure patterns in the confusion matrix are reproduced across architectures.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Compared to existing research, the differences in this paper are reflected in four points. First, unlike the self-reported evaluations of original papers: this paper does not train (for three/four repositories), only infers, changing the meaning of the numbers from "performance reachable by this method" to "actual performance of this published artifact." Second, unlike leaderboards: this paper first performs a runnability filter (42 → 12 → 4), recording the reason for elimination at each step (no code, link failure, repository error, or irrelevant to the task), effectively performing a "stress test" on the leaderboard. Third, unlike conventional benchmark papers: this paper introduces an extended dataset newly created according to the original authors' recording specifications, upgrading "evaluation within in-domain splits" to "generalization testing on newly recorded audio," directly filling the gap confirmed by the literature review. Fourth, unlike dataset papers: the extended set does not pursue scale (3,710 entries is far smaller than GSC's hundred-thousand level), but pursues strict homomorphism with the original set and controlled conditions, positioning it as an "audit probe" rather than a "training resource." Incidentally, the paper's outlook on the quantum direction (QCNN, etc.) also positions this benchmark as the foundation for future quantum-classical comparisons.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Three datasets are involved in the experiments. First, Google Speech Commands Version 1: 30 words, 64,727 recordings. Second, Version 2: 35 words (adding backward, forward, follow, learn, visual), 105,829 recordings, approximately 1,500–2,500 per word, all one-second, 16 kHz, mono recordings (Section III). Third, this paper's extended set: 106 non-native (Polish/Ukrainian) speakers × 35 words × 1 utterance = 3,710 recordings, recorded in an ITU-T P.800 standard room with an MXL770 condenser microphone and Focusrite Scarlett Solo sound card, originally sampled at 512 kHz, downsampled to 16 kHz, and trimmed to one second. When performing 12-class evaluation on the extended set, each of the ten core commands has exactly 106 samples (the diagonals of confusion matrices Tables VII, X, XIII are all 106 or slightly lower), while the silence and unknown classes have no test samples (corresponding rows are all zeros); when evaluating on GSC, the support for each command reaches thousands (diagonals in Table XIV range from 2,846 to 3,186). It should be noted that the paper does not report which official split of v2 was used for the GSC re-test (training/validation/test lists), nor does it report which set the support numbers of approximately 368–410 per class in the Attention RNN confusion matrices (Tables XVI/XVII) correspond to, which is a gap in reproducibility.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metrics adopt a combination of classification reports (precision, recall, F1, support) and confusion matrices (end of Section IV). The rationale stated in the paper is straightforward: aggregate metrics evaluate overall effect, while confusion matrices expose "failure patterns at the single-class level"; combining both allows one to see both the total score and the短板 (shortcomings); the diagonal represents correct classification, off-diagonal represents misclassification, allowing one to find hidden dangers such as "high overall accuracy but low recall for a certain class." The main text presents accuracy and confusion matrices as the primary focus; complete per-class precision/recall tables are not reported in the paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**ML-KWS-for-MCU (Tables III/V, S/M/L three classes)**: Performance on the extended set presents three forms. Form one, DNN collapses completely—S class document self-reported 0.846, GSC re-test 0.8525, extended set drops to 0.5670; M class 0.864/0.8801/0.5943; L class 0.867/0.8967/0.6509. Even increasing the model size to 500 KB/80 MOPS, the extended set still drops nearly 25 percentage points compared to GSC; the conclusion section explicitly states this implies overfitting and that this model should be disqualified (drop magnitude approaching 30 percentage points). Form two, CNN partially drops—S class 0.916/0.9158/0.8774 (extended set is actually about 3.8 percentage points lower than GSC), but M and L classes recover to 0.9377 and 0.9500. Form three, other architectures do not drop but rise on the extended set: Basic LSTM three classes are 0.9453/0.9575/0.9538, LSTM is 0.9509/0.9623/0.9538, GRU is 0.9670/0.9642/0.9736, CRNN is 0.9726/0.9670/0.9764, DS-CNN is 0.9519/0.9500/0.9632—generally 2–3 percentage points higher than the GSC re-test values, many even exceeding the original authors' self-reported values. The gap between GSC re-test and document self-report is generally within 1 percentage point (conclusion section), indicating high reproduction fidelity of these published artifacts. Two structural observations are worth emphasizing: first, in each class, recurrent models (GRU/CRNN) achieve the highest scores; the more advanced the architecture, the more stable the results; second, some S-class models (e.g., CRNN-S 0.9726, GRU-S 0.9670) reach or even exceed their own M/L class performance, indicating that some low-complexity models are equally effective and are preferred options for resource-constrained deployment. At the confusion matrix level, DNN (Table VI) is riddled with errors: 23 samples in the up row predicted as down, 19 falling into unknown, 16 predicted as stop, 12 predicted as off; 28 samples in the down row predicted as no, 29 falling into unknown; 44 samples in the go row predicted as no, 21 falling into unknown, with only 33 correct. CRNN (Table VII) is almost entirely correct, with the only large-scale error being 12 samples in the down row predicted as no, along with sporadic errors such as off→up 6, stop→up 4, go→no 5.

**Keyword Transformer (Table VIII)**: All four models demonstrate extremely high accuracy and robustness. KWT-1: GSC 0.9922 / Doc 0.9856 / Extended 0.9972; KWT-2: 0.9988 / 0.9843 / **1.0000**; KWT-3: 0.9995 / 0.9808 / 0.9991; MHAtt-RNN: 0.9947 / 0.9836 / 0.9981. The differences among the three KWTs on the extended set are within a few thousandths—the three scales of 607K, 2394K, and 5261K parameters hardly affect the results, with the smallest KWT-1 tying with the largest KWT-3; all models exceed 0.98 on both GSC and the extended set, and re-test values are generally higher than document self-reports. KWT-2 achieves perfect classification on the extended set (Table X: ten commands all 106/106 on the diagonal, no misjudgments anywhere), and the authors cautiously offer two explanations: the model fits the characteristics of this test set particularly well, or it is highly robust to acoustic variations. KWT-1 (Table IX) has only sporadic single-digit errors, with the main text specifically pointing out the down–no confusion pair; KWT-3 (Table XI) has only one error: 1 sample in the go row predicted as on (105/106). MHAtt-RNN (Table XII) also has only sporadic single-sample errors. The paper judges the KWT series to be the most stable and robust among the four frameworks, corroborating its fifth-place ranking on the old leaderboard.

**Howl (Section VI-C, Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Tables XIII, XIV)**: The only model trained from scratch in this paper. Training curves are healthy: loss drops rapidly in the first few epochs, validation loss stays close to training loss with no obvious divergence, showing no signs of overfitting (Fig. 1); training accuracy exceeds 0.95 within a few epochs, gradually approaching about 0.97–0.98, and from about the fifth epoch onwards, training/validation curves almost overlap, converging stably without oscillation (Fig. 2). On the extended set (Table XIII), it is almost entirely correct: yes/no/left each 106 perfect scores, down 104, right 103, on 105, off 104 (1 predicted as go), stop 105 (1 predicted as go), go 104 (1 predicted near no), with errors all at the single-sample level. On GSC (Table XIV), the diagonals for each main command reach 2,800–3,200 correct classifications (yes 3186, no 3034, up 2846, down 3041, left 2955, right 2942, on 2989, off 2873, stop 3058, go 3018), with the vast majority of errors being single occurrences; the largest deviation is 81 samples in the no row predicted as left, with other large-scale misjudgments including up→off 20, off→up 25, on→off 23, off→on 23, up→on 10, all related to the acoustic similarity of word pairs and the high diversity of GSC recordings. The conclusion section summarizes: Howl's validation accuracy on both the original data and the extended set is close to 100%, confirming the quality of this framework (fourth place on the old leaderboard, highest rank in this actual test).

**SpeechCmdRecognition / Attention RNN (Table XV)**: The most anomalous performance. Document self-report 0.9390, extended set 0.9353 (almost consistent with self-report), but on GSC re-test only 0.7845—about 15.5 percentage points lower than self-report. The authors' explanation path is worth reading carefully: this model performs well on both "the dataset the document is based on" and this paper's extended set, but performs poorly on the original GSC, indicating sensitivity to acoustic consistency between training and test data; GSC has huge variations in microphones, environments, and accents, while the data used for retraining is quite homogeneous, so this model may require stronger generalization techniques (larger-scale data augmentation or transfer learning) to remain stable under wide acoustic conditions. In other words, high scores on the extended set do not always equal strong generalization—when the training data itself is homogeneous, "another homogeneous test set" will give inflated readings, which is a hidden but important methodological warning in the paper. Its confusion matrix (Tables XVI/XVII, selecting ten classes) shows 409 yes, 379 no, 386 up, 378 down, 388 left, 377 right, 368 on, 373 off, 404 stop, 374 go correct, with the main text pointing out that diagonals are generally over 360, with stop and yes exceeding 400, indicating that the attention mechanism effectively captured acoustically distinctive command words; main errors include up→off 17, down→go 7, off→up 8, on→off 6, go→down 6.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not report traditional component-level ablation experiments (no gradual removal of modules, no controlled replacement of modules), which is a natural attribute of re-test papers. However, the evaluation matrix embeds two groups of "de facto ablations," whose findings are quite informative. The first group is **capacity ablation**: the S/M/L three classes of ML-KWS and the 607K/2394K/5261K three classes of KWT are both controlled capacity scans. The results overwhelmingly show that capacity is not the bottleneck for cross-dataset generalization; architecture is. DNN from S to L (0.5670 → 0.5943 → 0.6509) remains stuck in the 0.57–0.65 interval on the extended set, with five times the storage offering no salvation; CRNN and GRU achieve 0.9670–0.9726 even in the smallest S class; all three KWT classes are squeezed in the narrow band of 0.997–1.000. The second group is **architecture family ablation**: the ranking DNN → CNN → RNN/GRU → CRNN → DS-CNN → Transformer is consistent in direction on both datasets under the same framework, same training protocol, and same size class, but the magnitude is amplified on the extended set—pure feedforward DNN drops 30 percentage points, while models with temporal modeling or attention actually rise 2–3 percentage points. Additionally, confusion pair ablation (the same down–no confusion reproduced in CRNN and KWT-1) suggests this is an acoustic-temporal attribute of the word pair itself, rather than a defect of a specific model.

## Main Contributions

The paper's contributions can be summarized in five points. First, **rescued and reconstructed a reproducible slice of the GSC benchmark**: Against the backdrop of Papers with Code shutting down and results being lost, 12 runnable entries were screened from archives and systematic re-testing of 4 frameworks was completed, replacing "unverifiable leaderboard numbers" with re-runnable numbers unified in hardware, preprocessing, and metrics, and the doc/Google/ours three-column comparison allows reproduction fidelity and cross-domain generalization to be measured separately for the first time. Second, **published an extended dataset strictly homomorphic to GSC v2**: 106 non-native speakers, full 35-word vocabulary, 3,710 entries, ITU-T P.800 controlled recording, specifications completely aligned with the original set, directly usable for auditing the cross-accent extrapolation ability of any GSC model. Third, **provided an empirical ranking of cross-architecture generalization ability**: Self-attention (KWT, including KWT-2 achieving 100% perfect score on the extended set) and attention-mixed architectures are the most stable, the recurrent family (GRU/CRNN) is second best and small models suffice, while pure DNN collapses by nearly 30 percentage points under acoustic condition changes and should be disqualified. Fourth, **established an "audit paradigm of testing new recordings directly with pre-trained published artifacts"**, shifting the object of reproduction research from method to delivered artifact, and demonstrating the benchmark audit syntax of three-column reading + confusion matrix. Fifth, **laid the benchmark foundation for quantum speech processing**: Explicitly pointing out that quantum hybrid methods (QCNN, etc.) entering the speech domain will need reliable comparison with classical SOTA, with this benchmark providing the classical side reference system for future comparisons.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

The extended dataset itself has three limitations. First, **single accent source and narrow group**: All 106 people come from the same university, primarily Polish native speakers with a few Ukrainian native speakers, representing only a slice of "Slavic-accented English," with limited extrapolation power for global deployment scenarios (East Asian, South Asian, Western European accents, children and elderly speakers). Second, **only one utterance per word per person**: Among the 3,710 recordings, there is no intra-speaker repetition, making it impossible to distinguish "model systematic failure on a certain speaker" from "model sporadic failure on that specific utterance by a speaker," nor to estimate intra-speaker variance; single-utterance sampling means the 106 samples per word are actually 106 independent but non-repeated observations. Third, **controlled environment brings homogeneity bias**: A single microphone, single room, and single sound card make the extended set acoustically highly consistent; this is both the design intent (isolating the speaker variable) and a weakness—the results of Howl and Attention RNN have already demonstrated that homogeneous test sets may give inflated readings, and "high scores on the extended set" cannot be directly read as "strong generalization." Fourth, the scale is only 3,710 entries, sufficient only for a test probe, not enough to serve as fine-tuning or training resources.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

There are several gaps and flaws in the experimental reporting. The most obvious is that **Tables XVI and XVII have identical values**: one labeled as the confusion matrix for Attention RNN, the other labeled as the confusion matrix for that model on GSC, both having identical diagonals (409/379/386/378/388/377/368/373/404/374) for the ten classes and all off-diagonal elements one by one, while Table XV clearly reports a 15 percentage point difference in accuracy between the two datasets (0.9353 vs 0.7845); moreover, the support numbers of approximately 368–410 per class in these two tables match neither the 106 per class of the extended set nor the ~3,000 per class magnitude shown in Table XIV for GSC—calculating the overall accuracy roughly from the matrices gives about 0.97, which also does not match any reported value; the two tables are likely products of repeated layout or misplacement. Second, **key evaluation protocol details are not reported**: which official split was used for GSC re-test, the sample base for "Acc on Google," and the source of support numbers for the Attention RNN matrix are not explained by the paper; per-class precision/recall/F1 were declared as collected in the methodology section, but complete tables are not presented in the main text. Third, **no statistical significance analysis**: all comparisons are single point estimates, with no confidence intervals, no multiple-seed repetitions (the $95.12 \pm 0.18$ in the literature review is a number cited from another paper, and all numbers in this paper have no error bars); KWT-2's 100% perfect score on 1,060 samples lacks confidence interval support. Fourth, **attribution lacks controlled experiments**: DNN dropping 30 percentage points on the extended set is directly attributed to overfitting, but no controlled experiments such as removing microphone channel effects or re-evaluating with data augmentation were performed; alternative explanations (differences in capacitor microphone frequency response vs GSC consumer-grade devices, uneven impact of non-native vowel shifts on pure fully connected structures) were not excluded; similarly, the counter-intuitive data point of CNN-S being about 3.8 percentage points lower on the extended set than GSC is not discussed in the main text. Fifth, **original sampling rate recorded as 512 kHz** (Section III), far exceeding common recording equipment specifications, suspected to be a typo for 48 kHz; as per the original text, readers should remain skeptical. Sixth, only 4 of the 12 runnable entries were tested; the screening criteria were not fully quantified, and high-ranking schemes like Triplet Loss were abandoned due to engineering complexity, leaving gaps in the coverage of benchmark reconstruction.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The directions pointed out by the authors themselves focus on quantum speech processing: the team has investigated preliminary schemes such as using annealing quantum frameworks to extract formant features and resonance angle quantum neural networks for vowel recognition, planning to explore hybrid quantum-classical architectures combining classical convolutional layers with quantum kernel methods, and pipelines where data is first dimension-reduced by classical autoencoders before entering quantum processors; and emphasizing that these schemes must be evaluated on real quantum hardware and simulation environments accurately modeling noise characteristics of the NISQ era, at which time they need to be compared with classical SOTA on reliable benchmarks like this one (conclusion section, citing their team's quantum machine learning speech acoustics review at CINTI 2025). On the classical side, natural extensions from this paper's data include: expanding the extended dataset to a larger group with multiple accents, ages, and devices, and adding intra-speaker repetition; supplementing each model with multiple-seed and confidence interval reports; systematically testing the remedial magnitude of data augmentation and transfer learning for DNN-sensitive models; completing protocol details such as GSC official splits and support numbers to make re-testing fully reproducible; and institutionalizing "new recording audits"—any new leaderboard should be accompanied by readings from a probe set that has never participated in training. A deeper implication is at the infrastructure level: the death of Papers with Code proves that hosting community benchmarks on a single commercial platform is fragile; decentralized, archivable, verifiable benchmark repositories are public goods that the field needs to supplement, and this is the finallanding point of the term "benchmark tests" in the title of this paper.
