## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Smart speakers, AI assistants, digital human interfaces, and service robots have seen rapid adoption in recent years, driving a surge in demand for human-machine voice communication. Personalized voice entry technology has become a necessity. User-Defined Keyword Spotting (UDKWS) is a key capability in this domain: users are no longer satisfied with vendor-provided wake words and desire the freedom to customize their own wake phrases. The true practical test for UDKWS arises in barge-in scenarios—where the user speaks while the device is simultaneously playing audio, causing the two sound sources to overlap at the microphone. Because the microphone is physically much closer to the device's speaker than to the user's mouth, this overlap is far more destructive to keyword spotting systems than general noise: it can range from a collapse in detection rates to self-referencing issues, where the device mistakenly identifies its own played wake word as the user calling it, thereby waking itself up. The paper provides a concrete example: when a phone generates and plays prompt sounds like "Hey Siri" or "Hi Bixby," the KWS system should remain silent, but a model relying solely on microphone input will almost certainly trigger falsely. This problem is not only fatal to KWS but also to Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and device-directedness detection (determining whether a sentence is addressed to the device).

The traditional weapon against barge-in issues is Acoustic Echo Cancellation (AEC). The principle of AEC is as follows: the device knows what it is playing (the audio-out signal serves as a reference), estimates the room impulse response from the speaker to the microphone, and subtracts the played signal from the mixed signal captured by the microphone. There are two research trajectories in this area: the signal processing route represented by adaptive filters and Kalman filters, and the neural network route that has emerged in recent years. A landmark conclusion from the ICASSP 2021 and 2022 AEC challenges is that improvements in AEC models not only enhance echo cancellation itself but also boost downstream ASR performance. However, traditional AEC has a hard constraint in deployment: it typically uses the audio-out signal as a reference and embeds its workflow into hardware components such as microphone modules, making it effective only on devices equipped with such specific hardware. To provide consistent AEC capabilities across various in-service personal devices with disparate hardware specifications, a software-level implementation is required. However, the computational overhead of software-level AEC is a significant burden for always-on, lightweight KWS tasks. This constitutes a typical conflict in the engineering triangle of "accuracy, speed, and efficiency": the "accuracy" of echo cancellation traditionally relies on an independent AEC module, while the "efficiency" of KWS does not allow for a heavyweight module at the front end. Consequently, the iAEC (implicit AEC) route, which integrates AEC implicitly into the KWS model, has emerged in recent years. This paper represents the implementation of this route in zero-shot UDKWS.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

First, the mainstream approach for early UDKWS was the query-by-example route: the user first records a registration audio, and the system determines whether to wake up by comparing the similarity between the registration audio and the input audio. The fundamental inconvenience of this route lies in the registration process itself—the user must record audio for each new wake word, resulting in a fragmented user experience, and the quality of the registration audio directly determines the performance. To address this, the academic community has shifted toward zero-shot UDKWS, which changes keywords via text input without any additional training, leading to significant performance improvements in the last two years. However, the paper points out a common weakness in these models: they accept only single-channel microphone input, and their performance degrades immediately when human voice overlaps with device-played audio.

Second, connecting traditional AEC as a cascaded front-end to KWS seems like the most direct solution, but the paper falsifies this route through experiments (Section 4.3, Table 1). They connected the NLMS (Normalized Least Mean Squares) adaptive filter, widely used in the AEC field, to the front of the baseline model, using parameter settings from the literature. The results showed no significant performance improvement in Music and Speech playback scenarios, nor any significant enhancement in self-referencing scenarios. In Table 1, the baseline's self-referencing MAE was 73.97%, 71.72%, 82.75%, and 85.40% across the four test sets. After connecting NLMS, these values only dropped to 72.55%, 69.86%, 72.91%, and 72.12%. The model's average output remained above 0.7, meaning it triggered every time audio was played. The paper explains that residual echoes not filtered by AEC are still detected by the KWS model. Moreover, if the improvement direction makes the UDKWS model more robust to echoes (more likely to classify speech as a wake word), it may worsen false triggers in self-referencing scenarios. This finding is one of the most valuable negative pieces of evidence in the paper: cascaded AEC cannot save KWS in barge-in scenarios.

Third, existing iAEC works (e.g., Cornell et al., SLT 2022) embed echo cancellation within the KWS model, which is the correct approach, but their mask generation sub-network consists of dense layers: the mixed signal embedding and the played signal embedding are concatenated along the embedding dimension, passed through a fully connected layer and sigmoid, and generate a mask frame by frame. The paper points out the limitation of this frame-by-frame linear mapping—it cannot account for time delays. The played signal is emitted from the speaker, propagates through the room, and re-enters the input signal via the microphone with a propagation delay. The echo of the played signal from frame $t-k$ is mixed into the microphone embedding at frame $t$, while the dense mask can only see the two embeddings at the same time index, making it naturally mismatched. Additionally, this structure has a high parameter overhead (Mask Subnet D configuration in Table 1 has 688K parameters, about 5% more than the baseline's 655K).

Fourth, the dependency issue at the training data level. Most echo cancellation training schemes require clean target signals as supervision (e.g., constructing ideal mask labels using clean speech). However, real edge devices cannot obtain the user's clean speech—they only have access to the speaker output and the microphone input. An AEC scheme that requires a clean target for training is a castle in the air under real deployment conditions.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The paper aims to simultaneously balance four mutually constraining objectives: First, embed echo cancellation capabilities implicitly into the KWS model, with the parameter cost kept negligible (the abstract states the model increases by only 0.13%); otherwise, the budget for always-on operation on edge devices would not allow it. Second, in self-referencing scenarios where the ground truth for all samples is 0, the model's average output (i.e., the MAE for this scenario) must be reduced from the baseline level of 0.7 to 0.85 to near zero. This is a veto item for "accuracy." Third, clean target signals must not be used during training; only mixed signals with labels for the target speech are allowed, to match real device conditions. Fourth, anti-echo capabilities must not degrade the basic detection performance in echo-free scenarios; non-playback scenarios allow only minimal performance regression. Together, these four constraints dictate that the solution must be "embedding a very small, delay-aware, explicitly unsupervised mask module into an existing efficient model," rather than attaching an external AEC front-end.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall architecture of iPhonMatchNet (Fig. 1) consists of five modules: a shared audio encoder, a text encoder, a Refiner, a Pattern Extractor, and a Pattern Discriminator. The model takes three inputs: the microphone mixed signal, the device playback signal, and the keyword text; the output determines whether "the mixed signal after removing the playback signal matches the given text." The paper explicitly states that all components and hyperparameters, except for the Refiner, are exactly the same as its predecessor, PhonMatchNet. The motivation behind this design decision is worth expanding: on one hand, it ensures strict comparability with the baseline, allowing performance differences in ablation studies to be cleanly attributed to the Refiner module alone; on the other hand, from an engineering perspective, it means that existing PhonMatchNet deployments can be upgraded at nearly zero cost—the detection backbone is not rebuilt, but rather a small echo-aware sub-network is inserted into the embedding stream, allowing "echo removal" and "detection" to be jointly optimized under the same loss function. This is the design philosophy of the entire paper: AEC is not an independent task, but an internal step in the KWS discrimination process.

Fig. 1 provides a贯穿 example: the user says "Hallow" (phoneme sequence HH, AE1, L, OW0), while the device is simultaneously playing "Hello" (HH, EH1, L, OW0), and the registered text is "Hello" (HH, EH1, L, OW0). The model must attribute the acoustic components of "Hello" in the mixed signal to the device playback rather than the user's pronunciation, and then determine whether the user's speech matches the text. This example cleverly demonstrates the subtlety of the task: the phoneme sequences of the two words in the mixed signal overlap significantly, differing only in the middle vowel. If the frame-by-frame mask is not aligned in time, the attribution will be incorrect.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

According to the notation in Section 2 of the paper: the mixed signal and the playback signal are encoded by the shared audio encoder to obtain embeddings $E_m$ and $E_p$, both of which are $T_a \times 128$ matrices ($T_a$ is the time length, 128 is the embedding dimension); the text is encoded by the text encoder to obtain $E_t$, which is $T_t \times 128$.

The shared audio encoder (Section 2.1) uses two feature extractors: one is a pre-trained speech embedder (from Reference 19, which trained a keyword detector using limited and synthesized speech) responsible for representing general pronunciations; the other is a fully trainable extractor responsible for adapting to special pronunciations. Subsequently, 1D transposed convolution upsampling is used to produce 128-dimensional feature vectors. The mixed signal and the playback signal share this single encoder, with the purpose of "ensuring consistency in the embedding space of the two signals"—only in the same space does frame-by-frame mask multiplication have geometric meaning. The text encoder (Section 2.1) follows the approach in Reference 16: a pre-trained Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) model followed by a fully connected layer and ReLU, extracting the G2P embedding from the last hidden state of the encoder.

The Refiner is the true incremental contribution of this paper (Section 2.2). Its function can be summarized in one sentence: use $E_m$ and $E_p$ to generate a mask, multiply it element-wise with $E_m$ to obtain the enhanced embedding $E_a$. The paper describes this process in text without explicit formulas, but it can be formalized as: $E_a = E_m \odot M$, where $M = \sigma(f(E_m, E_p))$, $\sigma$ is sigmoid, and $f$ is the mask sub-network, with two configurations:

- Mask Subnet D (from Reference 12): Concatenate $E_m$ and $E_p$ along the embedding dimension to form $T_a \times 256$ (labeled Concat [$T_a$, 256] in Fig. 1), pass it through a Dense layer to map to $T_a \times 128$, and then through Sigmoid to get the mask. This is a linear mapping plus non-linear compression per time frame, where the mask for each frame depends only on the embeddings of the two signals in the same frame.
- Mask Subnet C (proposed in this paper): Stack $E_m$ and $E_p$ to form a two-channel $T_a \times 128 \times 2$ tensor (labeled Concat [$T_a$, 128, 2] in Fig. 1), and sequentially pass it through Conv2D (8@3×3), ReLU, Conv2D (8@3×3), ReLU, Conv2D (1@3×3), and Sigmoid, outputting a $T_a \times 128$ mask. That is, treat the (time × embedding) embedding map as a single-channel image, with the two signals as two channels, and use a 3×3 2D convolution kernel to slide jointly over the time-embedding plane.

The Pattern Extractor (Section 2.3) calculates scaled dot-product self-attention on the enhanced embedding $E_a$ and the text embedding $E_t$: the two single-modal embeddings are concatenated along the time dimension to form a joint embedding $E_j$, followed by self-attention, and a lower triangular matrix is used as the attention mask to preserve causal information within each modality. The Pattern Discriminator (Section 2.4) outputs two probabilities: the sentence-level matching probability is obtained by processing $E_j$ along the time dimension with a 128-dimensional GRU layer, taking the final hidden state, passing it through a fully connected layer and sigmoid; the phoneme-level matching probability is obtained by slicing the phoneme sequence from $E_j$ and passing it through a fully connected layer and sigmoid. The training criterion (Section 2.5) is exactly the same as in Reference 17, with the ground truth for both sentence-level and phoneme-level detection losses taken from the labels of the target speech in the mixed signal.

The most noteworthy point at the mathematical level is that the mask $M$ has no explicit supervision throughout the entire process. There are no ideal ratio mask labels, no clean speech as a regression target; the echo cancellation capability is completely inferred backward from the end-to-end detection loss—this is the precise meaning of the word "implicit" in the title. The premise for its validity is that the dual-granularity discrimination heads provide sufficiently dense supervision signals: the phoneme-level loss pushes gradients to the frame granularity, forcing the mask to learn to distinguish "which phoneme in the device echo does the energy of this frame belong to"; the sentence-level loss ensures the overall discrimination direction. Another point is the rationality of the mask acting in the embedding domain rather than the spectral domain: the 128-dimensional embedding is much lower-dimensional than the original spectrum. Echo at the encoder output approximates the delayed superposition and distortion of the playback embedding, allowing the mask sub-network to be extremely small—this is the structural reason why the parameter cost can be compressed to 0.13%.

The mathematical formulation of the delay problem can be stated more clearly. Let the microphone signal be the sum of the user's voice and the playback signal convolved with the room impulse response. The simulation in Section 3.2 samples the propagation delay between 0.01 and 0.1 seconds. This means that the component of the playback signal from frame $t-k$ is mixed into the microphone embedding at frame $t$, where the value of $k$ varies with the delay and frame rate. The functional form of the dense mask determines that it can only establish frame-to-frame pairing relationships (the mask for frame $t$ is determined only by the embeddings of the two signals in frame $t$), making it powerless against misalignments where $k \neq 0$; whereas the 3×3 convolution kernel naturally covers adjacent time frames, and stacking two layers further expands the receptive field, effectively giving the mask sub-network an implicit sliding window registration capability. It should be noted that the paper does not report the time frame rate of the embeddings, so it is impossible to calculate how many frames 0.1 seconds of delay corresponds to, and whether the convolutional receptive field covers the worst-case delay cannot be verified. This point is discussed in the Limitations section.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Delay-Aware Convolutional Mask Subnet (Mask Subnet C)

This is the core innovation of the entire paper, with the motivation stated plainly in the paper: the playback signal inevitably re-enters the microphone with a time delay, and dense layers cannot account for this delay, so convolutional layers are used to capture distortions in the time dimension (Section 2.2). The ablation in Section 4.2 provides strong backing: Mask Subnet D and Mask Subnet C have significantly different parameter counts, but their performance trends are opposite—C, with fewer parameters, wins comprehensively. The reason is attributed to the fact that the relationship between the mixed signal and the playback signal itself "leans towards convolution rather than linear layers."

The parameter accounting is worth doing (calculated based on the structure labeled in Fig. 1): The Dense layer in configuration D maps 256 dimensions to 128 dimensions, with weights plus biases totaling approximately 33,000 parameters; the three 3×3 convolutional layers in configuration C have a parameter magnitude of $3 \times 3 \times 2 \times 8$ plus $3 \times 3 \times 8 \times 8$ plus $3 \times 3 \times 8 \times 1$, totaling approximately 800. The difference is about forty times, which precisely matches the total model parameter difference reported in Table 1 (688K minus 656K equals 32K). In other words, C bought delay modeling capability with one-fortieth of D's parameters and simultaneously improved performance—in the context of edge models, this is a rare case of achieving both "efficiency" and "accuracy."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Shared Encoder and Dual-Extractor Design

The mixed signal and the playback signal sharing the same audio encoder is not just a parameter-saving move, but a prerequisite for the mask mechanism: the two embeddings must fall into the same geometric space, so that frame-by-frame multiplication by the mask is doing addition/subtraction of the same thing rather than a misaligned dialogue between two spaces. The encoder internally retains the dual-branch design of a pre-trained speech embedder plus a fully trainable extractor, with clear division of labor—the zero-shot keyword requirement demands generalization to unseen pronunciations, which relies on the universal representation of the pre-trained embedder; while the playback path undergoes speaker re-radiation and room coloring, shifting the training distribution, which is absorbed by the trainable branch. The two branches are upsampled and merged into a 128-dimensional output, providing a unified interface for all subsequent modules.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: iAEC Training Without Clean Signals

The end of Section 3.2 specifically emphasizes the construction principle of training data: although clean speech data is actually available, only the simulated output mixed signals are used during training, with the ground truth being the labels of the target speech in the mixed signal. The reason is that real devices can only record the speaker output and the microphone input, and cannot obtain the user's clean speech. The significance of this self-imposed limitation is twofold: first, it strictly aligns training conditions with deployment conditions, so the model learns "how to suppress echoes when only noisy mixed signals and reference signals are available," rather than relying on a clean target that does not exist at deployment; second, it explains why mask learning must be implicit—without a clean reference, ideal mask labels cannot be constructed, and the only remaining supervision signal is the detection loss. The paper lists this as one of its three major contributions and reiterates in the conclusion that "achieving competitive performance without using clean user speech reflects the real deployment conditions of smart devices."

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Transfer and Reuse of Dual-Granularity Discrimination Heads

The dual-head discrimination of sentence-level GRU plus phoneme-level fully connected layers is inherited from PhonMatchNet and is not new in this paper, but the fact that it operates on enhanced embeddings changes its role: the phoneme-level head becomes the most direct supervisor of mask quality. If the mask mistakenly kills frames of user speech, the phoneme-level matching probability drops immediately, and gradients flow simultaneously to the mask sub-network and the shared encoder. This structure of "discriminator feeding back to enhancer" is同源 to the idea in speech enhancement of training enhancers end-to-end using ASR losses, but with much lower implementation costs.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to hardware-embedded AEC: Traditional solutions embed AEC into microphone module hardware, benefiting only devices with specific components, leading to inconsistent cross-device experiences; iPhonMatchNet only requires the software layer to have access to the audio-out reference signal, with the detection model digesting the echo itself, decoupling from hardware specifications. Compared to cascaded software AEC (NLMS front-end plus KWS): Experiments in Section 4.3 of the paper prove that the cascaded scheme has no significant benefits in playback scenarios and has no solution for self-referencing scenarios (residual echoes still trigger the model), while the embedded iAEC reduces the self-referencing MAE from above 0.7 to below 0.15; the essence of the difference is that in the cascaded scheme, no one covers the errors of AEC, while in the embedded scheme, echo removal and discrimination are jointly optimized, and errors can be perceived by the discrimination head and propagated back. Compared to iAEC by Cornell et al. (Reference 12): This paper points out that its dense mask cannot handle delays and has excessive parameters; after switching to a convolutional mask, parameters dropped from 688K to 656K, and performance was comprehensively better; the essence of the difference is upgrading the mask generation from frame-by-frame linear mapping to local spatiotemporal non-linear mapping. Compared to query-by-example UDKWS: No registration, no recording required; text enters the model directly via G2P, and it is the only design that incorporates the playback reference signal as input. Compared to its predecessor PhonMatchNet: The only structural change is the Refiner, with all other hyperparameters inherited, making the upgrade path nearly zero-cost.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Four datasets were used for training and evaluation (Section 3.1): LibriPhrase (Reference 16), Google Speech Commands V1 (denoted G, Reference 20), Qualcomm Keyword Speech (denoted Q, Reference 21), and MUSAN (Reference 22). MUSAN is used specifically as the playback signal source, while the other three are used for UDKWS evaluation. The LibriPhrase training set was used during the training phase, along with babble noise from the MS-SNSD dataset (Reference 23) to enhance detection robustness; evaluation was performed on four test sets: G, Q, LibriPhrase-easy (LPE), and LibriPhrase-hard (LPH), with the division of LPE and LPH following Reference 17. The specific duration, number of sentences, and number of speakers for each dataset are not reported in the paper.

Playback and reverberation data are all synthesized via room acoustic simulation (Section 3.2): the target voice is convolved with the room impulse response to generate the mixed signal, using the tool pyroomacoustics (Reference 24). The scenarios are divided into four types: no playback, music playback, speech playback, and self-referencing. The first three use music and speech from MUSAN as playback sources; in the self-referencing scenario, the device playback signal is identical to the target voice, corresponding to the situation where the phone plays prompts like "Hey Siri" itself. Simulation parameters are all randomly sampled: room area follows a uniform distribution of 10 to 50 square meters, floor height 2.5 to 5.0 meters, reverberation time 0.2 to 0.6 seconds, propagation delay 0.01 to 0.1 seconds, and signal-to-interference ratio (SIR) from -12 to +3 dB. The engineering implications of these parameters are worth highlighting: an SIR lower bound of -12 dB means the playback sound can have an amplitude more than double that of the user's voice (amplitude ratio of $10^{12/20}$, close to 4 times), simulating the real geometry of a speaker close to the microphone and the user speaking from across the room; the upper bound of reverberation time of 0.6 seconds covers the late reflections of typical living rooms. The paper open-sources the data generation script at github.com/ncsoft/PhonMatchNet as part of its reproducibility commitment.

Implementation details (Section 3.4): Adam optimizer with default parameters, fixed learning rate of 0.001, trained for 100 epochs, batch size 1024, trained for one week on a single V100. It is worth mentioning that the paper's original words state that the optimal model was selected based on performance on the test set—this detail will be criticized in the experimental design section.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Three metrics are used (Section 3.3): Equal Error Rate (EER), Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), and Sample-level Mean Absolute Error (MAE). The choice of metrics is strongly coupled with the scenarios: in the no-playback, music playback, and speech playback scenarios, the test sets have both positive and negative samples, and EER and AUC are standard metrics independent of the decision threshold, facilitating fair cross-model comparison; however, in the self-referencing scenario, the ground truth for all samples is 0 (the correct behavior is always "not to trigger"), there are no positive samples, and EER and AUC are mathematically inapplicable, so only MAE is used. Section 4.1 of the paper specifically explains the unique reading of MAE in this context: when the ground truth is all 0, MAE is exactly equal to the average of the model's outputs, so it directly quantifies the average intensity of false triggers—the baseline model has an MAE as high as 73.97% to 85.40% in this scenario (Table 1), with the physical meaning that the model's average output probability is 0.7 to 0.85, triggering almost every time audio is played; whereas the model in this paper reduces it to 0.13% to 3.11%, meaning that on most test samples, the model outputs a probability close to zero. This metric design turns "self-referencing false triggering" from engineering mysticism into a directly readable quantity, which is the smartest stroke in the entire evaluation system.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table 1 presents four rows of comparison: Baseline PhonMatchNet (no AEC, 655K parameters), Baseline with NLMS front-end (655K), iPhonMatchNet with Mask Subnet D (688K), and iPhonMatchNet with Mask Subnet C (656K). Breaking down by scenario:

Self-referencing scenario (main battlefield): The baseline MAE on G, Q, LPE, and LPH is 73.97%, 71.72%, 82.75%, and 85.40% respectively; the NLMS front-end only drops to 72.55%, 69.86%, 72.91%, and 72.12%, which is almost ineffective; Configuration D drops to 0.70%, 0.65%, 1.44%, and 7.74%; Configuration C drops to 0.15%, 0.13%, 0.31%, and 3.11%. Calculating from Table 1 numbers, the reduction relative to the baseline for C is 99.8%, 99.8%, 99.6%, and 96.4% respectively. The abstract uniformly states an MAE reduction of approximately 95% and a model size increase of only 0.13% (the parameter count in Table 1 goes from 655K to 656K, which calculates to 0.15%, while the abstract takes 0.13%; there is a slight discrepancy in口径 but the magnitude is consistent).

Music playback scenario: The increase in AUC for C relative to the baseline on the four test sets is +3.18, +3.25, +5.81, and +6.19 percentage points (88.85 to 92.03, 82.37 to 85.62, 89.31 to 95.12, 71.21 to 77.40), and the EER decreases by 4.17, 3.08, 6.64, and 4.58 percentage points. Speech playback scenario: The increase in AUC is +8.74, +7.33, +10.10, and +8.50 percentage points (71.61 to 80.35, 71.47 to 78.80, 79.52 to 89.62, 63.51 to 72.01), and the EER decreases by 7.03, 4.83, 8.60, and 7.56 percentage points. There is a phenomenon worth digging into in comparing the two playback scenarios: the improvement in the speech playback scenario is generally larger than in the music playback scenario. The reason is not hard to understand—music and speech have large differences in spectral structure, and the baseline model can somewhat rely on acoustic differences to cope; whereas when the playback content and the target voice are both speech (especially in the extreme case of self-referencing), a single-channel input model has no way to distinguish at the representation level, and the baseline's absolute performance under speech playback is indeed the worst (EER as high as 40.35% on LPH). However, even with Configuration C, the absolute performance in the speech playback scenario remains the worst among all scenarios (EER of 32.79% on LPH speech playback), and the paper's conclusion admits this is the difficult scenario with the smallest relative improvement.

Cost in no-playback scenarios: The change in AUC for C relative to the baseline is -1.92, 0, +0.30, and -0.29 percentage points (98.11 to 96.19, 98.90 to 98.90, 99.29 to 99.59, 88.52 to 88.23), and the change in EER is +1.32, +0.92, -0.40, and +0.88 percentage points. Overall, it is a slight regression. The paper's statement is that "even in no-playback scenarios, it only shows a slight error increase relative to the baseline"—this is the necessary cost of introducing a playback path to exchange for anti-echo capabilities, controlled to around 1 percentage point, which is acceptable in engineering.

The relationship with SOTA needs to be stated truthfully: the paper does not end-to-end搬来 the original iAEC system from Reference 12 for comparison, nor does it place other zero-shot UDKWS methods from References 16 and 18 in the barge-in scenario for comparison; Reference 12 only enters its own ablation in the form of Mask Subnet D configuration. Strictly speaking, the comparison object is its own baseline plus a traditional AEC front-end, and external SOTA comparison is absent.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Ablation 1: Mask Subnet Structure D vs. C (Section 4.2 plus Table 1). This is the most critical ablation in the paper: C outperforms D comprehensively in all scenarios—self-referencing MAE on LPH drops from 7.74% to 3.11% (a five-fold difference), music playback EER on LPE drops from 14.61% to 12.76%, no-playback EER on LPE drops from 3.30% to 2.40%, while the parameters are 32K fewer. The paper attributes this to the fact that the relationship between the mixed signal and the playback signal is essentially a local correlation with time delay, and the inductive bias of the convolutional structure is more aligned with this data structure than frame-by-frame linear mapping; playback signals re-entering the microphone inevitably carry delay, and C, which can express this delay, is a more efficient mask method. The methodological value of this conclusion extends beyond this paper: it indicates that in the design variables of iAEC mask sub-networks, "ability to register across time" is much more important than "capacity size."

Ablation 2: AEC Implementation NLMS vs. iAEC (Section 4.3). Three observations about connecting NLMS to the baseline front-end are worth recording: First, no significant performance improvement in music and speech playback scenarios (in Table 1, most indicators fluctuate within ±1 percentage point, and some even worsen, e.g., G music scenario AUC drops from 88.85 to 87.64); Second, performance enhancement is observed in no-playback scenarios (e.g., Q AUC rises from 98.90 to 99.29), the paper does not delve into the mechanism, but a reasonable guess is that NLMS incidentally suppresses some additive noise; Third, no significant enhancement in self-referencing scenarios, with MAE still at the 0.7 level. The paper's explanation is that residual echoes not filtered by AEC are detected by the model—linear adaptive filters have residuals under low SIR and strong reverberation that are sufficient to trigger KWS. The judgment derived from this is quite sharp: improving the robustness of existing UDKWS (making it more tolerant of noisy speech) may worsen performance in self-referencing scenarios, so schemes containing iAEC structures are the correct solution. The cascaded route is negated, and the embedded route wins; these two sets of positive and negative evidence constitute the complete evidence chain for the paper's conclusion.

Ablation 3: The overall comparison with and without iAEC is as described above: non-playback scenarios are basically flat (local small regression), playback scenarios have comprehensive benefits, and self-referencing scenarios see order-of-magnitude improvements.

## Main Contributions

The paper lists three contributions, each with solid support. First, it proposes an efficient model structure to solve the self-referencing problem: exchanging an approximate 95% MAE reduction for a 0.13% parameter increase (abstract口径; Table 1 shows 655K to 656K), reducing the model's average output in the self-referencing scenario from above 0.7 to below 0.15 (Table 1). Second, it proves that iAEC functionality can be learned without clean target signals: training uses only mixed signals with target speech labels, aligning with real device conditions (Section 3.2, Conclusion). Third, it open-sources the generation scripts for the training and testing databases (github.com/ncsoft/PhonMatchNet), ensuring precise experimental reproducibility. Beyond this, there is one more contribution not explicitly stated by the paper but well-supported by evidence: it provides a route-level judgment through controlled experiments that "cascaded AEC front-ends cannot save KWS in barge-in scenarios, while embedded iAEC is effective" (Section 4.3), which has direct guiding significance for the architecture selection of on-device speech teams, with value comparable to the model itself.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, the absolute performance in high-correlation playback scenarios is still unusable. The conclusion admits that the improvement is relatively small in difficult scenarios like "speech playback"; in Table 1, Configuration C still has an EER as high as 32.79% and an AUC of only 72.01% under LPH speech playback—when a user shouts a wake word while the device is broadcasting speech, there is still a miss rate of one in three. The resolution of the self-referencing problem (MAE reduced to 3.11%) and the insufficient detection rate under speech playback are two sides of the same coin: the model has learned "echo is not a wake word," but becomes cautious when the true wake word and echo are highly similar.

Second, there is a small but consistent regression in no-playback scenarios (Table 1: G EER rises from 6.77 to 8.09, LPH from 18.82 to 19.70). Introducing the playback path gives the model an additional judgment dimension, which only brings interference in pure speech scenarios. For commercial deployments extremely sensitive to false triggers, this 1 percentage point regression needs to be weighed.

Third, the matching between convolutional receptive field and delay upper bound cannot be calculated. The simulation samples propagation delay up to 0.1 seconds (Section 3.2), but the paper does not report the embedding frame rate or upsampling rate, so it is unknown how many embedding frames 0.1 seconds corresponds to; if the effective receptive field of two layers of 3×3 convolution is estimated at a frame rate of 10 milliseconds, it only covers tens of milliseconds, and it is doubtful whether the mask can register under the worst-case delay. The gap between D and C has already proven that time registration is a key variable, so the receptive field size is the upper bound of this key variable, but the paper does not perform a receptive field ablation.

Fourth, both training and evaluation are built on linear RIR convolution simulation using pyroomacoustics (Section 3.2); common components of real device echo paths such as speaker non-linear distortion, microphone non-linearity, automatic gain control, and audio codec links are not covered; the paper does not verify on real device recordings, so the statement in the conclusion that it "is suitable for real deployment conditions of smart devices" only holds for the point of "not having access to clean signals," not for channel authenticity.

Fifth, only parameter count is reported (Table 1); inference computational load, latency, and real-time factor are all unreported; whether the GRU plus self-attention structure can be streamed, and whether the causal mask is sufficient to support low-latency always-on operation, is not discussed by the paper. Small parameter count does not equal fast computation; these are two separate accounts in edge devices.

Sixth, the scheme implicitly assumes a deployment prerequisite: the software layer must be able to obtain the audio-out reference signal. This is fine for system prompts, but whether audio played by third-party applications or copyrighted sound sources can be stably recaptured is a constraint at the operating system level, which the paper does not discuss (this is an inference based on deployment conditions, not an experimental conclusion of the paper).

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

The most glaring issue: the original words in Section 3.4 state that the optimal model was selected based on performance on the test set. Without an independent validation set, this is equivalent to using the test set for model selection, and all numbers in Table 1 carry a systematic risk of being overly optimistic. Although trend conclusions (order-of-magnitude MAE improvements) are unlikely to be overturned, comparisons at the 1 percentage point level (such as the regression magnitude in no-playback scenarios) cannot withstand scrutiny under this protocol. Second, the paper does not report any variance from multiple runs or statistical significance tests; single-run numbers cannot support fine-grained conclusions. Third, data scale information is missing: the scale of the LibriPhrase training set, the number of positive and negative samples in each test set, and the number of simulated playback samples generated are all unreported; only that training took one week on a single V100 (Section 3.4) is mentioned, making the training compute a not-so-small irony for the so-called lightweight narrative—inference with 656K parameters is very efficient, but a week on a V100 for training is not cheap. Fourth, the comparison scope is narrow: there is no end-to-end comparison with the original iAEC system from Reference 12, no horizontal comparison of other zero-shot UDKWS methods in the barge-in scenario, the self-referencing scenario has only one MAE number, and there is a lack of deployment threshold analysis and operational-level metrics such as false triggers per hour. Fifth, the SIR range only samples up to -12 dB (Section 3.2); more severe near-field high-volume playback was not tested.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper mentions only one direction for improvement: enhancing performance in high-correlation playback (speech playback) scenarios, which the authors themselves admit is the most painful point. Following the evidence of this paper, there are several natural paths. First, make the temporal receptive field of the mask sub-network an explicit design variable: dilated convolutions, larger convolutional kernels, or small-scale temporal attention, directly aligning with the 0.1-second delay upper bound, and supplementing with frame rate reports and receptive field ablations. Second, introduce real device recordings for channel simulation correction, or at least add speaker and microphone non-linearities and AGC links in the simulation, to fill the gap in the linear RIR assumption. Third, semi-explicit mask supervision: since clean targets are unavailable, a pre-trained explicit AEC model can be used as a teacher, distilling its output to provide the mask learning with a smoother guidance signal than pure detection loss. Fourth, streaming and on-device testing: report real-time factor and latency, and verify the actual overhead of the GRU plus causal attention structure under always-on operation. Fifth, complete the evaluation system: add deployment threshold scanning and false triggers per unit time metrics, so that the improvement in the self-referencing scenario falls from MAE readings to operational language. None of these issues shake the core conclusion of this paper—that embedded iAEC solves self-referencing false triggering at nearly zero cost—they determine how far this route is from productization.
