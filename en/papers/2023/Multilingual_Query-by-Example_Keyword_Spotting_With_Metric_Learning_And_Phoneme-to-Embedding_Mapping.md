## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Voice assistants have permeated various devices such as smartphones, smart speakers, headphones, and in-car systems. Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the primary gateway for voice interaction on these devices. The paper’s introduction outlines three generations of technical approaches, each with its own structural flaws: The first generation, based on Large Vocabulary Continuous Speech Recognition (LVCSR), decodes the entire sentence first and then searches for keywords within the word lattice. While this approach offers high recognition accuracy, it incurs significant computational overhead and introduces higher latency, making it unsuitable for deployment on small electronic devices (citing references [1][2]). The second generation uses Keyword/Filler Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), where predefined keywords are modeled using keyword HMMs and non-keyword audio segments are modeled using filler HMMs. Optimal paths are found in the decoding graph via Viterbi decoding. However, the computational load of decoding heavily depends on the HMM topology, leading to similar computational power issues (citing references [3][4]). The third generation employs Deep Neural Network (DNN) word classifiers, which surpass HMM-based solutions in accuracy (citing reference [5]) and have become the current mainstream. However, the majority of works (citing references [5][6][7][8]) treat KWS as a closed-set classification problem—where both target and non-target words are predefined during training.

The closed-set setting creates a real-world product pain point: if users want to customize wake words or command words, they must collect new data and retrain the model. This is nearly infeasible for individual users and imposes high maintenance costs on device manufacturers. The correct form of personalization should be "changing words by simply recording a few examples," or even without recording, and it should not be limited to a specific language—users in a multilingual household might use English, German, French, or any other language for their wake words. The paper aims to build a user-customizable KWS system that supports arbitrary languages and words, with registration costs reduced to just a few examples (or even zero recordings), and capable of running directly on continuous audio streams.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper addresses several technical routes for user-customizable KWS, each with clear shortcomings:

- **The "unknown" class problem in closed-set classifiers**. Compressing all non-target words into a single unified "unknown" class for training forces the model to represent the infinitely diverse non-target sounds of the real world with one class. Reference [14] has already found that such systems perform poorly on unseen non-target words, and this paper’s experiments re-verify this point—the baseline method achieves an average Equal Error Rate (EER) of 3.76% on languages unseen during training (Figure 2b). The intuitive explanation is that the "unknown" class seen during training can only cover negative samples within the training distribution. When the language changes, the distribution of negative samples shifts entirely, causing the classification boundary to fail.
- **Few-shot fine-tuning schemes (Reference [13], also the baseline for this paper)**. A three-class softmax (target/unknown/background) is attached to a pre-trained multilingual classification model, and gradient fine-tuning is performed using 256 training samples constructed from 5 target word examples. There are two main issues: First, fine-tuning must be run every time a keyword is changed (10 epochs), meaning registration is not instantaneous. Performing this on the edge side also raises concerns about computational power and stability. Second, the fine-tuning objective remains classification cross-entropy, so the inter-class separability of the embedding space itself is not explicitly optimized, effectively inheriting the structural defect of the "unknown" class.
- **Template matching based on phoneme posterior grids and Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) (Reference [9])**. Both user examples and test audio are converted into phoneme posterior grids before DTW alignment. This approach is sensitive to duration variations and accent differences, and template-by-template alignment incurs significant retrieval overhead for long audio.
- **CTC-based schemes (References [11][12])**. These use small-footprint ASR to output posterior grids, then rely on beam search or hypothesis graphs based on finite state transducers to search for keywords. The decoding chain is long, and engineering complexity is high.
- **Query-by-String (Reference [18])**. Directly inputting keywords in text form seems more convenient, but it carries a fundamental risk: the model does not know how the user actually pronounces the word. Performance collapses when there is a pronunciation mismatch. This is precisely the advantage of example queries (where the user records themselves)—the examples naturally carry the user’s true accent and pronunciation.
- **Inconvenience of registration for example queries themselves**. Even with audio registration, it is inconvenient in certain usage scenarios (as explicitly stated in the first sentence of Section 2.5). Users may configure devices in environments where speaking is inconvenient, or the recorded examples may vary significantly in quality.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

In summary, the paper faces four mutually constraining challenges: First, an open vocabulary and open language—the model must work for words unseen during training (out-of-vocabulary, OOV) and even for languages completely unseen during training (out-of-embedding). This requires the embedding space to learn cross-lingual shared word-level acoustic structures rather than specific word class boundaries. Second, registration costs must be extremely low—five examples are the upper limit, and it is best to degrade to zero recordings (predicting the target embedding vector directly from the phoneme sequence obtained from a pronunciation dictionary). Third, the discriminative objective of the embedding space must be rewritten—since there are infinitely many non-target words, one cannot "cluster all non-targets into one class"; instead, one must directly minimize the similarity between targets and non-targets. This is where metric learning comes into play. Fourth, everything must be implemented in a streaming scenario—the miss rate under a constraint of false alarms per hour with a 1-second sliding window is the true metric from a product perspective; classification accuracy on isolated sentence sets does not count.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system is a verification pipeline consisting of an "embedding extractor + cosine template matching," rather than a classification pipeline.

**Front-end Features**: 40-dimensional Mel-filterbank features, with a Hann window of 25 ms and a frame shift of 10 ms. One second of audio is taken, and maximum value normalization and logarithmic transformation are applied to each segment (Section 2.1). One second is chosen because the extraction upper limit for training words is 1 second, aligning the input length with the task granularity.

**Backbone Network**: Fast-ResNet-34, taken directly from a classic work in speaker verification (Reference [21], "In Defence of Metric Learning for Speaker Recognition"). Based on ResNet-34 (Reference [22]), but each residual block retains only one-quarter of the channels. The total number of parameters is 1.4 M, compared to 22 M for the original ResNet-34, making it a small-footprint model (Section 2.1). An noteworthy selection logic here is that the authors did not choose a backbone from KWS literature but from speaker verification—because this backbone is inherently an embedding extractor designed for the "metric learning + cosine matching" paradigm. Its pooling structure and output space have been fully validated on verification tasks, so transferring it over involves the same usage pattern.

**Network Details** (Table 1, input 1×40×T): conv1 is 7×7, 16 channels, stride 2×1—note this stride notation: it downsamples the frequency dimension by 2x while keeping the time dimension at original resolution, resulting in an output of 16×20×T; subsequently, conv2 (3×3, 16 channels) is repeated 3 times with stride 1; conv3 (3×3, 32 channels) is repeated 4 times with stride 2, halving both frequency and time, resulting in an output of 32×10×T/2; conv4 (3×3, 64 channels) is repeated 6 times with stride 2, resulting in an output of 64×5×T/4; conv5 (3×3, 128 channels) is repeated 3 times with stride 1, resulting in an output of 128×5×T/4; then, the mean is taken along the frequency dimension to get 128×1×T/4, followed by time average pooling to get 128×1, and finally, a fully connected layer outputs a 256×1 utterance-level embedding. This structure has two design intents: First, the time dimension is downsampled by only 4x in total (10 ms frame shift becomes 40 ms frame rate), so the temporal structure within keywords (phoneme order, duration ratios) is preserved before entering the pooling layer. Second, time average pooling compresses utterances of arbitrary duration and speed into a fixed-length vector, which is naturally insensitive to duration—this, combined with speed resampling augmentation (0.85 to 1.15x) during training, embeds "speed robustness" into both the data and the structure.

**Training Data Construction** (Section 2.2): Montreal Forced Aligner (Reference [23]) is used for multilingual forced alignment on the crowdsourced Common Voice (Reference [19]), from which all words longer than 3 characters and shorter than 1 second are extracted. Words with more than 500 samples in English, German, French, and Catalan are retained, capped at 10,000 samples per word, with 25 samples reserved for validation during training. The scale is shown in Table 2: English 2,014 words totaling 3,694,626 samples, German 703 words totaling 1,400,878 samples, French 630 words totaling 1,130,341 samples, and Catalan 570 words totaling 887,858 samples, totaling 3,917 words and 7,113,703 samples. To allow the model to adapt to both isolated command words and keywords embedded within sentences, each extracted item generates two versions: one padded with silence, and one retaining the original context audio (following Reference [13]), doubling the sample count to approximately 14 million. A key detail is that segments with context likely contain multiple words. The authors force the target word to be centered in the middle of the 1-second audio, teaching the model to primarily extract information from the middle time segment (Section 2.2). This "center alignment" serves as a bridge between training and inference—during streaming detection, the sliding window registers a hit when the keyword roughly falls into the center of the window, and the centering bias during training teaches the model this geometric convention.

**Data Augmentation** (Section 2.3): Three types. Speed perturbation: resampling with factors between 0.85 and 1.15 (following the streaming KWS recipe in Reference [8]); Random time offset: within ±0.05 seconds, to prevent the model from overfitting to the exact position of the keyword; Additive noise and room impulse response simulation: strictly following existing recipes from speaker verification (References [24][25]). Each training sample randomly falls into one of five categories: speech, music, noise, room simulation, or no augmentation. Noise is taken from the MUSAN corpus (Reference [26]), and simulated room impulse responses are from Reference [27]. The random combination of augmentation categories exposes the model to sufficiently diverse acoustic conditions, which is the reason for maintaining performance under babble noise later.

**Two-Stage Training Strategy** (Section 2.4): The authors found on the development set that "classification pre-training followed by metric fine-tuning" outperforms direct metric training (the paper does not report specific numbers for this comparison), so the entire training is divided into two steps. Step 1: Classification Pre-training. A classification head with output dimension N=3917 (exactly equal to the vocabulary size, see Table 2) is attached to the fully connected layer, and trained for 40 epochs using cross-entropy loss. The initial learning rate is 0.001, decaying via cosine annealing after the 10th epoch, combined with balanced batch sampling and a batch size of 128. Step 2: Metric Fine-tuning. The classification head is removed, all weights from conv1 to conv4 are frozen, and only conv5 and the fully connected layer are fine-tuned. The loss is changed to circle loss (Reference [28]), with hyperparameters set to γ=80 and m=0.4 as suggested in the original paper. Sampling uses the P-K strategy—P=32 samples per class, K=5 classes per batch (i.e., 32×5=160 samples per batch). Training lasts for 10 epochs, with the learning rate starting at 0.001 and decaying after the 3rd epoch, also using cosine annealing. Both stages use the Adam optimizer. The logic for "freezing lower layers and only tuning upper layers" is: lower-layer convolutions learn general acoustic texture features with strong cross-word sharing. Full fine-tuning is more likely to destroy already learned general representations and overfit to the training vocabulary on small models. The upper layers (conv5 and fc) are where word-level discrimination is carried, so the metric loss should focus here.

**Registration and Matching**: During inference, the user records 5 examples of the target word. The model performs a forward pass to get 5 embeddings, which are averaged to form the template. The embedding of the test audio is compared with the template using cosine similarity; if it exceeds the threshold, it is classified as the target. Note that there is no gradient update during the entire registration process—this is the most essential usage difference from the baseline method.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Circle Loss** (The paper directly cites Reference [28]; organized here according to the original form): For an anchor embedding, let the intra-class similarity within the batch be $s_p$ and the inter-class similarity be $s_n$. The loss is:

$$\mathcal{L} = \log\Big(1 + \sum_{j}\sum_{k} \exp\big(\gamma(\alpha_n^j s_n^j - \alpha_p^k s_p^k)\big)\Big)$$

where the weights are $\alpha_p = [O_p - s_p]_+$ and $\alpha_n = [s_n - O_n]_+$, the optimal similarity anchors are $O_p = 1+m$ and $O_n = -m$, and $m$ is the slack margin. Its relationship to triplet loss is one of "generalization": triplet loss processes only one (anchor, positive, negative) triplet at a time using a hinge loss with a fixed slope. Circle loss unifies all intra-class pairs and inter-class pairs within the batch into a log-sum-exp aggregation, and assigns its own weight $\alpha$ to each similarity score—the further a score is from its optimal value, the larger the gradient it receives; when it is close to the optimal value, the gradient automatically decays. The paper’s statement about "learning intra-class and inter-class similarities at different speeds" refers to this property: pairs that are already well-separated no longer waste gradient capacity, while hard pairs are pulled heavily. $\gamma$ controls the sharpness of the penalty, and $m$ controls the minimum gap that must be maintained between classes. The P-K sampling (P=32, K=5) is not an optional engineering detail here: the loss form requires the batch to simultaneously contain multiple samples of the same class (providing rich intra-class pairs) and multiple different classes (providing rich inter-class pairs); otherwise, the pair set degenerates into an approximate triplet. Why use cosine similarity for matching: after metric training, the inter-class angular intervals in the embedding space are explicitly optimized. Cosine distance is exactly the quantity optimized by the loss. The training objective and inference metric are consistent, avoiding the mismatch of "optimizing A during training and using B during inference."

**Phoneme-to-Embedding Mapping (P2E)** (Section 2.5, structure shown in Table 3): The goal is to directly regress the KWS model's embedding vector from the target word's phoneme sequence, skipping recording. The model is simple: an embedding lookup table of size 70×128 (69 English phonemes plus one padding entry) maps each phoneme to a 128-dimensional vector. This is processed by a two-layer LSTM (input 128 dimensions, hidden layer 256 dimensions). The mean of the hidden state sequence from the second LSTM layer is taken to obtain a fixed-length representation of 256×1, which is then passed through a fully connected layer with 256 outputs to produce the final embedding prediction. The training target is the "average embedding" of approximately 7,000 English words—each word takes 50 recordings, calculates the mean of their embeddings after forward pass through the KWS model, and uses this as the regression target. The loss uses cosine loss (the paper does not give the specific form of the loss, but by convention, it should be 1 minus the cosine value). There are 69 distinct phonemes in the training set. The inference flow is extremely simple: look up the pronunciation dictionary to get the target word's phoneme sequence, perform one forward pass through the LSTM, obtain the pseudo-template embedding, and subsequent matching is identical to audio registration. Using the "mean embedding of multiple recordings within a word" as the regression target is a clever construction: the mean removes the variance of speakers and recording conditions, retaining only the center position of the word in the embedding space. The LSTM learns the mapping from the phoneme sequence to this center, naturally focusing on the word's linguistic content rather than acoustic details.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: First Combination of Multilingual Crowdsourced Data and Metric Learning

The paper's first contribution point (explicitly stated as "first time" in the introduction) is combining multilingual crowdsourced speech data (Common Voice, 3,917 words, approximately 14 million training samples, Table 2) with circle loss metric learning for the KWS task. The value of this lies not in a single technical point, but in the structure: large-vocabulary multilingual data provides extremely rich inter-class structure, and circle loss directly optimizes "target and non-target are not similar" on top of it. The learned embedding space is naturally oriented towards an open vocabulary—words unseen during training, as long as their pronunciation structure can be encoded acoustically, will fall into positions in the embedding space close to their own template and far from others. Experiments (Figures 2a, 2b) provide quantitative support: the average EER for OOV words drops from the baseline's 1.96% to 0.82%, a relative reduction of 59.2%; for unseen languages (Portuguese, Turkish, Arabic, Indonesian), it drops from 3.76% to 2.00%, a relative reduction of 47.9%. More importantly, every test language in both scenarios outperforms the baseline (Section 4.1.1), indicating that the improvement is not an average pulled by individual languages.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Registration-Free—Predicting Embeddings Directly from Phoneme Sequences

The P2E mapping solves the last mile of QbE: even 5 recordings can be saved. Users (or manufacturers) only need a pronunciation dictionary. Technically, it is a "sequence-to-vector" regression in the embedding space. Effectively, the paper reports that its classification accuracy is close to that of registering with 5 real recordings (Figure 2c), making it a clearly superior option when fewer than 5 examples are available (Section 4.1.2). This module also has an analytical significance beyond engineering value: if a simple LSTM can accurately predict the KWS model's embedding from a phoneme sequence, it proves that the KWS model's embedding space indeed encodes phoneme-level (phonological) information, rather than just memorizing the acoustic fingerprints of training words—this is a behavioral evidence of the "linguistic interpretability" of the embedding space (last sentence of Section 4.1.2).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Complete Evaluation Protocol for Streaming

The paper does not stop at sentence-set classification but constructs a complete streaming evaluation (Figure 1): a 1-second window length with a 0.1-second step slides forward through the audio stream. Audio within the window is passed through the model to get an embedding, which is compared with the template using cosine similarity; if it exceeds the threshold, a detection is reported. To prevent a single word from triggering multiple detections, output is suppressed for 1 second after each detection. The time tolerance window for determining whether a detection matches the ground truth is set to 0.75 seconds, following the Google Speech Commands evaluation protocol (Reference [30]). The construction of test audio also has two deliberately distinguished task versions (Section 3.3): The keyword detection scenario concatenates 20 random recordings of target words with 200 random Common Voice sentences of the same language, simulating "wake words or isolated commands mixed in continuous speech." The keyword retrieval scenario concatenates 20 sentences containing the target word with 200 random sentences, simulating "finding words within natural utterances." The former is simpler because there are no directly interfering non-target words; the latter is harder because the target word is embedded in co-channel speech. The two tasks are reported separately (Figures 2d, 2e), avoiding mixing two different difficulties into a single number.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

The difference from Reference [13] (baseline) is global: the baseline requires one three-class fine-tuning run for each new keyword (256 constructed samples, 10 epochs, batch size 12, learning rate multiplied by 0.7 per epoch). In this paper's method, the registration phase involves zero gradient updates, requiring only 5 forward passes and one mean calculation. The baseline's training objective is to compress non-targets into the "unknown" class, whereas this paper directly optimizes the similarity structure. When reproducing the baseline, the authors specifically did not freeze weights (contrary to the original setting in Reference [13]), observing a slight improvement in the baseline (Section 3.1). This means the comparison is made on an enhanced baseline, making the conclusions more conservative and fairer. Compared to DTW template methods (Reference [9]), this paper moves "template matching" to cosine matching in a learned embedding space. Robustness to duration and accents is handled by training data and pooling structures, rather than being hard-resisted by alignment algorithms. Compared to CTC-based methods (References [11][12]), this paper does not require beam search or hypothesis graph construction. Decoding degenerates into a single similarity calculation plus threshold judgment, resulting in a much shorter chain. Compared to Query-by-String (Reference [18]), although P2E also starts from the text side, the predicted target is the "embedding distribution center corresponding to how the user would pronounce it," and audio registration is retained as a calibration method. The two registration methods are interchangeable within the same framework.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Training side (Table 2): Common Voice in four languages (English, German, French, Catalan) totaling 3,917 words and 7,113,703 extractions, doubled to approximately 14 million samples via silent and context versions (Section 2.2); noise for augmentation comes from MUSAN (Reference [26]), and room impulse responses are from the simulated set in Reference [27]. The baseline fine-tuning additionally uses a pre-constructed 5,000-sample four-language keyword pronunciation library for negative samples, as well as background noise from Google Speech Commands (Reference [30]) (Section 3.1). P2E training uses the average embedding of approximately 7,000 English words, with 50 recordings per word as the target, covering 69 phonemes (Section 2.5).

Testing side: The OOV test set consists of 500 words unseen during training per language, with 50 silent-padded extractions per word (Section 3.2); the unseen language test set follows the alignment results from Reference [13], taking 100 words per language from Portuguese, Turkish, Arabic, and Indonesian, with 25 silent-padded samples per word. On the Hey-Snips dataset (Reference [20]), 100 speakers (57 male, 43 female) manually cut out 3 utterances of "Hey Snips" each. During testing, all 297 utterances of "Hey Snips" from the remaining speakers are concatenated with 1,000 random common sentences. Each speaker takes turns being the registrant, accumulating 121.4 hours of test audio (Section 3.3). In streaming experiments, the KWS scenario uses 20 target words concatenated with 200 random sentences, and the retrieval scenario uses 20 sentences containing the word concatenated with 200 random sentences (Section 3.3). The paper does not report finer statistics such as the number of speakers in the test set or SNR distribution.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

On the classification side, EER (Equal Error Rate, Section 4.1.1) is used: the intersection point where the false alarm rate and miss rate are equal when sweeping the threshold. The reason for choosing it is threshold-independence—this paper's matching is a verification process of "template + cosine + threshold," and the final deployment threshold varies by scenario. EER decouples threshold selection from evaluation, measuring the discriminative power of the embedding space itself. Additionally, EER is insensitive to the ratio of positive to negative samples, making it suitable for per-word calculation and then cross-word averaging (the experimental design where each word acts as the target once, with other words of the same language serving as negatives, Section 3.2). On the streaming side, the miss rate at a fixed false alarm rate (FNR@FA/h, Section 4.2.2) is used: it reports FNR of 1.7% at 1 FA/h and FNR of 5.4% at 0.1 FA/h for clean audio. The reason for choosing it is product alignment—the actual constraint for wake word systems is a false alarm budget like "at most a few false wake-ups per day." It is meaningful to look at miss rates under this budget. 0.1 FA/h is a quite strict low-false-alarm tier. The time matching tolerance window for detection and ground truth is 0.75 seconds (Section 3.3, following the evaluation protocol in Reference [30]). Why not use overall accuracy: in open-set tasks, negative samples far outnumber positive samples, so accuracy would be dominated by negative samples and lose discriminative power.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

**Classification Accuracy Comparison** (Figures 2a, 2b, Section 4.1.1): For OOV words in the four training languages, the circle loss fine-tuned version outperforms the baseline (three-class fine-tuned version) in every language. The average EER drops from 1.96% to 0.82%, a relative reduction of 59.2% (Figure 2a); for the four unseen languages, the average EER drops from 3.76% to 2.00%, a relative reduction of 47.9% (Figure 2b). Accuracy for seen languages is generally high (Figure 2a), while unseen languages show a slight decrease but still maintain good generalization (Figure 2b). The authors interpret these results as support for the conclusion in Reference [14]: systems that represent non-targets as a single "unknown" class perform poorly on unseen non-target words. It is better to directly minimize the similarity between non-targets and targets than to force infinitely diverse sounds to be similar to each other. Note that the baseline uses this paper's own enhanced reproduction (weights not frozen), and both methods use exactly the same registration examples and test recordings (Section 3.1), ensuring clean control of comparison variables.

**Registration Example Count and P2E Comparison** (Figure 2c, Section 4.1.2): On English OOV words (these words were excluded from the P2E training set in advance to avoid information leakage), changing the number of registration examples from 1 to 20: accuracy increases monotonically with the number of examples. The increase is significant between 1 and 5 examples, and the gains diminish after 5 examples—thus, 5 examples is a reasonable trade-off between user cost and accuracy. The accuracy of P2E (zero recordings, dictionary lookup only) is very close to the level of registering with 5 real recordings, making it a superior option in scenarios with fewer than 5 examples. The paper explicitly states its boundary: P2E is limited by the set of phonemes seen during training (69 English phonemes), so keywords outside this phoneme set cannot be used.

**Streaming Comparison** (Figures 2d, 2e, Section 4.2.1): Isolated keyword detection (Figure 2d) is generally better than in-sentence keyword retrieval (Figure 2e), for the reasons mentioned above—the former has no directly interfering non-target words. In the retrieval task, the gap between seen and unseen languages widens significantly, indicating that co-channel speech within sentences tests embeddings much more severely than isolated words, and the loss of cross-lingual generalization is amplified here.

**Hey-Snips Real Wake Words** (Figure 2f, Section 4.2.2): For clean audio, FNR is 1.7% @ 1 FA/h and 5.4% @ 0.1 FA/h; curves for babble noise at 6 dB and 12 dB SNR are given in the figure, but specific values are not reported in the text. The error rate is far lower than in the Common Voice streaming experiments (Figure 2d). The authors give two reasons: The Common Voice test set retains uncleaned interference factors (alignment errors, sub-words, plural forms like refugee/refugees, near-homophones like prays/praise), which inflate false alarms (making the numbers conservative and fair, as noted at the end of Section 3.3 and beginning of Section 4); and "Hey Snips" itself is longer and less likely to be confused with other words. The paper does not make a direct numerical comparison with the original Hey-Snips paper (Reference [20]) or any external SOTA systems; the horizontal axis only includes its own baseline.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have a formal ablation table but embeds five groups of ablation-like comparisons: First, training strategy ablation—classification pre-training plus metric fine-tuning compared to direct metric training. The two-stage approach was confirmed to be superior on the development set, but the paper does not report specific numbers. Second, baseline reproduction ablation—unfreezing weights during fine-tuning compared to freezing the entire network. Unfreezing brought a slight improvement (Section 3.1), but the paper does not report the difference. Third, registration example count ablation (1 to 20 examples, Figure 2c), concluding that 5 examples is the sweet spot. Fourth, noise condition ablation (clean, babble 6 dB, 12 dB, Figure 2f), with specific values only in the figure. Fifth, task difficulty ablation (isolated word detection compared to in-sentence retrieval, Figure 2d compared to Figure 2e). Obvious missing items: ablation on the split of frozen layers (why freeze conv1 to conv4 and not other positions), ablation on the individual contributions of augmentation strategies, and ablation on the impact of embedding dimensions and the number of P2E training words—the paper does not report these. Additionally, on the curve of 1 to 20 examples, P2E is a horizontal reference line rather than varying with the number of examples, which itself constitutes a direct reading of "zero recordings vs. how many recordings" (Figure 2c).

## Main Contributions

First, at the methodological level, this is the first time multilingual crowdsourced speech data and metric learning are combined for keyword detection (first contribution in the introduction). By using circle loss to train multilingual embeddings on 3,917 words and approximately 14 million samples extracted from Common Voice, it achieves generalization capabilities with a relative reduction in average EER for unseen words of 59.2% (from 1.96% to 0.82%, Figure 2a) and a relative reduction for unseen languages of 47.9% (from 3.76% to 2.00%, Figure 2b), and outperforms the enhanced reproduction of the classification-based baseline in every language.

Second, expansion of the registration paradigm—the Phoneme-to-Embedding mapping proves that the word embeddings learned by the KWS model can be accurately predicted from phoneme sequences using a simple LSTM (Figure 2c). This provides a zero-recording option for registration, and simultaneously constitutes behavioral evidence that the embedding space encodes phoneme-level information (Section 4.1.2).

Third, complete implementation in streaming scenarios—the protocol with a 1-second window, 0.1-second step, 1-second suppression after detection, and 0.75-second tolerance window (Figure 1) is validated on two tasks (keyword detection and keyword retrieval, Figures 2d, 2e) and 121.4 hours of Hey-Snips concatenated audio, achieving FNR of 5.4% @ 0.1 FA/h and 1.7% @ 1 FA/h under clean conditions (Section 4.2.2).

Fourth, engineering usability—the Fast-ResNet-34 backbone with 1.4 M parameters (compared to 22 M for ResNet-34, Section 2.1) combined with zero gradient updates during the registration phase. The overall system form (one forward pass plus one cosine calculation) is suitable for edge-side deployment.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**P2E's Language Closure** is the most explicit one: the phoneme table contains only 69 English phonemes, and the regression targets come only from approximately 7,000 English words (Section 2.5). The paper itself admits that P2E is "limited by the set of phonemes used during its training" (Section 4.1.2). This means that the system's most attractive multilingual capability and the most convenient zero-recording registration are currently mutually exclusive—non-English keywords must still go through audio registration. **Hard Constraint on Input Length**: The 1-second window and the construction of training words not exceeding 1 second determine that longer multi-word wake phrases cannot be handled directly. **Scope of False Alarm Numbers**: The conclusion section explicitly states that the reported false alarms per hour apply only to pure speech scenarios. The authors expect them to be much lower in daily acoustic environments—this inference is reasonable (test audio is seamlessly spliced speech, every second is speech-like content, which is the harshest scenario for false alarms; real environments are mostly silent and non-speech, making it easier for the model to reject). However, there is no data support for this, and performance in real environments awaits subsequent verification. **Missing Edge-Side Metrics**: The paper only provides the parameter count. Inference latency, real-time factor, memory usage, and power consumption are not reported. The requirement for forward speed imposed by the streaming protocol's 0.1-second step is not quantified. **Simplicity of Registration Templates**: The template is simply the mean of 5 example embeddings, with no speaker normalization or outlier example removal. There is a lack of protection mechanisms when poor-quality recordings are mixed into the examples.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

The Common Voice test set was not manually cleaned. Alignment errors, sub-words, plurals (refugee vs. refugees), and near-homophones (prays vs. praise) remain in it (Sections 3.3, 4 beginning). This inflates false alarms (making the numbers conservative and fair) but also introduces uncontrollable noise and variance. Unseen languages were only tested for Portuguese, Turkish, Arabic, and Indonesian (Section 3.2). With limited coverage of tonal languages and low-resource languages, extrapolating the conclusion of "generalizing to any language" requires caution. All per-language results are presented only as bar charts in Figure 2. The text only provides cross-language average EER, without a per-language numerical table, making third-party reproduction and comparison difficult. Horizontal comparison only includes Reference [13] as a baseline. There is no comparison with CTC-based, DTW-based, or Query-by-String QbE methods under the same protocol, nor is there a comparison with the original Hey-Snips system (Reference [20]). The Hey-Snips experiment uses 3 recordings from a single speaker for registration (Section 3.3). The registration sample speaker is singular, and registration robustness across speakers and accents is not systematically analyzed. Although the design of each speaker taking turns being the registrant controls for speaker leakage, the small sample size per registration makes it difficult to estimate result variance. Additionally, several key comparisons (the benefit of two-stage training, the benefit of unfreezing the baseline) are only qualitatively mentioned on the development set, and the paper does not report numbers, making it impossible for readers to assess the effect size.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The next step given by the authors themselves is to evaluate the KWS system on target devices in real acoustic environments (conclusion section), which simultaneously fills the gaps in real noise verification and edge-side latency/power consumption. Following the logic of this paper, several natural extensions can be seen: Expanding the P2E phoneme table from 69 English phonemes to a cross-lingual phoneme set or International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) coverage, allowing zero-recording registration and multilingual capabilities to truly coexist; Mixing audio registration and phoneme registration—using audio examples to calibrate the user's personal pronunciation and phoneme prediction as a fallback, combining the strengths of QbE and Query-by-String; Introducing speaker normalization or quality weighting for examples at the registration end to replace the naive mean template; and supplementing systematic ablation studies on design points that were not ablated, such as the split of frozen layers, augmentation combinations, and embedding dimensions.
