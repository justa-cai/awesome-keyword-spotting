# Few-Shot Open-Set Learning for On-Device Customization of KeyWord Spotting Systems

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Manuele Rusci, Tinne Tuytelaars (PSI Research Group, KU Leuven, Belgium)
- **Date**: June 2023 (arXiv:2306.02161v1, submitted June 3, 2023)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02161
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, few-shot learning, open-set recognition, on-device customization, prototypical network, triplet loss, metric learning

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the always-on voice interface for battery-powered smart audio sensors. Because the detection pipeline must run continuously on the target system, reducing the computational and memory overhead of deep learning-based KWS has been extensively studied in recent years (e.g., streaming mobile KWS and MicroNets, state-of-the-art approaches). However, this entire line of research addresses "how efficiently fixed keywords can be detected," whereas the recent focus of product requirements has shifted to "how quickly custom keywords can be adapted." Users of smart speakers and wireless earbuds wish to replace their wake-word with any arbitrary word without waiting for vendor firmware updates. The traditional customization process requires training a deep model on a user-collected speech dataset for the custom keyword. This path is constrained by two resources: server-side computational power and the volume of recorded data. Consequently, customization must leave the device and return to the cloud, preventing users from obtaining a personalized solution on-device in a short time. The paper highlights this contradiction at the outset: an effective customization process and the requirements of "speed" and "on-device" execution are mutually exclusive under existing paradigms.

On-device customization implicitly involves three coupled constraints, all of which are indispensable. The first is the data constraint: users are only willing to speak a few registration utterances into the device. The paper examines scales such as 5 utterances per word (5-shot) and 10 utterances per word (10-shot). It is impossible to collect hundreds or thousands of labeled utterances for each new word; this is precisely where Few-Shot Learning (FSL) intervenes. The second is the computational constraint: the target devices are microcontroller-level low-power hardware. Performing backpropagation training on such devices is practically infeasible, so the customization process is allowed only forward-inference-level computation. This is where the value of prototype-based classifiers lies: class prototypes are the means of registration utterance embeddings. Assembling the classifier requires no gradient updates, making it a "customization primitive executable on-device." The third is the open-set constraint: in real-world deployment, the vast majority of sliding window audio frames are neither any user keyword nor often similar in pronunciation to keywords. A model that performs only closed-set classification will classify all similar unknown words as keywords, and false alarms will directly destroy the product experience. Therefore, the correct problem formulation for on-device customization is the simultaneous satisfaction of "few-shot + open-set + no backpropagation," whereas existing literature almost always satisfies only one or two of these conditions.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

- **Closed-set evaluation dominates, making conclusions non-transferable to open-set scenarios.** Most ProtoNet-based works evaluate only on closed sets, using the same distribution of class subsets for training and testing (typically internal splits of the Google Speech Commands (GSC) dataset). Although Jung et al. proved that angular prototype loss outperforms classification-style encoders, their landmark 96% high score came from "fine-tuning on test classes after pre-training ResNet15 on 1000 LibriSpeech classes." Fine-tuning requires backpropagation, which directly conflicts with the on-device no-backpropagation constraint. More importantly, closed-set metrics do not measure the ability to reject unknown classes, which is a fatal blind spot in evaluation for a real KWS system.
- **Existing open-set methods have structural flaws.** Kim et al.'s Dummy Prototypical Networks (Interspeech 2022) jointly train an unknown-class "dummy prototype" generator. While the idea is elegant, both training and testing data are sampled from different class subsets of GSC (same distribution), which systematically overestimates performance in real cross-domain deployments. Furthermore, evaluation is limited to 5-shot-5-way, with a maximum accuracy of 86.9%. The PEELER method trains an additional network to estimate the variance of each class prototype and uses Mahalanobis distance for open-set decisions during inference. The memory cost of storing variance tensors and the estimator is heavy—this cost is quantified in Table 2 of this paper: an additional 6.3M parameters on the DSCNN-L backbone and an additional 341k on DSCNN-S (compared to the backbone itself having only 407k and 22k parameters, this equals a burden several times that of the backbone). Other works use triplet or angular prototype loss combined with SVM classifiers for open-set KWS, but SVMs are memory-expensive, lack few-shot evaluation, and mix user keywords into the training set, meaning the protocol itself deviates from the customization scenario.
- **High inference cost in the Query-by-Example line of work.** Early personalization schemes used two-layer LSTMs to take the final state embedding and compare similarity with reference vectors, or used LSTM encoder outputs to generate hypothesis graphs based on phoneme posterior grids for alignment. The LSTM load is heavy for memory-constrained devices and must be executed at every window step. The paper estimates that the runtime of such methods is 10 to 100 times slower than schemes that perform "one convolution per 1-second large window"—a magnitude of difference unacceptable for KWS systems that need to run continuously.
- **Highly fragmented domain, lacking design guidelines.** Encoder training losses (prototype / angular prototype / triplet), output normalization methods (CONV / RELU / NORM), and open-set classifiers (nearest class mean / OpenMAX / dummy prototype generator) each have literature support along three design axes. However, no work has conducted a horizontal comparison under a unified protocol. The original text judges that "current few-shot open-set KWS literature is highly fragmented, lacking clear design guidelines for on-device KWS customization." Engineers are handed a collection of isolated, even contradictory, conclusions rather than executable selection criteria.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

The paper essentially aims to answer an engineering selection question: under the constraints of cross-dataset settings (training on MSWC, testing on GSC, target classes not in the source class set), fixed training budget (40 epochs, 400 episodes per epoch), and fixed false acceptance operating point (5% false acceptance rate for unknown classes), which combination of "encoder training loss × output normalization method × open-set classifier" can achieve the highest known-class accuracy with the fewest parameters and zero additional modules. Broken down, there are three sub-challenges: First, the evaluation protocol must simultaneously cover few-shot (5/10-shot, 10-way) and open-set (20 unknown classes as negative set) and be cross-dataset; otherwise, conclusions remain closed-set illusions. Second, the candidate space must be sufficiently complete (3×3×3); otherwise, it cannot provide a "guide" but only "case studies." Third, the optimal combination must be verified on both DSCNN-L (407k parameters) and DSCNN-S (22k parameters) on-device backbones to avoid conclusions that only hold for large models and fail when applied to earbud-level small models.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire pipeline is divided into three stages (Figure 1). Stage A is offline training: the feature encoder is trained using the episode protocol on a large-scale source dataset (MSWC English partition) on the server. Once the encoder is trained, it is permanently frozen, with no further gradient interaction with the target domain. Stage B is classifier assembly: the device receives K registration utterances for each user keyword, passes them through the frozen encoder to obtain K embedding vectors, and calculates the class prototype (Equation 1) by taking the mean per class, along with the unknown class prototype, into the open-set classifier. The entire assembly process involves only K×N forward passes and storage of N+1 vectors, with no backpropagation. This is the actual meaning of "on-device customization." Stage C is inference: the test sample embedding calculates distances to all prototypes, outputting a probability vector containing the unknown class, and makes a decision based on threshold γ.

The "why" behind each design choice can be broken down as follows:
- **Why insist on prototype classifiers instead of fine-tuning**: On-device microcontrollers lack a training stack. The prototype method reduces "customization" from "training a model" to "performing a few forward passes and calculating a few means." This is the prerequisite for the entire technical route to be viable on devices. Compared to schemes requiring fine-tuning on few-shot data, the assembly cost of prototype classifiers is negligible.
- **Why enforce cross-dataset**: In real scenarios, it is impossible to predict what words users will register during training. The paper explicitly excludes the 10 target classes of GSC+ when sampling from MSWC and ensures that training and testing data come from different distributions. This directly corrects the protocol flaw of "same-distribution training and testing" and upgrades the problem from "in-domain generalization" to "cross-domain open-set generalization," making the difficulty closer to real customization.
- **Why choose DSCNN backbone**: DSCNN is the de facto standard backbone for on-device KWS, composed of stacked depthwise and pointwise convolution blocks, each followed by BatchNorm and ReLU after convolution. The two parameter sizes, 407k and 22k, exactly cover the resource tiers for "speaker-level" and "earbud-level." Moreover, "one convolution per 1-second large window" replacing "per-window stepping LSTM" is exactly the source of the 10-100x inference speed gap mentioned earlier—KWS systems running continuously are extremely sensitive to this.
- **Why use fixed training budget for comparison**: All losses are trained under the same number of epochs and episodes (40 epochs × 400 episodes), excluding the confounding variable that "some loss is just better with longer training," making the selection conclusions comparable.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Prototypical Networks (PN)**: Each episode samples S support samples and Q query samples from M random classes. The class prototype is the arithmetic mean of the support set embeddings:

$$c_j = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S} f(x_{i,j}^{S})$$

The loss minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the query sample class prediction, with the scoring function taking the negative Euclidean distance:

$$L_{PN} = -\frac{1}{Q \cdot M}\sum_{i=1}^{Q}\sum_{j=1}^{M} \log \frac{\exp(s(x_{i,j}^{Q}, j))}{\sum_{k=1}^{M}\exp(s(x_{i,j}^{Q}, k))}, \quad s(x,j) = -d_{L2}(f(x), c_j)$$

In plain language: This makes the probability that "the query sample is closest to its own class center" maximal in the softmax, which is equivalent to clustering embeddings of each class tightly and pushing the centers of different classes apart.

**Angular Prototypes (AP)**: Replaces the Euclidean distance in the scoring function with cosine similarity with a learnable scale w, bias b, and margin m: $s(x,j) = w \cdot (\cos(f(x), c_j) - m) + b$. The motivation is that cosine similarity looks only at direction, not magnitude. Combined with a non-zero margin m, it forces an angular margin between true and non-true classes. This is a mature recipe from the speaker recognition metric learning line transplanted to KWS few-shot.

**Triplet Loss (TL)**: Each episode constructs Nt triplets within the batch (anchor $x_i$, positive sample $x_i^+$, negative sample $x_i^-$, where the negative sample is randomly taken from different classes within the batch), in hinge form with a margin of 0.5:

$$L_{TL} = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t} \max(0,\; d_{L2}(x_i, x_i^+) - d_{L2}(x_i, x_i^-) + m)$$

The essential difference from PN lies in the form of the supervision signal: TL only constrains "relative distance relationships" (positive pair distance plus margin is less than negative pair distance) and does not require any absolute geometric position, giving the embedding space greater freedom. Ablation studies later will show that this freedom, combined with L2 normalization, instead fosters a cleaner class structure—one of the most important experimental findings in the paper.

**Open-Set Decision (Equation 6)**: The classifier outputs an N+1 dimensional probability vector, where $p_0$ is the score for the unknown class, and $p_{i\neq 0}$ is maximal when the distance is nearest. The sample is accepted as known class i if and only if $\arg\max p_i \geq \gamma$; otherwise, it is judged as the unknown class. γ is a manually adjustable operating point knob, shifting between precision and recall—the paper adjusts γ to the position where the negative set false acceptance rate is exactly 5% before reporting metrics.

**OpenMAX (Equation 7)**: For each known class, the tail of a Weibull distribution is fitted using the 5 largest distances from the K registration samples to the prototype. During testing, the distance from the sample to each class prototype is scaled by a coefficient $w_i^{wb} \in [0,1]$ (the closer the sample falls to the tail of that class's Weibull distribution, the closer the coefficient is to 1). The unknown class score is the negative sum of $(1-w_i^{wb}) \cdot d_{L2}$ across all classes, followed by Softmax normalization. Translation: If a sample is "like an outlier" to all known classes, probability mass is transferred to the unknown class.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Unified Evaluation Framework and Reproducible Implementation

The first innovation of the paper is in the form of contribution: pinning the fragmented design space onto the same experimental platform. A full grid comparison of 3 training losses (PN / AP / TL) × 3 output variants (CONV / RELU / NORM) × 3 open-set classifiers (openNCM / OpenMAX / DProto) is conducted under a unified data pipeline (MSWC→GSC cross-dataset, DEMAND noise augmentation), unified training budget, and unified operating point (FAR=5%), with all code open-sourced. The value of such benchmarking work lies in replacing the narrative of "my method is good" in the literature with "which combination is good under this protocol," establishing a reproducible and comparable benchmark for subsequent research. Since DProto did not release official code, the authors reproduced it themselves (Footnote 1 explicitly states this). According to the original settings, the generator produces 3 dummy prototypes for unknown classes, and the one closest to the test sample is used for scoring during inference. This reproduction itself also became a contribution of the paper.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Combination of Triplet Loss and L2 Normalized Output (TL + NORM)

The DSCNN-NORM variant follows the practice of the paper "Classification is a Strong Baseline for Metric Learning": replacing the last BatchNorm with LayerNorm and performing L2 normalization after average pooling, so that all output embeddings lie on the unit hypersphere, and class discrimination relies entirely on the "phase" (direction) of the embeddings rather than magnitude. This variant itself is not invented by this paper, but this paper discovers a strong interaction pattern between it and the training loss: PN with NORM collapses (accuracy of only 0.21 for 5-shot on DSCNN-L, Table 1), while TL with NORM tops the charts (5-shot 0.71, 10-shot 0.76, Table 1). The paper authors hypothesize that the normalized feature encoder promotes class separation processes based on embedding phase. Mechanistically, this can be understood as follows: the class prototype of PN is the arithmetic mean of normalized vectors, and the norm of the mean vector is naturally less than 1. When the directions of inter-class prototypes are already close, the Euclidean score differences in the softmax are compressed, and gradients struggle to effectively pull apart inter-class angles. In contrast, the hinge of TL only constrains relative distances. After normalization removes the magnitude degree of freedom, all optimization pressure is squeezed into the direction dimension, and the inter-class angular structure is instead forged cleaner. In other words, the "tightness of the loss function" and the "freedom of the feature space" must be designed in tandem. This is a counter-intuitive but data-solid guideline provided by the paper.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Zero-Parameter Open-Set Nearest Class Mean Classifier (openNCM)

openNCM extends the closed-set NCM to open-set: the unknown class prototype $c_0$ is estimated using K random utterances from the target domain that do not belong to user keywords (in implementation, taken from the 5 classes: backward, forward, visual, follow, learn). The probability vector is Softmax over N+1 distances; if the encoder is trained with AP loss, L2 normalization is performed first before calculating Euclidean distance to match the metric during training. Compared to two alternative routes: DProto requires jointly training a generator (adding training complexity and reproduction risk), and OpenMAX requires fitting Weibull distributions (with only 5 distance samples per class available for fitting under 5-shot, statistical modeling is inherently unreliable—Table 1 shows OpenMAX lagging significantly behind at 5-shot, only catching up at 10-shot). The additional cost of openNCM is almost zero: storing one extra prototype vector and one extra dimension in Softmax. The conclusion that "the simplest baseline is sufficient on well-trained embeddings" is the most practical pruning guideline the paper offers to engineers—it implies that on-device open-set capability need not come at the cost of any additional models.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Difference with DProto**: The optimal combination of this paper, openNCM+TL+NORM, achieves 0.76 accuracy / 0.94 AUROC for 10-shot 10-way on DSCNN-L (Table 2), while the reproduced DProto is 0.71 / 0.93. Moreover, this paper's training is cross-dataset, and the number of classes is doubled (10-way vs. 5-way), making the protocol stricter and thevalue higher. DProto does indeed have gains over openNCM on PN encoders (see ablation), but the unknown-class priors learned by the generator still cannot overcome a better-trained embedding geometry.
- **Difference with PEELER**: On DSCNN-L, both methods have持平 (equal) accuracy (0.76 vs. 0.76, Table 2), but PEELER carries an additional 6.3M parameters for the variance estimation network—more than fifteen times the backbone's 407k parameters. On DSCNN-S, PEELER leads TL by 0.60 vs. 0.56 (Table value difference 4 points, stated as +5% in the text), at the cost of an additional 341k parameters. That is, on large models, the "free openNCM" catches up with the "expensive variance modeling," and only on small models does the latter start to be worth the ticket price.
- **Difference with Classification-Style Encoder Baselines**: A classification-style encoder trained under the same framework plus openNCM (Classif+NORM) achieves only 0.52 on DSCNN-L (Table 2). The paper attributes this to the overconfidence of the softmax classifier on incorrect predictions—closed-set training never teaches the model "I don't know this thing," so its open-set scores are unreliable. This directly falsifies the naive idea that "adding a threshold to a normal classifier can do open-set."
- **Difference with End-to-End Target Domain Training**: TL+openNCM on DSCNN-L achieves 0.76, catching up with models end-to-end trained on complete target domain data (Table 2), whereas the latter consumes all target domain labeled data. The end-to-end model can reach 95% known-class accuracy at γ=0, but fails to reject unknown classes unseen during training (low accuracy on the negative set)—this precisely shows that under open-set protocols, "retraining on target data" cannot replace open-set design, but instead exposes the blind spots of closed-set training.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

**Training Side**: MSWC (Multilingual Spoken Words Corpus) English partition, sampled limited to the 500 classes with the most utterances after excluding GSC+ target classes (total number of training utterances not reported by the paper). Training samples are augmented with additive background noise from the DEMAND dataset: noise added with a uniform probability of 0.95, with signal-to-noise ratio randomly between 0 and 5 (units not specified in the original text, conventionally dB). MSWC was chosen instead of the common LibriSpeech word list to obtain a large-scale labeled source organized by word, while allowing explicit exclusion of test classes.
**Testing Side**: GSC (Google Speech Commands) dataset, 35 classes, 1 second per utterance. The paper splits it into three parts: GSC+ contains only 10 target classes (yes, no, up, down, left, right, on, off, stop, go), serving as the positive set for few-shot classification; the unknown class prototypes for openNCM are taken from random utterances of another 5 classes (backward, forward, visual, follow, learn); GSC− consists of test samples from the remaining 20 classes, specifically used to measure false acceptance. Registration utterances are taken from the original GSC train split, and evaluation is done on the test split, ensuring registration and evaluation samples are disjoint.
**Backbone and Input**: DSCNN-L has 407k parameters and outputs 256-dimensional embeddings; DSCNN-S has 22k parameters and outputs 64-dimensional embeddings. Input MFCC uses a 40 ms window with 50% stride. Power spectrum is used instead of log spectrum to save square root operations, taking the first 10 coefficients to obtain a 49×10 feature map.
**Training Details**: Fixed 40 epochs, 400 episodes per epoch; Adam optimizer with initial learning rate 0.001, decaying by 10 times after 20 epochs. The TL episode batch is 20 samples × 80 classes, margin 0.5, negative samples randomly taken from different classes within the batch; PN and AP loaders sample 40 classes per episode, 10 support and 30 query per class, with AP margin also 0.5; for DProto training, 16 of the 40 classes are marked as unknown. Results in Table 1 are averages of 3 training seeds, and test statistics are means of 10 repetitions (variance or standard deviation not reported by the paper).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Three metrics: **ACC5%+** (classification accuracy on GSC+ when γ is adjusted so that the false acceptance rate on GSC− is exactly 5%), **FRR5%+** (proportion of true keywords mistakenly rejected as unknown at the same operating point), and **AUROC**. Rationale: Open-set KWS is essentially a detector; a single closed-set accuracy cannot fully characterize the trade-off between "false alarm" and "missed alarm." Fixing FAR at 5% is equivalent to comparing the detection capabilities of various methods at a false alarm level close to product concerns (the definition of FAR is given in Footnote 2: proportion of negative samples classified as positive); FRR supplements the cost of missed detection at the same operating point; AUROC is a threshold-independent overall assessment of ranking quality, avoiding bias from operating point selection. This metric design restores "accuracy" to "joint performance of recall and rejection under fixed false positives," which is a key part of the neutrality of the entire evaluation protocol—and also the reason it can place open-set and closed-set methods on the same table.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Table 2 provides a horizontal comparison in the 10-shot 10-way open-set scenario (Accuracy / AUROC). DSCNN-L (407k parameters) line: Classification-style encoder baseline 0.52 / 0.89 at the bottom; DProto 0.71 / 0.93; TL+openNCM 0.76 / 0.94; PEELER 0.76 / 0.94; End-to-end target domain training 0.76 / 0.93. DSCNN-S (22k parameters) line: Classification baseline 0.47 / 0.85; DProto 0.44 / 0.82; TL+openNCM 0.56 / 0.89; PEELER 0.60 / 0.88 (additional 341k parameters); End-to-end 0.72 / 0.93.

Three layers of conclusions can be read from this table. First layer: TL+openNCM on the large model catches up with PEELER (saving 6.3M parameters) and the model end-to-end trained on full target domain data (saving all target domain labels) with zero additional parameters. This is a direct proof of the cost-effectiveness of the "source domain training + no-backprop assembly" route—the source of the 76% figure in the abstract. Second layer: The ranking changes after model shrinking, and PEELER's variance modeling starts to be worth the ticket price (leading by 4 points), while the advantage of end-to-end full-data training expands to 16 points. This indicates that when embedding quality is constrained by backbone capacity, the marginal benefit of target domain supervision increases, and the optimal selection on small devices differs from that on large devices. Third layer: The classification-style encoder is at the bottom in both sizes, verifying that "overconfidence of closed-set training" is a structural defect in open-set scenarios, unrelated to model size.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The grid of loss × output variant × classifier × shot number × model size in Table 1 provides four clear interaction patterns:

1. **Strong interaction between loss function and output normalization; cannot select independently.** PN is best on RELU output (0.56 for 5-shot with openNCM on DSCNN-L, 0.63 for 10-shot), but collapses to 0.21 with NORM; TL is exactly the opposite, with NORM being the optimal point (5-shot 0.71, 10-shot 0.76), 4 to 5 points higher than RELU and CONV; AP is in the middle and stably outperforms PN by 8 to 10 points (DSCNN-L, regardless of pairing with openNCM or DProto). This means any isolated conclusion in the literature that "a certain loss is better" must be conditional on the output normalization method.
2. **Strong interaction between open-set classifier and number of registration samples.** OpenMAX lags significantly behind at 5-shot (e.g., TL+DSCNN-L-NORM: 0.37 vs. openNCM's 0.71). The root cause is that only 5 distance samples per class are available to feed the Weibull fitting function, and the statistical model cannot fit it—the paper also states "the correct statistical model cannot be inferred from very few samples"; at 10-shot, OpenMAX catches up with openNCM (0.71 vs. 0.76). Statistical open-set methods have a hard lower limit on the number of registration samples, which is a pitfall easy to fall into during selection.
3. **DProto's gains are real but have a limited ceiling.** On the PN encoder, DProto gains +8 points accuracy and −9 points false rejection rate compared to openNCM in the DSCNN-L 10-shot configuration (0.71 vs. 0.63; FRR 0.28 vs. 0.37), indicating that the jointly trained dummy prototype generator indeed learned useful unknown-class structure; but the globally optimal TL+openNCM combination is still higher (0.76 vs. 0.71), and the generator's unknown-class prior cannot overcome better embedding geometry.
4. **Model size shrinking flattens or even reverses patterns.** On DSCNN-S, the gap between NORM and RELU narrows overall, and RELU surpasses NORM by 4 points when paired with OpenMAX at 10-shot (0.58 vs. 0.54); the advantage of AP over PN basically disappears. Selection conclusions for small models cannot be directly copied from large models and must be verified separately.

Another consistent pattern: Adding registration samples from 5-shot to 10-shot consistently improves accuracy for all combinations (e.g., TL+DSCNN-L-NORM from 0.71 to 0.76), with stable benefits even with double the registration utterances, indicating that the bottleneck has not saturated to the point where "more samples are useless."

## Main Contributions

1. **Unified evaluation framework for on-device KWS customization**: Cross-dataset (MSWC training, GSC testing, target classes not in source class set), open-set (10 target classes plus 20 unknown negative classes), few-shot (5/10-shot, 10-way), fixed training budget (40 epochs × 400 episodes), covering a full grid of 3 losses × 3 output variants × 3 open-set classifiers, with open-source reproducible code.
2. **A set of directly executable selection guidelines**: TL plus L2 normalized output plus openNCM is the optimal point for accuracy and parameter count; angular prototype loss is a robust second-best; PN must be paired with non-normalized output; OpenMAX requires at least 10 registration samples per class; patterns reverse on small models and need separate verification. Each has backing data from Table 1 / Table 2.
3. **Strong results for cross-dataset open-set few-shot**: 76% accuracy at 10-shot 10-way with FAR fixed at 5%, catching up with PEELER (saving 6.3M additional parameters) and target domain end-to-end training (saving all target data), with customization entirely free of backpropagation.
4. **Independent reproduction and correction of DProto**: Implementing the dummy prototype generator without official code and providing a more冷静 (calm) positioning under a stricter protocol (cross-dataset, 10-way) than the original paper.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Absolute accuracy is still significantly distant from productization.** 76% accuracy means that with a fixed 5% false acceptance rate, about one-quarter of true keywords are still mistakenly rejected (FRR5%+ for the optimal combination in Table 1 is 0.21 for 10-shot), whereas closed-set few-shot literature has reached levels of 96-97%. Open-set combined with cross-dataset raises the difficulty of the problem by a whole level; this gap itself is the current state of this technical route.
- **γ threshold relies on manual tuning.** The paper adjusts γ to the point where the negative set false acceptance rate is exactly 5% before reporting metrics. In actual deployment, either users need to provide negative samples for calibration, or an automatic calibration mechanism is needed. The paper does not provide a calibration-free solution, which is a gap between the evaluation protocol and the product.
- **Representativeness of unknown class prototypes is an implicit assumption.** openNCM's c0 relies on the setting of "randomly taking words from 5 fixed classes" (backward, forward, visual, follow, learn). Which words are selected, how many are needed, and what happens if words with pronunciation closer to the target word are used are not ablated in the paper—and the quality of the unknown prototype directly determines the false acceptance level.
- **Conclusions are bound to specific backbones and whole-window inputs.** All experiments are based on DSCNN and 1-second whole-window inputs. It is not verified whether the patterns hold for streaming per-window decoding, shorter analysis windows, or other backbones. Whether the advantage of TL+NORM is a DSCNN-specific trait is not answered by the paper.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **"On-Device" in the title lacks device empirical evidence.** The entire paper has not measured latency, memory, and energy consumption on real microcontrollers or embedded hardware. The argument for "on-device feasibility" remains at the level of indirect reasoning ("no backpropagation plus few parameters"). Even the claim that LSTM is 10-100 times slower is a cited estimate rather than actual measurement; the actual cost of K forward passes during the assembly phase is not reported by the paper. Strict readers would position this as an "offline study for screening methods for on-device customization."
- **Operating point tuning and evaluation data are not explicitly separated.** γ is tuned on GSC− to FAR=5%, and metrics are then reported on the same negative-set-related split. The paper does not state whether an independent calibration set was used, posing a risk of optimistic bias.
- **Only English tested and single test set.** Although MSWC is a multilingual corpus, the paper only used the English partition, without cross-lingual investigation; the negative set only has the 20 classes of GSC, and whether it can represent the speech distribution of the real open world is questionable.
- **Incomplete statistical reporting.** Table 1 is the mean of 3 training seeds but does not report variance. Differences of within 4 points (e.g., AP vs. TL, RELU vs. CONV) may fall within the noise interval. The PEELER small model advantage in the text is stated as +5%, but the table value calculates to 4 points. The inconsistency in these two places also indicates a loosening of precision control.
- **Coverage of selection guidelines still has gaps.** No comparison with fine-tuning-based few-shot methods (e.g., MAML, large encoder transfer learning) under the same open-set protocol, whereas these methods are exactly the opponents that the "no-backpropagation" claim needs to respond to directly.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- Quantify the latency, energy consumption, and memory usage of assembly and inference on real low-power hardware, turning the "10-100 times faster" from a cited estimate into actual measurement data, completing the last link promised by the title.
- Replace manual γ and fixed unknown prototypes with lighter open-set scoring mechanisms, such as lightweight energy scoring or parameter-free manifold variance modeling; or study active collection strategies for registration samples to pick words with the highest information content to construct unknown prototypes.
- Extend the 1-second whole window to streaming per-window plus frame-level aggregation decoding-level KWS, closer to the form of real wake-up engines, and investigate open-set behavior under frame-level decisions.
- Extended evaluation on cross-lingual and larger negative set vocabularies to verify the generalization boundaries of the design guidelines; and combining few-shot assembly with continual learning to support online customization where users can add or delete keywords later.
- The combination of TL embedding geometry and DProto unknown-class generator (better embeddings plus learned unknown priors) is a direction naturally pointed to by the data in Table 1 and Table 2, but not attempted by the paper.
