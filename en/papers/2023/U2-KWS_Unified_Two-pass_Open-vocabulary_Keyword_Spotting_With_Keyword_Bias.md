## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points
Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the task of detecting predefined keywords from a continuous audio stream, serving as the core entry point for edge devices with voice interfaces (smart speakers, in-car voice assistants, earphones, etc.). Traditional KWS adopts the HMM modeling route of keywords/fillers. With the rise of deep learning, the mainstream solution has become a single neural network directly predicting keywords or subword sequences of keywords (e.g., end-to-end models like DNN/CTC/Transformer). These closed-vocabulary schemes have two structural defects: first, each keyword requires a large amount of dedicated training data, and the model has no concept of unseen words; second, keywords cannot be changed after training, and users can only passively remember the vendor-fixed wake-up words. These two defects directly conflict with real product requirements—scenarios such as in-car and smart speakers increasingly emphasize the ability for "user-customized wake-up words." Therefore, open-vocabulary KWS, which allows users to register new keywords via text after training, has become a hot topic in recent years.

There are two technical routes for open-vocabulary KWS. The first is query-by-example (QbyE): the user registers a speech segment of the keyword, and the system compares new speech with the reference speech. The drawbacks are practical: users must record during customization, and performance is significantly affected by the quality of the reference speech (speaker, channel, and noise will all pollute the registration template). The second route is text-based: it borrows an ASR acoustic model to convert speech into phonetic posteriorgrams, and then uses HMMs or other post-processing methods to determine the existence of keywords. Text registration does not require recording and is stable across different speakers, making it the route this paper belongs to.

The core pain point of the text-based route lies at the system architecture level: in practice, a single streaming acoustic model generates a large number of false alarms because the streaming model can only see a limited right context and is optimized uniformly for all phonemes rather than specifically for keywords. Therefore, engineering practice generally introduces a multi-level cascaded strategy—the first level is a resident lightweight detector, and candidates are sent to subsequent levels for verification only after detection. Multi-level systems have many modules, complex structures, and are difficult to build and maintain. Thus, there is a trend to "unify multi-level systems into a single model," with CATT-KWS being a representative work: it borrows a two-pass ASR model, using the streaming part as the first-level detector and the non-streaming part as the second-level verifier.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods
- **Acoustic models are optimized for ASR objectives, leading to under-optimization for the KWS task.** This is the fundamental problem repeatedly emphasized in this paper. Although methods like CATT-KWS have modified the inference flow of the two-pass model (changing from "recognition + re-scoring" to "multi-level KWS"), the model itself is still trained according to ASR criteria—it optimizes for the overall recognition accuracy of all words and does not explicitly utilize keyword information. Uniformly allocating modeling capacity for all words means there is no extra tilt towards the phoneme sequences users truly care about, and the ability required for KWS ("extreme sensitivity to specific sequences") is not trained.
- **Existing keyword bias methods use only a single type of attention, unable to balance streaming and accuracy simultaneously.** Recent works inject keyword representations into the acoustic model using cross-attention, categorized into two types based on the query approach: acoustic-query attention uses acoustic representations as queries and keyword representations as keys/values, allowing frame-by-frame processing suitable for streaming, but single-frame information is limited and cannot fully model the relationship between the entire audio segment and the keyword; keyword-query attention uses the keyword as the query to perform cross-attention with the entire audio segment, capable of modeling global dependencies between the keyword and speech (its output can be interpreted as the agreement between audio and text), and can be directly used for keyword prediction. However, due to being global attention, it is not suitable for streaming inference. Existing works choose one side, effectively forcing a choice between "can stream" and "sees fully."
- **Multi-level system structures are complex.** Traditional multi-level KWS has independent modules at each level, requiring separate training, separate parameter tuning, and manual design of inter-level interfaces (candidate passing, timestamp alignment), resulting in high costs for construction and iteration.
- **The QbyE route suffers from poor registration experience and robustness** (recording dependency, speaker sensitivity issues described in the background section), while the text-based route has long been stuck by "high false alarms in single-level systems and difficulty in building multi-level systems."

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper
How to use a unified two-pass model (rather than a stack of independent multi-level modules) to simultaneously achieve three things: (1) first-level streaming detection capability (resident operation, low-latency discovery of candidates); (2) second-level high-precision verification capability (significantly reducing false alarms); (3) explicit sensitivity of the model to user-customized keywords—by injecting keyword information into both branches in an appropriate attention form, transforming the model from an "ASR generalist" to a "KWS specialist." At the same time, edge-side constraints must be maintained: the additional parameters for resident operation must be sufficiently small (this paper achieves only 0.04M additional resident parameters relative to the baseline, with a total model size of 4.75M), and the verification branch is only activated when the first level detects a candidate. The ultimate goal is: at a fixed false alarm rate of 0.5 times/hour, achieve a 41% relative improvement in wake-up rate compared to traditional custom KWS systems (abstract).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation
The name U2-KWS directly points out its lineage: it inherits from the two-pass ASR model U2/WeNet system (shared encoder + CTC streaming branch + attention decoder non-streaming branch), but the training and inference are redesigned for the KWS task. The overall architecture (Fig. 1) contains four parts:

- **Shared Encoder**: A multi-layer Conformer, trained with a dynamic chunk strategy. Conformer is chosen because it is already the mainstream backbone for streaming ASR; its convolution module is good at capturing local spectral patterns, and self-attention models long-range dependencies. The motivation for choosing dynamic chunk training is to allow the same encoder to perform forward passes in small chunks for streaming (used by the first level) and in full chunks for re-scoring (used by the second level), achieving controllable latency—this is the prerequisite for the zero-cost reuse of the encoder in the subsequent "full decoder" strategy.
- **Bias Module**: A multi-head attention layer plus a linear layer, responsible for fusing keyword information into acoustic representations, used only on the streaming branch side.
- **CTC Decoder**: A linear layer + log-softmax, outputting phonetic posteriorgrams, serving as the output head for the streaming branch.
- **Attention Decoder**: A Transformer decoder, but the input is not the decoding hypothesis of the streaming branch, but the keyword itself, performing cross-attention with the acoustic representations corresponding to the keyword, acting as the second-level verifier.

Additionally, there is a yellow **Keyword Encoder**: a single-layer LSTM (including embedding) that encodes the keyword text sequence (actually using phoneme sequences in practice) into high-level representations. The keyword encoder runs only once during system initialization (when the user inputs a custom keyword), and the generated high-level representations are reused throughout the session. This is the key engineering decision that results in only 0.04M of additional resident parameters.

Division of labor logic: The green CTC branch is the first level, detecting keyword candidates chunk-by-chunk on the audio stream; the blue attention decoder branch is the second level, activated only when the first-level score exceeds the threshold, performing keyword-level re-scoring to reduce false alarms. The essence of this design is to transform "inter-level passing in a multi-level system" into "two forward passes within the same model." During training, they are jointly optimized, and the interface (timestamp cropping) is provided by the model's own CTC posterior, eliminating the need for external alignment modules.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms
Cross-attention is the mathematical skeleton of the entire paper, uniformly written as (Equation 1):

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

The two bias methods correspond to two different approaches for taking the query:

**Acoustic Query Attention (Streaming Branch, Equations 2-3)**:
$$h_a \xrightarrow{\text{linear}} Q_a;\quad h_k \xrightarrow{\text{linear}} K_k, V_k$$
$$\tilde{h}_a = \text{Attention}(Q_a, K_k, V_k)$$

Where $h_a$ is the acoustic representation and $h_k$ is the keyword representation. The output $\tilde{h}_a$ has the same dimension as $h_a$ and can be combined with the original acoustic representation before being sent to the CTC head to predict phoneme posteriors. Since the $h_a$ of each frame only "queries" the keyword representation (K/V is a fixed-length small set), this attention can be performed frame-by-frame, naturally supporting streaming; the cost is that single-frame information is limited, allowing only frame-level biasing, and cannot model the relationship between the entire audio segment and the keyword.

**Keyword Query Attention (Non-Streaming Branch, Equations 4-5)**:
$$h_k \xrightarrow{\text{linear}} Q_k;\quad h_a \xrightarrow{\text{linear}} K_a, V_a$$
$$\tilde{h}_k = \text{Attention}(Q_k, K_a, V_a)$$

The keyword acts as the query, performing attention aggregation on the entire acoustic representation, with the output representing the matching agreement between audio and text. The information flow in this direction can fully model the correlation and temporal correspondence patterns between the keyword and speech, but it requires seeing the entire audio segment, so it can only be placed in the non-streaming branch. **Streaming uses acoustic query, non-streaming uses keyword query**—this is the most core design judgment in the paper regarding "assigning attention direction according to constraints."

**Joint Loss (Equation 6)**:
$$L = \lambda L_{ctc} + (1-\lambda) L_{att}$$

Where $L_{ctc}$ is the CTC loss for the streaming branch, $L_{att}$ is the attention loss for the decoder branch, and $\lambda$ controls the proportion between the two (the paper does not report the value of $\lambda$). Training is divided into two stages: first, train the streaming branch separately (pure CTC loss), then jointly optimize the entire two-pass model.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Dual-Path Heterogeneous Keyword Bias
Unlike previous works that use only a single type of attention, U2-KWS uses two opposite attention directions in the two branches (Fig. 2):

- **Streaming Branch (Fig. 2a)**: The keyword is first converted into an embedding via the keyword encoder; in the bias module, acoustic query attention is used to fuse keyword information into the acoustic representation of the shared encoder; the attention output is concatenated with the original acoustic representation and then projected back to the original dimension via a linear layer ("Cat Linear"). The motivation for concatenation rather than replacement is to preserve the original acoustic information from being overwritten by the bias operation—the encoder output after biasing contains both acoustic and keyword information, available for subsequent inference.
- **Non-Streaming Branch (Fig. 2b)**: The standard structure of the Transformer decoder (self-attention + cross-attention + feed-forward) is retained, but the input is fixed to the keyword rather than the decoding result of the streaming branch. At this point, the self-attention layer is responsible for encoding the keyword into a high-level representation, and the cross-attention is keyword query attention. The decoder outputs token-level posterior probabilities, and the probability of the keyword path is taken as the second-level score. The paper explicitly points out that this structure models the correlation between keyword and speech more fully but consumes more resources, so it is only used in the non-streaming branch—another trade-off of "assigning structure according to resource constraints."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Keyword Sampling Training and `<eok>` Label Augmentation
To make the model learn to be "biased by keywords," there must be positive and negative keyword samples during training, but the training corpus is standard ASR transcription without keyword annotations. This paper's approach is to **sample from the transcription text to simulate user-customized keywords**:

- Positive samples: Randomly select a continuous word subsequence from a transcription sentence as the keyword (this sentence constitutes a positive sample containing this keyword);
- Negative samples: Randomly combine words not present in the transcription sentence from the dictionary as the keyword;
- An **`<eok>`** (end of keyword) token is appended after the keyword, similar to the label-augment method in CLAS context bias. This token is the anchor for all subsequent timestamp mechanisms.

Table 1 provides a complete example (the original text illustrates with words, but phonemes are actually used in training and inference): For the sentence "Call you Jarvis", the positive sample keyword is "Jarvis", the keyword form is "Jarvis\<eok\>", the CTC target is "Call you Jarvis\<eok\>", the decoder input is "\<sos\>Jarvis", and the decoder target is "Jarvis\<eok\>"; for the negative sample keyword "Alex" (not in the sentence), the CTC target remains "Call you Jarvis" (without \<eok\>, meaning the negative sample does not teach the CTC branch to end), the decoder input is "\<sos\>Alex", and the decoder target is "\<eos>\<eos>" (directly predicting end).

**Why design the CTC target this way**: For positive samples, appending \<eok\> after the keyword forces the streaming branch to learn to "give an explicit end signal when the registered keyword is finished"—this signal is used to locate the keyword tail frame during inference. For negative samples, the CTC target does not contain \<eok\>, so the streaming branch never sends an end signal for keywords that do not appear, and the keyword path cannot be found at the first level, which is the mechanism for reducing false alarms.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Redefining the Attention Decoder as a "Keyword Re-scoring Token Classifier"
During the second stage of training, the decoder's input and target are redefined: the input is fixed to the "\<sos\> + keyword" sequence, the positive sample target is "keyword + \<eok\>", and the negative sample target makes the model directly predict \<eos\> (Table 1). The forward calculation and loss are exactly the same as the ASR attention decoder, only the semantics of the input and output are changed. The decoder trained this way is no longer a general speech decoder, but a token-level classifier focused on keyword sequence prediction: feeding in keyword tokens one by one, calculating the probability of the next token, and the probability of the entire keyword path is the second-level score. **Why not let the decoder re-score the first-level hypotheses like CATT-KWS**: Re-scoring hypotheses is still "ASR thinking," where the model's discrimination object is the recognized text; directly feeding the keyword shrinks the task to a binary classification (is this word in this audio segment?), making the task simpler and supervision more concentrated. Experiments prove this is better (see the gain brought by the decoder in the experimental section).

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Timestamp Cropping Based on Spikes
Inspired by end-to-end segmentation methods in two-pass ASR, this paper uses the timestamp information from the streaming branch to crop the encoder output before feeding it to the decoder, allowing the decoder's cross-attention to focus only on the acoustic segment containing the keyword—reducing the decoder's task complexity (it doesn't need to search for the keyword in the entire audio segment itself) and improving KWS performance. The estimation of timestamps is completed entirely by the model's own signals, without requiring external alignment:

- **Tail frame**: Take the frame with the highest posterior probability for \<eok\>;
- **Head frame**: Drawing on the idea of spike-triggered CTC, record "non-blank tokens with posterior exceeding a preset threshold" as a spike; count spikes backwards from the tail frame towards the start of the speech. When the number of spikes exceeds the length of the keyword sequence, the last counted spike is the starting frame.

The paper explains why spike counting is used instead of CTC alignment probability: the spike method derives timestamps from the number of peaks rather than specific content, requiring no additional alignment information, and the output length is more stable; although it may crop redundant segments, the decoder's cross-attention is resilient to a small amount of redundant information. Comparative experiments (Fig. 4) verify that this is the best among the three cropping strategies.

### Key Technical Innovation 5: Causal Decoder and Full Decoder
Inference flow: During the initialization phase, the keyword encoder encodes the user-input keyword into a high-level representation (only this once); the first level processes the audio stream chunk-by-chunk to obtain CTC posteriors, searches for the keyword path on the posteriors, and takes the highest probability as the first-level score; if it exceeds the threshold, the acoustic representation is cropped according to \<eok\> and non-blank spikes, and sent to the decoder for token-by-token re-scoring. The score is compared with the threshold to obtain the final decision. Since the encoder is trained with dynamic chunks, when the second level is activated, **the audio segment containing the candidate can be fed to the encoder in full-chunk mode**, obtaining a more context-complete acoustic representation for re-scoring by the decoder. This defines two second-level strategies: directly reusing the streaming encoder output is called the **causal decoder**, and re-encoding with full chunks is called the **full decoder**. The full decoder exchanges a small amount of additional latency for a stronger representation, isomorphic to the two-pass re-scoring idea of U2 ASR, but here the object of re-scoring is the keyword rather than the recognition hypothesis.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods
- **Compared to CATT-KWS (the most direct similar work)**: Both use two-pass models for multi-level KWS. The difference lies in the training objective and the role of the decoder. CATT-KWS's two-pass model is trained according to ASR criteria, and the decoder re-scores the first-level hypotheses; the model does not know what the keyword is. U2-KWS redesigns the training flow (keyword sampling, \<eok\>, positive/negative sample construction) and injects keyword information into both branches via attention, with the decoder input being the keyword itself. In one sentence: CATT-KWS is "an ASR model borrowed for KWS," while U2-KWS is "a two-pass model retrained for KWS."
- **Compared to QbyE methods**: The registration modality changes from audio to text, eliminating the need for recording and providing stability across speakers; the cost is the need for a dictionary/G2P frontend.
- **Compared to single-attention bias methods**: The acoustic query attention school gains streaming capability but has weak global modeling, while the keyword query attention school (e.g., audio-text agreement methods) has strong modeling but cannot stream. U2-KWS deploys the two query directions to the streaming/non-streaming branches respectively, taking the strengths of each—the unified framework of "assigning attention direction by branch within a single model" is the distinguishing feature of this paper.
- **Compared to traditional multi-level systems**: Cascading becomes two forward passes in a single model, with inter-level timestamps provided by the CTC's \<eok\> and spikes, eliminating the need for external aligners.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale
- **Internal In-Car Corpus**: Mandarin ASR corpus collected in hybrid electric vehicle environments, 1000 hours, used as the training set; the development set is randomly split from the training set. The positive test set is recorded in the same environment as the training: 30 different keywords, 200 samples each, totaling 3000 positive samples, with keywords consisting of 2-4 Chinese characters. The negative test set is an independent 60-hour audio corpus, mainly containing chitchat, commands, and radio broadcasts. All data is anonymized and manually transcribed. The value of this setup lies in the fact that both training and testing are in real in-car noise environments, making the evaluation conclusions directly meaningful for in-car wake-up scenarios.
- **AISHELL-1**: Public Mandarin corpus, train/dev used for training and validation; the test set is constructed from the AISHELL-1 test set using the same keyword sampling method as training, containing 7176 pairs of positive and negative samples. The motivation for public set experiments is to ensure reproducibility.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics
- **ROC Curve**: For each keyword, generated using its positive samples plus the 60-hour negative test set; to provide an overall evaluation for all keywords, the false rejection rate of all keywords is averaged at the same false alarm rate to draw the overall ROC. Rationale: KWS is an extremely imbalanced detection task, where accuracy alone is meaningless; comparison must be made on the "missed wake-up vs. false alarm" trade-off curve, and averaging at a fixed false alarm rate allows the difficulty of different keywords to be fairly summarized.
- **F1 Score**: F1 is calculated for each keyword and then averaged. Rationale: F1 constrains both precision and recall, making it suitable for single-point summary comparison using sampled positive/negative sample sets (such as the 7176 pairs in AISHELL-1), supplementing the overall quantification beyond the ROC curve.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods
Model Configuration (Sec. 3.2): 80-dimensional log Mel filter bank features, 25ms window length, 10ms frame shift; SpecAugment data augmentation (2 frequency masks, max F=10; 2 time masks, max T=50); two-level convolutional subsampling in the encoder frontend (kernel 3×3, stride 2). The baseline CTC model is a 12-layer Conformer, 128-dimensional input, 4 self-attention heads, 256 linear units; the large baseline expands the input to 256 dimensions and linear units to 2048, with a parameter count 9 times that of the baseline. Keyword bias parts: the keyword encoder is embedding + single-layer LSTM (128 dimensions); the bias module is 128 hidden states, 4-head multi-head attention; the decoder branch is a 2-layer Transformer, 512 linear units. All models are trained with dynamic chunks and inferred with chunk=8. Output units are 210 context-independent (CI) phonemes plus \<sos/eos\> and \<eok\>. Parameter counts: encoder 3.75M, CTC decoder 0.2M, attention decoder 0.6M, keyword encoder 0.16M, bias module 0.04M, totaling 4.75M. Key ledger: the keyword encoder runs only at initialization, and the decoder activates only after the first level detects a candidate, so the additional resident parameters relative to the baseline are only the 0.04M of the bias module—this is the quantitative basis for the claim of "suitability for edge deployment."

Internal Test Set Results (Fig. 3 and Table 2):

- After adding keyword bias to the baseline (encoder + CTC decoder) to become the first-level model, the wake-up rate increased by 20% relative to the baseline under the condition of 0.5 false alarms/hour. This independently verifies the core hypothesis that "injecting keyword information is very effective for open-vocabulary KWS," and the gain costs only 0.04M resident parameters.
- The causal decoder of the second level (reusing streaming encoder output for re-scoring) further improved the wake-up rate by 6% at 0.5 false alarms/hour, due to better utilization of context.
- The full decoder (full-chunk re-encoding) improved by 10% under the condition of 0.25 false alarms/hour, exchanging a small amount of additional latency for a more accurate acoustic representation.
- Cumulative effect of each level: The abstract reports a 41% relative improvement in wake-up rate relative to traditional custom KWS systems at the anchor point of 0.5 false alarms/hour.

Table 2 shows the F1 scores for different word lengths (Overall / 2-char / 3-char / 4-char): baseline 0.790 / 0.711 / 0.813 / 0.847; +Keyword Bias (Encoder Integration) 0.872 / 0.791 / 0.893 / 0.932; +Causal Decoder 0.894 / 0.793 / 0.926 / 0.963; +Full Decoder 0.910 / 0.807 / 0.946 / 0.977. It can be seen that 2-character keywords are the most difficult low point in the field (baseline only 0.711, full model only reaches 0.807), while 4-character keywords reach 0.977 with the full model—the impact of word length on performance is a variable that system design must正视 (face squarely).

Results on AISHELL-1 (Fig. 5): U2-KWS significantly outperforms the baseline and the 9-times larger large baseline (ROC x-axis up to 4.0 false alarms/hour). The value of this comparison lies in the fact that stacking parameters (9-times large model) is inferior to changing the training method—the 4.75M model with keyword bias + two-pass verification actually beats the large model, indicating that the bottleneck of the baseline is not capacity but task alignment (the model was not taught to be a KWS specialist). The conclusions on the public set are consistent with the internal in-car set, verifying the generalization of the method. The paper does not report specific numerical tables for AISHELL-1 (only the ROC curve figure).

### Findings from Ablation Experiments
- **Impact of Keyword Length (Table 2)**: Performance improves as the keyword gets longer, and the gain brought by the decoder branch is larger for longer keywords. The paper gives two reasons: first, the structural advantage of the decoder "fully utilizing context" becomes smaller on short sequences (2-character words are inherently short, with limited decodable context available); second, longer sequences require more steps in the decoder, allowing the decoder's discrimination capability to be fully exerted. At the same time, short keywords are more prone to false alarms (the F1 range of 0.711-0.807 for 2-character words confirms this), which explains why short-word scenarios rely more on the second-level verification but benefit the least from it—this is a structural shortcoming of this method.
- **Decoder Cropping Strategies (Fig. 4)**: Comparing three encoder output cropping methods using the causal decoder—no cropping, CTC alignment cropping, and spike cropping. It was found that: when not cropped, the task of "searching for the keyword in the acoustic representation" is entirely pressed onto the decoder's cross-attention, increasing task complexity and resulting in the worst performance; after cropping using first-level timestamps, the decoder focuses on the keyword verification task, and cropping is better than no cropping; the CTC alignment method has very accurate timestamps on clean positive samples, but outputs too long or too short cropping ranges for negative samples and difficult positive samples, leading to decoder instability; the spike method is based on peak counting rather than specific content, with more stable cropping ranges. Although it may crop in redundancy, the decoder's cross-attention is resilient to a small amount of redundancy, making it the best overall. The deep insight from this ablation: the quality of the inter-level interface in two-pass KWS does not depend on the "precision" of the timestamp, but on the "stability" of the timestamp on difficult samples.
- Not reported by the paper: specific values for the spike trigger threshold, two-stage thresholds (first-level/second-level), the value of the $\lambda$ loss weight, training epochs and optimizer details, and specific millisecond-level latency and RTF numbers.

## Main Contributions
1. **Proposed the first unified two-pass open-vocabulary KWS framework (U2-KWS) with a training flow redesigned for the KWS task**: Unlike the CATT-KWS route of directly repurposing ASR two-pass models, this paper trains the two-pass model as a "detection + verification" KWS specialist from three levels: loss, sample construction, and decoder input/output, solving the KWS under-optimization problem caused by acoustic models being optimized for ASR.
2. **Dual-path heterogeneous keyword bias**: Using acoustic query attention (processable frame-by-frame) in the streaming branch and keyword query attention (modeling global audio-keyword agreement) in the non-streaming branch, this is the first time attention direction is assigned by branch according to streaming constraints within a unified framework, simultaneously preserving streaming capability and global modeling capability.
3. **Self-supervised-style positive/negative sample construction via keyword sampling + `<eok>` label augmentation**: Simulating training for user-customized keywords using ASR corpus without keyword annotations. The \<eok\> serves simultaneously as the CTC end signal, timestamp anchor, and decoder target boundary, with one token supporting three mechanisms.
4. **Alignment-free timestamp cropping based on spikes**: Using \<eok\> peaks to determine the tail frame and non-blank spike counting to determine the head frame, replacing external aligners, and proving more stable on negative and difficult samples than CTC alignment (verified by ablation in Fig. 4).
5. **Edge-friendly activation-based architecture**: The keyword encoder runs only at initialization, and the decoder runs only when candidates are activated, with only 0.04M additional resident parameters and 4.75M total parameters; achieving a 41% relative improvement in wake-up rate at 0.5 false alarms/hour on the internal in-car set (abstract), and significantly exceeding the 9-times larger baseline on AISHELL-1 (Fig. 5), proving that "changing task alignment" is superior to "stacking capacity."

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method
- **Threshold coupling problem in two-pass cascading**: The first-level CTC threshold determines when the second level activates, and candidates missed by the first level can never be rescued by the second level. The two thresholds need joint tuning, but the paper does not report threshold values or joint tuning methods. In actual deployment, each new keyword may require re-tuning—diminishing the "openness" of "open-vocabulary" at the threshold level.
- **Redundancy introduced by spike cropping**: The paper itself admits that the spike method may crop in redundant segments, relying on the decoder's cross-attention resilience to redundancy as a fallback; for long audio or speakers with extreme speech rates, whether the criterion of counting spikes exceeding the keyword sequence length remains robust is not analyzed at the boundaries.
- **Latency cost of the full decoder is not quantified**: Full-chunk re-encoding requires re-forwarding the encoder. The paper only states "introduces minimal latency" but does not report RTF or millisecond-level latency numbers, making it impossible to judge applicability for strict low-latency edge scenarios (such as earphones) from the paper.
- **Negative sample construction is too simple**: Negative samples are randomly combined words from the dictionary. Difficult negative samples with similar pronunciation to the registered keyword (homophones, longer/shorter phrases containing the keyword, e.g., "Xiao Jarvis tells stories" containing "Jarvis") are not explicitly mined, and such near-phoneme confusion is exactly the main source of false alarms in open-vocabulary KWS.
- **Keyword query attention only takes effect in the second level**: The first level (the part that is truly resident and streaming) only enjoys frame-level biasing, while global consistency modeling capability is left only for the on-demand activated verifier; if the first level has a high false detection rate, the computational waste of the second level also increases.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design
- **Limited scale and type of keywords**: The internal test only includes 30 keywords, all 2-4 character Chinese words; scenarios involving English, multi-syllable long words, and parallel registration of multiple keywords were not tested.
- **AISHELL-1 test set constructed using the same sampling method as training**: The distribution of negative samples in the test set is同源 (homologous) to the training distribution, and the evaluation may be overly optimistic; there is a lack of truly independent open-vocabulary evaluation protocols (such as fixed keyword sets tested across speakers and channels).
- **Lack of direct comparison with similar SOTA methods**: All comparison objects are its own baseline and enlarged baseline. Neighboring works such as CATT-KWS, audio-text agreement, and QbyE methods are not compared in the same table. The reference system details for the "41% relative improvement compared to traditional custom KWS systems" (abstract statement) are not expanded into a table in the main text.
- **Key numerical values rely on reading graphs**: AISHELL-1 results only have the ROC curve (Fig. 5) without numerical tables; values for $\lambda$, spike threshold, and absolute false rejection rates at various false alarm rates are not reported, requiring independent摸索 (exploration) for reproduction.
- **Single in-car environment**: The internal set is from a hybrid vehicle environment, covering neither quiet home environments, far-field scenarios, nor multi-speaker scenarios; speaker generalization and accent robustness are not analyzed.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement
- **End-to-end joint training and threshold learning**: Incorporate the selection of two-level thresholds into the training objective (e.g., using differentiable detection loss or reinforcement learning to jointly optimize detection-verification-gating), reducing parameter tuning costs during deployment.
- **Hard negative sample mining**: Construct near-phoneme negative samples based on phoneme overlap degree during keyword sampling, and generate adversarial negative examples using pronunciation confusion networks, specifically targeting first-level false detections and short-word false alarms.
- **Specialized enhancement for short keywords**: 2-character words are a performance low point (the full model only reaches 0.807 in Table 2). It is possible to explore phoneme-level acoustic models, streaming encoders with longer acoustic context, or second-level structures specifically for short words (such as frame-synchronous verification).
- **Multi-keyword and online registration**: The keyword encoder already supports text input. Extending to parallel biasing of multiple keywords (attention capacity allocation), zero-shot immediate effect upon registration, and adaptation with a small amount of audio after registration is a natural path for productization.
- **Integration with Transducer and streaming full context**: Introducing RNN-T into the first level, or approximating the full context representation of the full decoder using limited right context or intra-block full attention, can achieve a cheaper trade-off between latency and accuracy than "re-encoding."
- **Cross-lingual and G2P robustness**: Extending to multi-language/dialect keyword registration, studying the impact and mitigation of G2P errors on the text-based registration route (such as phoneme grid input).
