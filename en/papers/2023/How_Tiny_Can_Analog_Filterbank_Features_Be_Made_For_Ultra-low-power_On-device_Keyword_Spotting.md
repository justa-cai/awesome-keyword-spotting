## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The classic two-stage structure of Keyword Spotting (KWS) systems is "frontend feature extractor + backend neural network." The biggest difference between wake-word detection and general speech tasks is that it must operate in a 24/7 always-on listening mode. This "always-on" attribute dictates that the power budget for the entire audio pipeline is compressed to the microwatt level (Reference [6]’s Vocell chip targets 10 $\mu$W-level KWS), rather than the milliwatt level of on-demand wake scenarios. The paper opens by stating that pushing on-device KWS to ultra-low power levels is a key component in fulfilling the promise of pervasive intelligence.

The most striking observation of this paper is the flip in the bottleneck location. Over the past decade, the focus of KWS research has been almost entirely on the neural network side (References [4][12][16] cited in the paper refer to DNN-small KWS, CNN-small KWS, and Residual-small KWS, respectively), with model compression, quantization, and distillation being deployed in turn. This carries the implicit assumption that "the computational bottleneck lies in the model." However, chip-level measurements break this assumption: in an advanced KWS chip [6], the power bottleneck is not the backend neural network, but the frontend feature extractor—which consumes approximately 70% of the total KWS system power (Section 1). In other words, cutting the backend network by ten times would barely change the total system power; the real savings must come from the frontend. This is why "high-efficiency frontend feature extraction" deserves dedicated research.

There is a paradigm debate in frontend feature extraction (Fig. 1). The traditional paradigm is ADC-DSP: after the microphone and amplifier, Nyquist-rate analog-to-digital conversion is performed first, followed by signal processing in the digital domain to extract features. The unconventional but reviving paradigm is ASP-ADC: analog signal processing (Analog Signal Processing) is used first to perform frequency decomposition and envelope detection, pushing the ADC downstream to only quantize at the feature rate (far below the Nyquist rate). The energy efficiency gap is real: the paper states that the lowest-power ASP-ADC frontend [22] is 12 times more energy-efficient than the lowest-power ADC-DSP frontend (amplifier and ADC in [1], DSP in [14]) (Section 1). The cost of ASP-ADC is poor programmability and high variability, but the benefits of this route cover not only audio tinyML but also vision tinyML [10], so the paper argues that research into it has broad value.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The core module of the ASP-ADC paradigm is the analog filterbank within the ASP, which is precisely the环节 with the least design consensus in the entire chain. Table 1 summarizes the values for four advanced analog feature extraction chips: JSSC'22 [11] uses 31 filters, max frequency 4kHz, Q=3; JSSC'22 [7] uses 16 filters, 10kHz, Q=2; JSSC'21 [22] uses 16 filters, 5kHz, Q=2; TCASI'21 [20] uses 32 filters, 8kHz, Q up to 26. Taking the average yields "typical values": 24 filters, 7kHz, Q=8. The problem is obvious—four chips targeting the same task (KWS) differ by 2x in filter count, 2.5x in max frequency, and 13x in Q. This is not a difference in style, but a manifestation of "nobody knows."

The paper provides quantitative evidence for why this divergence is fatal: the power consumption of the filterbank is first-order proportional to each of the three architectural parameters (citing [18], under two premises: the SNR of each filter remains constant; the other two parameters remain constant). That is, just in terms of Q, the value of 26 in [20] relative to 2 in [22] could consume more than an order of magnitude more power. The lack of consensus in the context of first-order power proportionality almost inevitably leads to systematic over-design—chip designers, lacking data support, tend to stack parameters to ensure accuracy, and always-on devices pay for this conservatism in the long run.

A deeper shortcoming lies in the methodology: why has consensus not been formed in the past? Because the traditional practice of chip papers bundles architectural parameters, circuit noise, distortion, and other non-idealities together, measuring accuracy once after tape-out, and none report confidence intervals ([7][11][20][22], Section 3.3). The accuracy of a single network training inherently has random fluctuations; a one or two percentage point parameter effect is completely drowned out by training noise; the "net effect" of architectural parameters on accuracy has never been isolated and measured. The paper also makes an important conceptual distinction: architectural parameters (N, fmax, Q) and parameters characterizing analog non-idealities (noise and distortion) are two different categories, both strongly affecting power consumption; in the analog design flow, architectural parameters are usually defined first, followed by noise/distortion metrics, so the paper chooses to sweep architectural parameters first, leaving noise/distortion for subsequent work—this order itself respects the chip design flow.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The core question in one sentence: How small can the analog filterbank be made without appreciably harming the KWS accuracy passed down through the backend neural network. To answer this question rigorously, four sub-challenges must be solved simultaneously. First, how to define and measure "appreciable harm"—there must be a real backend network, end-to-end downstream accuracy, and it must come with confidence intervals; otherwise, the conclusion is not credible. Second, how to isolate attribution—the backend network architecture must be fixed, training hyperparameters must be fixed, and analog noise/distortion must be excluded from the model, so that changes in the accuracy curve can be attributed to the architectural parameters themselves. Third, how wide to sweep—the sweep range must extend far below and far above the values of existing chips, otherwise the boundary of accuracy collapse cannot be found. Fourth, how to translate the accuracy curve into power conclusions—using the first-order proportionality relationship, directly convert the "accuracy inflection point" into "power multiples." Putting these four things together constitutes the path from "lack of consensus" to "approaching the fundamental power limit."

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The complete form of the system pipeline is: Microphone $\rightarrow$ Amplifier $\rightarrow$ ASP (Analog Filterbank + Analog Envelope Detector Bank) $\rightarrow$ ADC (Feature-rate Quantization) $\rightarrow$ Digital Neural Network $\rightarrow$ Keyword Output. The filterbank consists of a set of bandpass filters that decompose the input signal into frequency components; the envelope detector bank detects the envelope (average power metric) channel by channel; thus, the output of the analog feature extractor is a feature vector, where each element corresponds to the time-varying value of the short-time average power in a certain frequency subband; stacking the feature column vectors horizontally along time yields a spectrogram, which serves as the input to the backend network (Section 2.1). This structure is mathematically equivalent to "analog Mel-filterbank energy," except that all processing is completed before quantization.

The simulation implementation is a custom MATLAB model, consisting of a "programmable filterbank + fixed envelope detector bank." The division of fixed and free parameters is very restrained, with every cut having a reason. The two parameters of the envelope detector (window length, window overlap) are not swept, for two reasons: advanced chips have reached a consensus (window length 20-32ms, overlap 10-16ms), and their impact on the power consumption of the envelope detector is inherently weak—here fixed at 20ms window length and 10ms overlap. Secondary parameters of the filterbank are also all fixed: the center frequency of the lowest bandpass filter is fixed at 100Hz (industry common 30-100Hz, and the power consumption of the lowest frequency filter accounts for a small proportion of the whole library, with weak impact); the center frequencies of the whole library follow an exponential distribution (biological cochlea motivation [8], some chips use Mel [7] or ERB [20] distributions); the filter order is set to second-order (existing chip accuracy is already sufficient, higher orders increase power consumption proportionally); the Q factor of the whole library is set to the same value. The only truly programmable and swept target parameters are three: filter count, maximum frequency of the filterbank (defined as the center frequency of the highest frequency bandpass filter), and filter Q factor. Fig. 2 visualizes the meaning of the three parameters using extreme value frequency response curves: (a)(d) compare 64 vs. 4 filters, (b)(e) compare 10kHz vs. 0.25kHz max frequency, (c)(f) compare Q=60 vs. 0.2—high Q means extremely narrow passband and wide passband gaps; low Q means filters have almost no selectivity and are redundant with each other.

This design of "fixing everything that can be fixed" is essentially a large-scale controlled variable experiment: if the backend, hyperparameters, secondary parameters, noise, and distortion are all unchanged, then any change in accuracy with respect to the three parameters can only come from the three parameters themselves. This is transplanting the discipline of ablation experiments from the software side to the analog frontend design space.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The definition of Q is the mathematical cornerstone of the entire paper: Q equals the center frequency divided by the 3dB bandwidth, i.e., the reciprocal of the fractional bandwidth, measuring the frequency selectivity of the bandpass filter (Section 2.2). This definition directly determines the bidirectional coupling of power and accuracy: high Q means accurate capture of energy at specific frequencies (accuracy benefit) but requires higher precision, lower noise resonant circuits to maintain the narrow passband (power cost); low Q means cheaper circuits but redundant information in adjacent channels and reduced discriminative power.

The first-order power model comes from Tsividis's SNR-Dynamic Range-Power triangle [18]: $P_{filterbank}$ is first-order proportional to the product of the three architectural parameters, provided that the SNR of each filter remains constant and only one parameter is changed at a time. Circuit intuition can explain "why" this way: the number of filters is a linear superposition of circuit instances; adding one channel adds one set of bias currents; the maximum frequency determines the rate level the circuit needs to support; the higher the center frequency, the larger the transconductance (i.e., bias current) required to maintain the same dynamic range; the Q factor determines the precision requirements for energy storage and amplification; the loop gain and noise margin of high-Q resonators must be bought with current. This explains why the paper says architectural parameters are "specified before" noise/distortion—the analog design flow is naturally to define architecture first and then suppress noise/distortion; the conclusion of 33.6x power ratio only holds under the premise that "SNR does not drop," which is one of the most critical assumptions in the full text that needs to be verified by subsequent work.

Exponential center frequency distribution combined with second-order filters and uniform Q across the library produces two geometric features (Footnotes 3, 4 of Fig. 2): center frequencies are linearly arranged on the logarithmic frequency axis; the 3dB bandwidths of all bandpass filters appear identical on the logarithmic frequency axis. This is consistent with the logarithmic frequency mapping of the cochlea.

The backend network is a small residual convolutional network res16 (original text in Section 3.3; written as res15 in Section 5, inconsistent naming within the paper, both referring to Tang and Lin's [16]), implemented using MATLAB Deep Learning Toolbox. The reason for choosing it is not optimal accuracy, but rather "it is the network used in the KWS demonstration of published analog audio feature extraction chips with optimal power and area"—meaning the simulation conclusions are directly comparable to the chip community. Training configurations are all fixed: SGD with momentum 0.9, initial learning rate 1 decaying by 0.9 times per epoch, 25 epochs total, minibatch size 64, L2 regularization coefficient 0.001; these hyperparameters were manually tuned on the validation set after feature extraction with typical filterbank parameters (24 filters, 7kHz, Q=8). Fixed hyperparameters are part of the isolation logic: if each sweep point tunes parameters individually, parameter effects and tuning effects would be mixed together.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Three-Parameter Independent Sweep Methodology with Confidence Intervals

This is the most fundamental form of contribution in the paper. The sweep ranges for the three parameters are: filter count takes 15 values: 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 48, 64; max frequency takes 15 values from 0.25 to 20kHz; Q factor takes 17 values from 0.2 to 60—totaling 47 sweep points. The principle for selecting the range is "extending far below and far above existing chip values" (Section 3.2), which ensures that the boundary of accuracy collapse can be found rather than just interpolating near chip values. Each sweep point corresponds to an independent instance of the analog feature extractor: the entire feature extraction front dataset is reprocessed using this instance to produce a new spectrogram dataset, the backend network is retrained and retested, and each point independently conducts 3 trials to construct a 95% confidence interval (eventually encrypted to 5 trials for comparison in Fig. 6). The insistence on confidence intervals is because analog feature extraction chip papers have never reported them (the paper explicitly names [7][11][20][22]), and the core conclusion of this paper falls exactly on the magnitude of "1.8 percentage points," which can be completely drowned out by single-training noise—without statistical tools, the conclusion does not hold. To support Figs. 3-5, a total of 141 training sessions are required (47 points $\times$ 3 trials), which is also the direct reason why the dataset had to be made small.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Selection Principle for "Tiny" Parameter Combinations—Riding the Inflection Point Rather Than Taking Optimal Values Per Parameter

The final tiny filterbank parameters are: 10 filters, max frequency 2kHz, Q=2. Relative to typical values (24, 7kHz, 8), they are reduced by 2.4x, 3.5x, and 4x respectively, with the product of the three yielding a 33.6x reduction in power consumption (Section 4.4). What is truly worth chewing on is the selection logic, which is not "taking the accuracy-optimal value for each parameter individually": the accuracy curves for filter count and max frequency both have plateau regions (accuracy basically does not move above 16 filters and 2kHz), and the tiny values are taken near the edge of the plateau; while the curve for Q factor is non-monotonic, with the optimal point at 4—the typical value of 8 itself falls in the shallow decay area to the right of the optimal point (pure waste relative to the optimal point), and the tiny value of 2 crosses the optimal point, pressing into the "rapid decay below 4" region. Daring to cross the boundary like this is because the absolute accuracy loss is still small (capped by the total bill of 1.8%), while Q is the parameter with the largest reduction among the three (4x). In other words, this is a combination decision of "advancing for power savings, stopping loss for accuracy": moving as far as possible in the slow-changing direction of the accuracy curve, and only walking to the position allowed by the total budget in the steep-changing direction. This selection strategy based on curve shape is closer to the power objective than "grid search for optimal values per parameter," and also explains why the final combination is (10, 2kHz, 2) rather than the superficially optimal (16, 2kHz, 4).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Establishing Mechanistic Intuition via Spectrogram Resolvability

The paper does not stop at digital comparisons, but provides three sets of visual evidence in Fig. 7: frequency response comparison of typical vs. tiny filterbanks (a)(d), spectrogram comparison of the word "yes" (b)(e), and confusion matrix comparison (c)(f). The tiny spectrogram is obviously "lower resolution"—fewer channels, lower frequency upper limit, wider passbands—but the two regions visible to the naked eye in the typical spectrogram (corresponding to the low-frequency "ye" and high-frequency "s" in "yes") can still be recognized in the tiny spectrogram (Footnote 5 honestly admits that the "s" segment is indeed hard to recognize). The value of this observation is providing mechanistic intuition for "why tiny does not collapse": the main part of discriminative information is hidden in the low-resolution energy envelope; although the energy of a few high-frequency details (fricatives) becomes blurred, it is not enough to drag down the end-to-end accuracy. The confusion matrix supplements this with a per-class perspective.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to analog feature extraction chip papers ([7][11][20][22]): they are engineering testimonies of "design-tapeout-measure accuracy once," while this paper is scientific measurement of "modeling-sweep-statistical inference"; the conclusions of this paper can guide the parameter specifications of the next round of tape-outs in reverse. Compared to the learnable filterbank route (PEAF [3], filterbank learning [9]): those works learn the "shape" of the filterbank (how to arrange center frequencies and bandwidths), while this paper answers the "lower size limit" under a fixed structure (how low can count, upper limit, and Q be pressed while still being sufficient); the two are orthogonal and can be stacked. Compared to digital frontends (calculating MFCC on DSP): the problem in this paper only exists within the ASP-ADC paradigm, and the paradigm diagram in Fig. 1 first clarifies the territory. Another easily overlooked difference: the evaluation metric in this paper is "downstream accuracy passed through a real backend network," rather than information content or reconstruction error at the feature level—this ensures that the conclusions are directly aligned with final task metrics.

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

The feature extraction front dataset is based on Google Speech Commands Dataset v0.02 [21]: 1-second long, 16-bit, 16kS/s audio signals, totaling 35 words. 10 of them are taken as keyword classes: yes, no, up, down, left, right, on, off, stop, go; the remaining 25 words are grouped into the 11th class "unknown" (Section 3.1). The composition of the small training set is: 200 training samples per keyword class, 800 for the unknown class, totaling 2,800 training samples. The reason for giving the unknown class 4 times the sample size is clearly written in the paper: this class is shared by 25 different words; without equal rights, its accuracy would be disadvantaged relative to the keyword classes. GSCD provides at least 3,000 samples per word, but only 200 are taken for each keyword, due to computational constraints—Figs. 3-5 require 141 training sessions. The remaining samples were not wasted; they were used to scale the validation and test sets to the same size, forming a 1:1:1 train:val:test split to improve the quality of test accuracy estimation. The final comparison (Figs. 6, 7) switched to a training set 10 times larger (28,000 training samples); under this configuration, the specific scale and composition of the test set were not explicitly reported by the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric is 10-word 11-class classification accuracy (including the unknown class), with a random guessing baseline of 9.1% (1 out of 11 classes). The reasons for selection are threefold: consistent with the caliber of chip KWS demonstrations, allowing conclusions to directly converse with chip literature; accuracy directly reflects end-to-end usability, without introducing additional mapping assumptions of feature-level metrics; combined with 95% confidence intervals (3 trials; 5 trials for Fig. 6), it can separate effects at the "1.8 percentage point" level from training noise. It should be noted that metrics commonly used in KWS chip literature, such as false alarm rate, miss rate, and energy per inference, are not adopted in this paper, and the paper does not report these metrics.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The core comparison is in Fig. 6 (28,000 sample training set): the downstream 10-word KWS accuracy of the typical filterbank (24 filters, 7kHz, Q=8) is 94.2%, and the tiny filterbank (10 filters, 2kHz, Q=2) reduces it by only 1.8%—calculated from these two numbers, the absolute accuracy of tiny is approximately 92.4%—while according to the first-order power model, the power of tiny is 33.6 times lower. The paper qualitatively describes this trade-off as "the cost paid for such extreme power saving is moderate" (Section 4.4), while reminding in Section 5: whether this deal is worth it ultimately depends on application requirements.

The cross-dataset scale effect itself is a valuable control: the same typical filterbank achieves 90.9% accuracy on the small training set (2,800) (red line in Figs. 3-5) and 94.2% on the large training set (28,000), a difference of 3.3%—the paper explicitly attributes this to the neural network's preference for more training data (beginning of Section 4). This control has two implications: first, the absolute accuracy of the small-set sweep curves is systematically underestimated; when reading Figs. 3-5, one should only look at the shape, not the absolute values; second, the 1.8% gap between tiny and typical is measured on the large set, indicating that this gap has already excluded the confounding factor of under-training.

The horizontal comparison with the values of each chip in Table 1 is also worth expanding: every parameter of tiny is pressed to the boundary or beyond the range of all chip values—the chip with the lowest filter count uses 16, tiny uses 10; the chip with the lowest max frequency uses 4kHz, tiny uses 2kHz; the chip with the lowest Q uses 2, tiny matches at 2. That is, no existing chip has pressed all three parameters to the lower limit simultaneously, and the entire space between the typical value (mean 24, 7kHz, 8) and tiny is potential power savings. The single-feature limit is equally shocking: with only 1 filter, the accuracy is still 62.2% (Fig. 3), far higher than the random baseline of 9.1%—this single dimension of energy envelope carries most of the discriminative information.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The body of this paper is essentially a set of ablation experiments, with three curves each revealing findings.

Filter Count (Fig. 3): Accuracy increases monotonically with count until a plateau appears, which is expected; what is truly unexpected is the shape of the plateau—the accuracy of 64 filters is roughly the same as that of as few as 16 filters. The paper points out that this is surprising to heuristic expectations: usually, only diminishing marginal returns are expected, not complete saturation. Why is the plateau worth being surprised? Because intuitively, more subbands should carry a little more information; but the experiment shows that for this 10-word task, the incremental information after 16 channels is zero after being passed through the backend network. Engineering implication: using more than 16 filters for 10-word KWS is pure power waste.

Max Frequency (Fig. 4): A plateau at the high-frequency end is completely unexpected—speech bandwidth is limited to 4kHz [8]; when the max frequency is set too high, the output of the highest frequency filter is constantly zero, carrying zero information, so accuracy naturally stops rising. What is truly unexpected is the low-frequency end: accuracy does not start to drop significantly until the max frequency falls below 2kHz. Why is this unexpected? Because speech bandwidth is clearly 4kHz; intuitively, cutting half the bandwidth should be felt; but the experiment shows that the discriminative performance energy of these 10 words is concentrated below 2kHz. Engineering implication: designs with max frequency greater than 2kHz are paying for invalid bandwidth.

Q Factor (Fig. 5): The only non-monotonic one among the three parameters. Accuracy is low at the low Q end because filter selectivity is poor and information between them is redundant (visualized in Fig. 2f); accuracy also drops at the high Q end because the passband is too narrow, leading to too wide passband gaps, and signal energy within the gaps is lost (visualized in Fig. 2c). Thus, there exists an optimal value, experimentally measured at Q=4: accuracy decays rapidly below it, and slowly above it. Engineering implication: Q greater than 4 is waste—and the typical value of 8 is already in the waste zone.

Additionally, there are two implicit ablations. One is training set scale ablation (2,800 vs. 28,000, same filterbank, 90.9% vs. 94.2%), which has been expanded above. The other is parameter interaction: the paper does not report any two-parameter or three-parameter joint sweeps; the sweep method is One-Factor-At-a-Time (OFAT); the tiny combination is only verified as a single point in Fig. 6; whether interaction effects exist is a question to be retained when extrapolating results. The confusion matrix (Fig. 7c, f) provides a per-class perspective, but the numerical values of per-class accuracy are not reported by the paper, only presented as figures.

## Main Contributions

First, transforming "how small the analog filterbank can be made" from an empirical question to a statistically answerable one: 47-point sweep, retraining and retesting at each point, 95% confidence intervals from 3 to 5 trials—this methodology is blank in the literature of analog feature extraction chips (the paper explicitly names four top-journal chip papers that do not report confidence intervals).

Second, providing the parameter combination for the tiny filterbank (10 filters, max frequency 2kHz, Q=2), and quantifying the trade-off: 33.6x reduction in filterbank power consumption, exchanging for only 1.8 percentage points of downstream 10-word KWS accuracy loss (Fig. 6). This is a design point that can be directly written into the specification sheet of the next chip.

Third, leaving three transferable criteria for over-design: for 10-word KWS, filter count exceeding 16, max frequency exceeding 2kHz, and Q factor exceeding 4 are all power waste (Figs. 3-5). The significance of these criteria extends beyond the specific network and dataset of this paper, because they characterize the information structure of the task (10 short words), rather than the preferences of a certain model—although this point still needs verification by subsequent work.

Fourth, establishing a research roadmap of "architecture parameters first, noise/distortion second": this paper explicitly excludes noise and distortion from the model to isolate architectural effects, and paves the methodological track for the next step of sweeping SNR and linearity.

Fifth, re-anchoring the direction of effort investment: the frontend accounts for about 70% of system power (chip [6]); the ASP-ADC paradigm has a 12x energy efficiency advantage relative to ADC-DSP ([22] vs. [1][14]); these two sets of numbers together indicate that the next stage of power saving space for on-device KWS is mainly in the analog frontend, not the neural network side which has been fully studied.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

The ideal model assumption is the biggest soft spot. The noise and distortion of the filterbank are completely unmodeled (admitted by the paper, left for future research), and the 33.6x first-order power conclusion has a key premise—the SNR of each filter remains constant. In real analog circuits, the bias current saved by shrinking parameters is likely to directly lower SNR and increase mismatch sensitivity (especially for high-Q small-current circuits), so the actual power savings will almost certainly be less than 33.6x, and accuracy degradation may be greater than 1.8%. In other words, this paper provides an upper bound on benefits.

Conclusions are bound to the backend network. All results were obtained on a small residual network published 4 years ago (referred to as res16 in Section 3.3 and res15 in Section 5 of the paper, inconsistent naming, and the paper does not report the parameter count and computational complexity of this implementation). The paper admits: changing to a stronger modern network may change the numerical conclusions, but the "essential conclusion (large power saving for small accuracy drop) is likely to remain unchanged." This defense is reasonable but unverified—a stronger network may squeeze more accuracy from tiny features (narrowing the gap) or may be more sensitive to feature degradation (widening the gap).

Narrow task scope. Conclusions only cover the configuration of 10 words plus unknown; the paper itself lists "what about 30 keywords," "VAD, wake-word detection, speaker recognition" as open questions in the conclusion. It is particularly doubtful whether the 2kHz frequency upper limit is still sufficient after expanding the vocabulary (more words mean finer phoneme contrasts).

Pure simulation, no tape-out. Absolute power numbers (microwatt or milliwatt level) are not reported by the paper, only relative multiples; the degree of realization of 33.6x on silicon, including second-order effects such as area, dynamic range, and clock feedthrough, is all unknown.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

The small training set (2,800 samples) causes the absolute accuracy of the sweep curves to be underestimated by 3.3 percentage points (90.9% vs. 94.2%), and the shape of the low-position segment of the curve may also be affected by under-training—the low-position segment is exactly where the "accuracy collapse boundary" is located, meaning the most critical inflection point region was measured under the least favorable training conditions. Fortunately, the plateau conclusion near the typical value is backed up by Fig. 6 of the 28,000 sample set.

Single-factor sweep lacks interaction term verification. The joint effect of the three parameters was only tested at one point (the tiny combination); if interactions exist (e.g., low Q and few channels mutually amplifying loss), the boundaries given by OFAT curves are overly optimistic. The paper does not report any two-parameter joint sweeps.

Lack of robustness conditions. GSCD is relatively clean near-field data; the experiment did not add noise, reverberation, far-field simulation, or other augmentations; noise robustness is not reported by the paper—while the real working environment of always-on devices is exactly far-field plus noise, this constitutes another distance between simulation conclusions and deployment (different from unmodeled circuit noise, this is environmental noise).

Single metric. Only accuracy is reported; metrics most sensitive to KWS deployment, such as false wake rate and miss wake rate (or DET-like metrics), energy per inference, and latency, are not reported by the paper; it is impossible to judge how much the 1.8 percentage point accuracy loss is amplified in the false wake rate.

Incomplete large-set configuration information. The scale and composition of the test set corresponding to the 28,000 sample training set are not explicitly reported; the parameter count of the backend network implementation is not reported; the specific variance values of each trial are not given in table form (only confidence interval boundaries are drawn in the figures).

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The next step named by the paper is to expand the sweep object from architectural parameters to non-idealities: using the same methodology to sweep the impact of SNR and linearity on KWS accuracy (Section 6), which will correct the "benefit upper bound" into an "engineering achievable value," and is also the key experiment to judge how much of the 33.6x can be realized.

Task generalization replication: repeating the same sweep on more keywords (e.g., 30 words), speech activity detection, wake-word detection, and speaker recognition (self-listed in the paper's conclusion section), to test the boundaries of the three over-design criteria, especially whether the 2kHz upper limit needs to be relaxed under a larger vocabulary.

Frontend-backend joint optimization: the paper admits to only optimizing the frontend while fixing the backend; a natural extension is to jointly search the backend network (or even learnable filterbanks, such as PEAF [3] and filterbank learning [9]) with filterbank parameters—learning the "shape" and compressing the "size" are two orthogonal halves, and their superposition may yield a frontend superior to tiny.

Silicon verification: tape out a test chip according to the tiny parameters, measure actual power savings and mismatch degradation, and simultaneously add deployment metrics such as false wake rate and energy per inference; this is the last kilometer to turn this methodology paper into a design manual.

Strengthening on the training data side is also worth mentioning: rerun the low-position segment sweep (especially the rapid decay area where Q < 4) with a larger training set to exclude the interference of under-training on the position of the collapse boundary, making the numbers of the three criteria more robust.
