# Small-footprint slimmable networks for keyword spotting

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Zuhaib Akhtar, Mohammad Omar Khursheed, Dongsu Du, Yuzong Liu (Amazon Alexa Perceptual Technologies; New York University; first author completed this work during an internship at Amazon)
- **Date**: April 2023 (arXiv:2304.12183v1, submitted April 21, 2023)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12183
- **Keywords**: slimmable neural networks, keyword spotting, weight sharing, switchable batch normalization, slimmable self-attention, on-device deployment, accuracy-efficiency tradeoff

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword spotting (wakeword detection) is the task of determining whether a specific keyword appears in a continuous audio stream. In its simplest form, it can be viewed as a binary classification problem: the audio contains the keyword, or it does not. It serves as the primary entry point for user interaction with voice assistants, powering ubiquitous services like Amazon Alexa and Apple Siri across diverse hardware forms such as wireless earbuds, smartphones, and smart speakers. KWS models must reside on-device for continuous listening without relying on cloud round-trips, thus squeezed by three constraints: model size (memory and Flash usage), CPU compute (multiply-accumulate operations and inference latency), and accuracy. False alarms and missed detections each carry real operational costs—false alarms consume user trust and device battery, while missed detections directly lead users to feel the device is "unresponsive."

The key pain point is that resource budgets are not a single set of numbers but a family of numbers. Earbud-level chips have significantly lower memory and compute budgets than smart speakers and TVs. The same company must maintain a family of models with different "accuracy-resource" tiers for the same wakeword feature. The traditional approach is to design and train a separate model from scratch for each resource tier. The paper's introduction explicitly states that this route is expensive in terms of computational cost and research time: the manual effort for design, the number of tuning rounds, and the training compute all scale linearly with the number of tiers. Furthermore, each tier's model undergoes evaluation, release, and long-term maintenance, multiplying the engineering burden by the number of tiers. The motivating scenario presented at the beginning of the paper is: rather than repeatedly training a pile of independent models, it is better to train a super-network that can execute under different budgets, extracting sub-networks on-demand according to device tiers.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper reviews existing work on the technical lineage of "deriving multiple sub-networks from a single super-network" and points out their misalignment with on-device KWS scenarios:

- **Original Slimmable Networks (Yu et al., ICLR 2019)**: Enable a single network to execute at different widths through weight sharing and switchable BatchNorm, serving as the direct source for this paper's method. However, it was only validated on medium-sized networks with 3-25M parameters. On-device KWS budgets are one to two orders of magnitude lower than this (this paper limits to within 250k parameters, with actual experiments covering 10k-250k). Under such tight parameter budgets, whether a single set of shared weights can serve multiple widths, and whether multi-width joint training can converge stably in the tiny model domain, remains unanswered by the original work.
- **Dynamic Sparsity Methods**: DSNN (ICASSP 2021) trains an over-parameterized super-network and samples sub-networks using pruning masks defined by a fixed set of sparsity rates, used for speech recognition; Omni-Sparsity DNN (ICASSP 2022) applies pruning masks at different layers during super-network training to sample sub-networks with different sparsity rates, and after training, uses an evolutionary search algorithm to find the optimal sub-network for a given sparsity requirement. These methods have heavy workflows and an additional search overhead, and they target streaming end-to-end ASR rather than wakewords.
- **Once-for-All (OFA) Networks**: Train a super-network once, then derive sub-networks according to resource constraints, simultaneously alleviating the huge computational overhead of traditional NAS. LightHuBERT applies OFA to speech pre-training. However, no one has validated this route on wakeword tasks with less than 250k parameters and latency sensitivity.
- **NAS Methods** (including NAS-Bench-ASR): Have high training costs themselves, directly conflicting with the goal of "quickly delivering models for a family of on-device budgets."
- **Per-tier Independent Training**: The baseline approach of training independently multiplies the training cost by the number of tiers and yields no benefits from cross-tier knowledge sharing.

There is also a background gap not elaborated in the paper but known to readers: prior to this paper, no work has simultaneously examined "multi-architecture (CNN and Transformer) + large-scale private wakeword data + tight parameter budget." The paper claims in its contributions to be the first to apply slimmable networks to multiple architectures including CNN and Transformer under a 10k-250k parameter constraint and on large-scale KWS datasets.

### Key Challenges to Solve

The core problem can be summarized as: Can we obtain a family of nested CNN and Transformer wakeword models that can switch widths instantly at different budgets, using a single training process, within an on-device budget of under 250k parameters, while satisfying three conditions—first, the accuracy of sub-networks at each width must match or even exceed models trained from scratch at the same size; second, the total overhead of training this family of models must be far lower than training them independently one by one; third, the method must hold true on both 20,000-hour-level private wakeword data and the public Google Speech Commands dataset. The sharpest suspense is: sub-networks with parameters as few as 10k have their shared parameters continuously "occupied" by gradients from larger widths; will they fail to learn effectively? This directly determines whether the slimmable paradigm can be migrated from million-parameter vision models to tiny-parameter on-device speech models, which is the fundamental question the entire paper seeks to answer.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The system pipeline follows the standard form of on-device KWS: audio extracts LFBE (Log Filter Bank Energy) features, which are fed into a backbone network for classification, either for wakeword detection or 35-class keyword classification. There are two backbone networks (Table 1):

- **CNN Backbone**: 5 convolutional layers. Layer 1 kernel (5,4), 32 channels, stride (1,2), pooling (2,1); Layer 2 kernel (3,4), 32 channels, stride (1,3), pooling (2,1); Layer 3 kernel (4,4), 40 channels, stride (1,2), pooling (2,1); Layer 4 kernel (7,4), 128 channels, stride and pooling both (1,1); Layer 5 is a 1×1 convolution with 160 channels. The text states this backbone has 199k parameters on both in-house and GSC datasets, but Table 3 measures the GSC version at 243k (the difference likely comes from the 35-class output layer). This is an inconsistency between the text and table in the paper; for deep reading, prioritize the table data.
- **Transformer Backbone**: A miniaturized version of KWT (Keyword Transformer, Interspeech 2021). The in-house configuration is dim=64, mlp-dim=128, 1 attention head, 3 layers, totaling 120k parameters; the GSC configuration is dim=64, mlp-dim=64, 1 head, 2 layers, totaling 67k parameters (Table 1). Choosing KWT and reducing dimensions is to fit the parameters into on-device budgets.

From a design motivation perspective, the contribution of this paper is not inventing new backbones, but completely migrating and adapting the slimmable training protocol to the above two backbones: the same super-network produces four tiers of nested sub-networks with widths 1.0/0.75/0.5/0.25, corresponding to 199k/122k/50k/13k parameters on the in-house CNN respectively (Table 2). Choosing width rather than depth as the pruning dimension has clear engineering reasons: width pruning keeps the number of layers and receptive field unchanged, so the temporal modeling capabilities of each sub-network are homologous, facilitating horizontal comparison of the relationship between capacity and accuracy; depth pruning would simultaneously change the receptive field and gradient path, introducing multiple variables. The paper leaves depth pruning for future work.

The input settings are also noteworthy: on in-house data, CNN uses 76 frames of input, Transformer uses 182 frames; on GSC, both backbones use 98 frames. Transformers require significantly longer context (182 frames) to leverage attention modeling capabilities, which echoes their profile of "few parameters but high compute": the in-house Transformer has only 120k parameters, yet 18M MACs, while the parameter-rich 199k CNN has only 3.5M MACs (Table 2, Table 5)—the quadratic cost of self-attention with respect to sequence length is significantly amplified at 182 frames. This reminds readers: in on-device deployment, parameter count and compute are two independent budget axes. Transformers have an advantage on the parameter axis but suffer on the compute axis.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Slimmable Training Protocol (Algorithm 1)**. Given a list of widths (default [1.0, 0.75, 0.50, 0.25]), each training step executes the following loop: for each width in the list, prune the network to that width and treat it as a normal network for one forward pass, obtain predictions, and calculate loss and gradients, but at this stage **do not update weights**, only store the gradients in a gradient list; the same batch of data is then fed into the smaller-width model (sharing parameters with the previous larger model), repeating until gradients for all sub-networks are calculated; finally, sum all gradients and update the entire network's weights in one go. Mathematically, this is equivalent to jointly optimizing the sum of losses for all width sub-networks. Because sub-networks are nested (the sub-network of width w is the first w proportion of channels of the complete network), large-width gradients push shared parameters towards "high-capacity representation," while small-width gradients push shared parameters towards "low-dimensional separability." The two博弈 (game/compete) on the same set of parameters to reach a compromise—this is the structural basis for the paper's later attribution of GSC accuracy gains to the "inherent distillation brought by shared weights": small-width sub-networks are equivalent to being continuously distilled by large-width sub-networks within the network.

**Mathematical Meaning of Width Pruning**. The weight tensor shape of a convolutional layer is (C_out, C_in, kH, kW). Width w does two things: output channels are truncated to w·C_out (entire kernels discarded), and input channels are truncated to w·C_in (each kernel shrinks in the input channel dimension). The same applies to dense layers in Transformers, where intermediate representations are reduced to w·dim according to width. Parameter count and MACs scale approximately by w², which explains the parameter ladder of 199k→122k→50k→13k in Table 2 (ratios approx 1/0.61/0.25/0.065, close to a mix of 1/w² and channel rounding) and the MAC ladder of 3.5M→2.1M→1.1M→0.35M.

**Slimmable Self-Attention Module (Eq. 1-3)**:

SlimAttn(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q[:i]·K[:i]^T / √(d_k[:i]) ) · V[:i]  (Eq. 1)

i ∈ [ d_k·width[0], d_k·width[1], …, d_k·width[n] ]  (Eq. 2)

width ∈ [ 1, (n−1)/n, (n−2)/n, …, 1/n ], n is an integer greater than 1  (Eq. 3)

Three details are worth digging into. First, Q, K, and V must be sliced synchronously to the first i dimensions; otherwise, the dot product QK^T dimensions will not match. Slicing acts on the Q/K/V weight matrices themselves, and dimensionality reduction occurs at the projection stage, not after projection. Second, the denominator √(d_k[:i]) dynamically adjusting with width is not just decoration—the variance of dot product logits grows linearly with dimension. If the head dimension is cut from d_k to w·d_k but still divided by a fixed √(d_k), the softmax temperature will systematically shift, causing the attention distribution to be too sharp or too flat; Eq. 1 explicitly performs temperature compensation. Third, taking the in-house configuration (dim=64, single head) as an example, the head dimensions for the four width tiers are 64/48/32/16 respectively. At the smallest dimension, attention effectively degenerates into an extremely narrow query space, which is one of the structural reasons for the greater accuracy fluctuation of Transformers at small width tiers.

**Switchable Normalization Layers**. On the CNN side, the original slimmable network's switchable BatchNorm is used: each width is equipped with an independent set of BatchNorm parameters and running statistics. On the Transformer side, there is no BatchNorm, so the paper lets **LayerNorm act as the width switch**, with an independent set of LayerNorm for each width, selected at runtime according to the current width, implemented isomorphically to switchable BatchNorm. Why must it be independent per width: the statistical distribution (mean, variance) of activations differs under different widths. If a single set of normalization statistics is shared, the distributions during training and inference will not align, especially since different widths use their own sliding statistics during inference. The cost is that the super-network must store an entire set of normalization parameters for each width tier; the more width tiers, the higher this marginal cost.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Layer-Differentiated End-to-End Pruning Rules (Fig. 1)

The pruning rules for three positions in the convolutional network are different, which is key to ensuring sub-networks are ready-to-use. **The first layer only discards kernels, not kernel width**: the input is fixed-dimension LFBE features, and all sub-networks share the same input interface, so only the number of output channels is pruned according to width. **Middle layers both discard kernels and shrink the input channels of each kernel**: because the output channels of the previous layer have been partially pruned, the input channel dimension of each convolutional kernel in this layer must shrink synchronously, otherwise the tensors will not connect. **The last layer only shrinks kernel width, does not discard kernels**: the number of output channels must equal the number of classes (binary classification for in-house or 35 classes for GSC) and cannot be pruned according to width. This rule ensures that the input and output interfaces of the network remain completely unchanged at any width—this is the prerequisite for "one function, multiple budget tiers" to be implemented; otherwise, changing the width tier would require changing feature dimensions or post-processing logic. Fig. 1 provides a diagram of this end-to-end pruning illustration.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Residual and Classification Interface Adaptation for Transformers (Fig. 2)

Three interface adaptations were made on the Transformer side to ensure structural completeness and independent forward propagation at any width. First, there is a dense layer at the beginning of each block that projects the input to a low dimension determined by the current width, ensuring that the dimensions on both sides of the residual connection addition are consistent at each width—residual structures have a hard constraint on dimension matching, which is the most easily overlooked but necessary step. Second, all dense layers within the block are pruned according to width, and intermediate representations are uniformly reduced to w·dim. Third, **the last dense layer of the Transformer always outputs a fixed dimension (number of classes)**, unaffected by input dimensions, echoing the CNN last layer's rule of "only shrink, do not discard." Coupled with per-width LayerNorm switches, this yields an end-to-end slimmable Transformer. The paper claims one of the main extensions to the original slimmable network is this "detailed design of the slimmable self-attention module": the original work only handled CNNs and BatchNorm; Q/K/V slicing for self-attention, temperature compensation, residual alignment, and LayerNorm switches are all adaptation work by this paper on the new architecture.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Sub-linear Overhead of Multi-Width Training (Table 4)

Table 4 shows the change in single-step training time with the number of widths: 1/2/3/4/5/10/20/40 widths correspond to 1.01/1.53/1.60/1.63/1.69/2.01/2.29/3.72 seconds respectively. As the number of widths increases from 1 to 40, the single-step time only increases by 3.72 times, which is clearly sub-linear. Why: on one hand, the compute of narrow-width sub-networks decays rapidly by w², so adding a forward/backward pass for a narrow width is much cheaper than one for full width; the compute of the width list is dominated by the largest tiers. On the other hand, the fixed overhead in a single step (data supply, optimizer update, scheduling) occurs only once and is amortized across all widths. The paper does not further decompose the sub-linear mechanism (e.g., distinguishing the time proportion of data loading vs. computation), which is a blank space in this table. From this, the paper calculates: training a super-network containing sub-networks of various widths saves more than 10 times the time compared to independent training per width—40 models require only 3.72 times the cost of a single model, 40/3.72 is approximately 10.8. This number is the most engineering-persuasive conclusion in the paper, directly supporting the deployment economics of "train once, deliver the whole family."

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **With Original Slimmable Networks**: Parameter budget compressed from 3-25M to 10k-250k, one to two orders of magnitude smaller; task domain changed from vision classification to streaming wakeword detection; and the complete pruning design for the self-attention module was added. In terms of conclusions, it validates feasibility in the tiny domain, which is an area not covered by the original work.
- **With DSNN/Omni-Sparsity**: The latter uses pruning masks plus sparsity rates to sample sub-networks, and requires evolutionary search after training to select the optimal sub-network; slimmable sub-networks are explicitly nested channel prefixes, selecting a width yields the sub-network directly, with no independent search phase, resulting in a shorter workflow.
- **With OFA**: Both train a super-network once, but OFA emphasizes progressive shrinking training and decoupling of training and search, with a heavier protocol; this paper uses the simplest protocol of a fixed width list, with the goal not to search for architectures but to quickly deliver a family of on-device models.
- **With Per-tier Independent Training**: One training replaces N trainings, Table 4 supports 10x-level savings; and small-width sub-networks surpass independent training in some settings, a benefit that independent training cannot provide.
- **With Quantization/Pruning Compression Methods** (e.g., Binary Neural Networks): Orthogonal dimensions—slimmable prunes channel counts, quantization compresses numerical precision; the two can theoretically be superimposed to form a 2D budget grid of "width tiers × bit tiers," which the paper does not experiment with.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Experiments cover two tasks. The first is detecting a single wakeword on in-house voice assistant data; the second is the 35-class classification task on Google Speech Commands (TensorFlow implementation). The in-house training set has over 20,000 hours of de-identified data, with a 100-hour evaluation set; GSC uses the complete training set and official test set, and the paper does not report the hours or number of utterances for GSC. Features are LFBE, calculated every 10ms with a window length of 25ms. The position of the wakeword in training samples is aligned using a DNN-HMM aligner (spotter model), placing the keyword in the center of the context window fed into the model—unifying the target word position reduces the positional variance the model needs to fit. Input frame settings: in-house CNN uses 76 frames, Transformer uses 182 frames; on GSC, both backbones use 98 frames. Training hyperparameters such as optimizer, learning rate, data augmentation, and batch size are not reported in the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The in-house task reports **relative False Accepts at a fixed miss rate**: using the full-width model trained from scratch as the baseline (denoted as 1), other models report relative multiples, with lower values being better. The GSC task reports absolute accuracy. Both tasks additionally report parameter count and multiply-accumulate operations (multiplies). The rationale for metric selection can be traced back to production logic: the core metric for online operation of voice assistants is "how low can false alarms be pressed under an acceptable miss rate." False alarms directly consume user trust and device battery, so fixing the miss rate and looking at relative changes in false alarms is closer to the real deployment working point than fixing the decision threshold; absolute false alarm values on private data are not comparable externally, so relative multiples facilitate cross-scenario transfer of conclusions. Parameter count and MACs proxy for memory and compute on-device budgets respectively. It should be pointed out that the paper does not report the specific value of the fixed miss rate, nor does it report any real-device latency metrics (such as single inference milliseconds or real-time rate). For a paper主打 (focusing on) on-device use cases, this is a significant gap.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**in-house CNN (Table 2, relative False Accepts, lower is better)**: Training from scratch for four widths has relative FA of 1 (199k parameters)/1.11 (122k)/1.28 (50k)/1.78 (13k), MACs 3.5M/2.1M/1.1M/0.35M; slimmable four tiers are 1.05/1.20/1.35/1.72. Two layers of information can be read. First, slimmable is 5% worse than independent training at the full-width tier (1.05 vs 1), and about 8% and 5% worse at the 0.75 and 0.5 tiers respectively—on 20,000 hours of big data, the constraint of one set of weights serving four widths requires paying an accuracy tax. Second, **the 0.25 tier (13k parameters) shows a reversal**: slimmable's 1.72 is better than independent training's 1.78; the smallest sub-network actually benefits from multi-width joint training. The overall trade-off is: parameters compressed to 1/15 (199k to 13k), independent training's false alarm rate increases by 78% (1 to 1.78).

**in-house Transformer (Table 5)**: Training from scratch for four tiers is 1/1.08/1.15/1.36 (120k/76k/43k/24k parameters, 18M/10.1M/4.5M/1.1M MACs); slimmable four tiers are 1.19/1.19/1.27/1.34. The pattern is consistent with CNN but the full-width tax is heavier: slimmable's full-width tier is 19% worse (1.19 vs 1), and the 0.25 tier shows a slight reversal again (1.34 vs 1.36). Two points are worth noting in deep reading: the slimmable full-width tax for Transformer (19%) is much higher than for CNN (5%), suggesting that the attention module is more sensitive to multi-width sharing—one set of Q/K/V weights must work simultaneously at four head dimensions, with tighter constraints; and slimmable has identical values for the 1.0 and 0.75 tiers (both 1.19), with width benefits flattening at higher tiers. The paper does not explain this architectural difference.

**GSC CNN (Table 3, absolute accuracy, higher is better)**: Training from scratch for four tiers is 0.8915/0.8878/0.8636/0.8455 (243k/138k/62k/16k parameters, 4.8M/2.9M/1.4M/0.48M MACs); slimmable four tiers are 0.9047/0.8967/0.8726/0.8446. Except for the smallest tier basically tying (0.8446 vs 0.8455, a difference of 0.09 percentage points), **slimmable surpasses independent training in all tiers**, with gains of +1.32/+0.89/+0.90 percentage points. The table also includes a Res26 reference point: accuracy 0.95, 438k parameters, MACs not reported (N/A)—this paper's 243k parameter CNN is about 4.5 percentage points behind it, but has nearly half the parameters, and Res26 does not have multi-tier switching capability; they are not the same type of product.

**GSC Transformer (Table 6)**: Training from scratch for four tiers is 0.8623/0.8463/0.8467/0.7295 (67k/44k/26k/15k parameters, 7.2M/4.5M/2.4M/0.9M MACs); slimmable four tiers are 0.8787/0.8766/0.8588/0.7840, **winning in all four tiers**, with gains of +1.64/+3.03/+1.21/+5.45 percentage points, with the smallest tier (15k parameters) having the largest gain. Training from scratch also shows non-monotonic phenomena (the 0.5 tier 0.8467 is slightly higher than the 0.75 tier 0.8463), while slimmable's accuracy-width curve is strictly monotonic—joint training inadvertently smooths out the variance fluctuations of small-data independent training. The paper explicitly attributes the advantage on GSC to the inherent distillation brought by shared weights.

The conclusions for the two datasets are in opposite directions (on in-house, slimmable is slightly worse in most tiers; on GSC, it wins almost everywhere). Why: a reasonable inference is that GSC data scale is limited, and independent training of small models is prone to underfitting or large variance, while cross-width distillation provides tangible regularization and supervision gains; whereas 20,000 hours of in-house data is sufficient for each independent model to train fully, and the "one set of weights serving four widths" constraint of joint training becomes a net loss, with only the smallest tier still benefiting from distillation. This is a deep-reading inference; the paper itself does not expand on this comparative analysis.

**Training Efficiency (Table 4 and text)**: The paper reports that slimmable models require more epochs to converge (20 epochs for training from scratch vs 30 for slimmable, noted as "20 vs 30" in the original text); even so, the total training time for producing four tiers of models is still significantly saved, with the original text stating "the training time is reduced by a factor of 4 per step"—understood as "merging four independent trainings into one," this is approximately a 4x saving; if calculated based on Table 4 measurements (4-tier single step 1.63 seconds vs single-tier 1.01 seconds, equivalent to four independent single steps of 4.04 seconds), the actual saving is about 2.5x, and the paper's claim of 4x should be understood as a rough account of tier merging. Combined with the sub-linear extrapolation of Table 4, the saving in the 40-tier scenario exceeds 10x. The conclusion section also points out the methodological utility: training a multi-width super-network and evaluating it on each sub-width can serve as a capacity probe to determine the minimum model capacity required for a dataset to "perform well," replacing repeated trial-and-error in capacity selection; since inference is much cheaper than training, this probe-style usage can be deployed at low cost.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have classic component-level ablations (no comparison between switchable BatchNorm and shared BatchNorm, no comparison with/without temperature compensation). The role of ablation is taken by the matrix of "width scan × dual architecture × dual dataset," from which four findings can be distilled:

1. **Width-Accuracy Trade-off is Monotonic and Steep**: On in-house, the 0.25 tier relative FA rises to the 1.72-1.78 range (Table 2, Table 5), indicating that the 13k-24k parameter tier is approaching the capacity lower bound for this task; on GSC, the 0.25 tier accuracy drops 4 to 13 percentage points compared to full width (Table 3, Table 6).
2. **Small-Width Reversal Phenomenon Occurs Systematically**: In three of the four comparisons, the 0.25 tier slimmable is better than independent training (in-house CNN 1.72 vs 1.78; in-house Transformer 1.34 vs 1.36; GSC Transformer 0.7840 vs 0.7295), and the fourth group (GSC CNN 0.8446 vs 0.8455) basically ties. Joint training provides stable assistance to small-capacity sub-networks, and the smaller the dataset and the weaker the architecture (GSC Transformer), the greater the assistance.
3. **Architectural Sensitivity Differences**: The slimmable full-width tax for CNN is about 5%, for Transformer about 19% (in-house, Table 2 vs Table 5), indicating that self-attention is significantly more sensitive to width sharing than convolution.
4. **Number of Widths Does Not Change the Magnitude of Single-Step Cost** (Table 4), increasing only 3.72 times from 1 tier to 40 tiers, supporting the deployment economics of "train once, choose any tier," and also implying that in actual deployment, more tiers can be trained than ultimately needed, leaving the choice to the deployment phase.

## Main Contributions

1. **First Proof of Paradigm Migration**: Completely moves slimmable networks into small-footprint KWS, validating feasibility for dual architectures (CNN and Transformer) on 20,000-hour-level private data and public datasets within 10k-250k parameters (one to two orders of magnitude smaller than the original work's 3-25M), answering the fundamental question of "whether slimmable can be used in the tiny domain."
2. **Slimmable Self-Attention Module**: Provides a complete design for synchronous slicing of Q/K/V, softmax temperature denominator varying with width, block-head projection to align residuals, fixed output dimension for the last dense layer, and per-width LayerNorm switches (Eq. 1-3, Fig. 2), filling in the Transformer side not covered by the original slimmable network.
3. **Quantified Conclusion on Training Overhead**: Uses Table 4 to prove that multi-width training overhead is sub-linear (40 tiers only 3.72x), deriving the engineering account that super-network training saves more than 10 times the time compared to independent training per tier, directly supporting the deployment model of sharing one super-network across multiple product lines (phones, earbuds, TVs).
4. **Directional Characterization of Distillation Effects**: Almost all tiers benefit on small datasets (GSC), only the smallest tier benefits on large datasets (in-house), small-width tier reversal, Transformer full-width tax higher than CNN—this set of empirical rules provides decision-making basis for "when it is worth using slimmable."
5. **Capacity Probe Methodology**: Proposes using multi-width super-networks as a tool to determine the minimum viable capacity of a dataset, replacing repeated trial-and-error in capacity selection with a single training.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Only Width Pruned, Not Depth**: Depth, input resolution, and number of attention heads (all Transformers in this paper are single-head, and pruning by head is completely unexplored) are outside the pruning space. Width nesting may not be the optimal search direction for on-device budgets. The paper itself lists depth pruning as future work in the conclusion.
- **Full-Width Tax is Real**: On in-house, slimmable full-width sub-networks are 5% (CNN) to 19% (Transformer) worse than independent training (Table 2, Table 5). For products that require "both the top-tier to be optimal and multi-tier switching," this top-tier accuracy loss must be weighed against the savings in maintenance costs; the paper does not provide this decision framework.
- **Width Tiers are Discrete and Subject to Divisibility Constraints**: Width can only take values stepped by (n−1)/n (Eq. 3). Arbitrary widths (e.g., 0.6) are not in the trained width list, their performance is unverified, and interpolation or continuous width capability is missing.
- **Normalization Layer Overhead Grows Linearly with Number of Tiers**: Each width tier has a set of BatchNorm/LayerNorm statistics; 40 width tiers mean 40 sets of normalization parameters. The paper does not calculate this portion of on-device memory usage.
- **Lack of Real-Device Validation**: The paper only reports parameter count and MACs as proxy metrics, with no real-device latency, peak memory, or power data. The claim of "on-device use case" remains at the proxy metric level. The paper also acknowledges in the conclusion that profiling is needed on various edge computing chips.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **No Component-Level Ablation**: The necessity of switchable BatchNorm/LayerNorm and the actual role of Eq. 1 temperature compensation (√(d_k[:i])) have no control experiments; readers can only rely on priors from the original slimmable literature.
- **Missing Training Details**: Optimizer, learning rate, batch size, data augmentation, fixed miss rate values, random seeds, and variance are all not reported in the paper. In-house results cannot be reproduced or verified, and GSC results are difficult to fairly align with subsequent work.
- **Narrow Comparison Scope**: On GSC, only one reference point, Res26 (0.95 accuracy, 438k parameters, Table 3), is placed. There is no comparison with stronger contemporary KWS architectures (such as MatchboxNet, BC-ResNet) or same-paradigm methods like OFA/DSNN under the same budget; on in-house, there are no external references at all, and the baseline for relative FA is the paper's own training-from-scratch model.
- **Training Efficiency Conclusion Relies on Extrapolation**: Table 4 only measures single-step time, without a joint comparison of end-to-end total training wall-clock time and final accuracy. The statements of "saving 10x" and "saving 4x per step" are not supported by complete training curves.
- **Text and Table Data Conflict**: The text states that the CNN backbone has 199k parameters on both datasets, but Table 3 shows the GSC version as 243k. The paper does not explain the source of the difference.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper lists four directions: replacing manual width lists with automated pruning techniques like AutoSlim; extending slimmable to more architectures such as RNNs; researching slimmable designs in the depth direction; and profiling on various edge chips to obtain real memory and compute profiles. Deep-reading supplementary feasible directions: (1) Superimpose with quantization to construct a 2D budget grid of "width tiers × bit tiers," covering a finer device spectrum; (2) Arbitrary width interpolation or continuous width training to eliminate discrete tier limitations; (3) Runtime dynamic tier switching—switching widths within the same deployment based on battery and CPU load status, upgrading slimmable from "one-time selection at deployment" to "adaptive at runtime," which is also the natural intersection of dynamic networks and slimmable routes; (4) Add explicit distillation weighting to small-width sub-networks to suppress the large-width accuracy tax in in-house scenarios; (5) Joint design of head-by-head pruning and head-dimension pruning for multi-head attention, filling in the other half of the Transformer pruning space.
