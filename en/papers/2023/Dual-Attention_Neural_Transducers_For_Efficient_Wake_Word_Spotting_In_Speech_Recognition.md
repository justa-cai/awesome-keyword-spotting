# Dual-Attention Neural Transducers for Efficient Wake Word Spotting in Speech Recognition

- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01905
- **Keywords**: wake word spotting, neural transducer, neural biasing, dual attention, dynamic catalog masking, on-device ASR, inference optimization

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

In the voice entry chain of commercial virtual voice assistants (such as Alexa), streaming end-to-end (E2E) ASR systems—CTC, LAS, RNN-T, transformer transducer, ConvRNN-T, conformer—have completely replaced traditional hybrid HMM-DNN architectures. Hybrid systems require the independent optimization of three components: the acoustic model (AM), the pronunciation model (PM), and the language model (LM). In contrast, E2E systems directly output word sequences from input audio sequences, jointly modeling these three components. Due to structural simplification and unified compression capabilities, E2E systems have become the mainstream choice for edge-side deployment, especially in low-resource scenarios.

However, the E2E architecture has a structural vulnerability: it performs poorly in recognizing words that are uncommon in the paired audio-text training data. These uncommon words are precisely the core of personalization for voice assistants—such as user-defined wake words (the paper cites examples like "Ziggy," "Hey Shaq"), contact names, proper nouns, and rare named entities. The reason is that all linguistic knowledge in E2E systems is "baked" into the network weights. After training, there is no independent vocabulary or external language model to inject new vocabulary at runtime. Meanwhile, wake words and contact lists are naturally private to each user and cannot be pre-included in the training set.

The industry's standard solution is attention-based neural biasing: a bias adapter is added on top of the audio encoder to calculate the similarity between the audio representation and catalog embeddings frame by frame. Attention weights are used to pull rare entities from the catalog into the decoding preference. While this method is effective in improving the recall of proper nouns, the cost is that attention must act on the audio encoding frame by frame. Dot-product attention has quadratic complexity, and the catalog scale expands to tens of thousands with the number of contacts. On embedded devices with limited memory bandwidth and CPU, this runtime latency becomes the biggest obstacle to the scalable deployment of neural biasing. The pain point can be summarized as: **the accuracy gain brought by biasing is bound to the per-frame computational overhead of attention, and the vast majority of entries in the catalog are irrelevant to the vast majority of frames.**

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper divides on-device ASR computational optimization into two categories: architecture modification (e.g., CIFG merging input and forget gates to save 25% parameters, SRU, low-rank decomposition, bifocal, dynamic encoder, amortized networks, linformer, performer, temporal downsampling layers) and weight interpretation (quantization, sparsity). This paper belongs to the first category. For the two technical lines directly related to this paper, the paper provides specific deficiency analyses:

- **Capacity Shortcoming of Bifocal RNN-T** (Section 2.1, Figure 1(a)): The bifocal neural transducer uses two audio encoders—a small, fast encoder to process the buffered lead-in segment (audio frames before and within the wake word) and a large, slow encoder to process the remaining audio. It dynamically switches between the two using wake word (WW) spotting. While it improves latency by routing wake word frames to the fast branch, **it does not design any mechanism for recognizing new or rare words**: the audio of user-defined wake words happens to be routed to the low-capacity small audio encoder, causing queries initiated by custom wake words to be incorrectly rejected or misrouted to another virtual assistant persona. This is a hard flaw in user experience—when a user changes their wake word, the assistant "pretends not to hear it."
- **Overhead and Defocusing of Single-Attention Neural Biasing** (Section 2.2, Figure 1(b)): Existing work uses a single MHA layer to process the entire sentence, **statically concatenating** wake word embeddings and proper noun embeddings without distinguishing sentence segments. This leads to three types of waste: First, tens of thousands of proper nouns in the catalog are indiscriminately scored by attention in every frame of the lead-in segment, even though contact names are impossible to appear in the lead-in segment. Second, the wake word catalog usually has only an order of magnitude of 10 entries; mixed with hundreds to tens of thousands of irrelevant candidates, the attention distribution is diluted, and focusing becomes worse. Third, the quadratic complexity of dot-product attention causes the scoring overhead for the entire catalog to deteriorate linearly with the catalog scale.
- **General Efficiency Methods Do Not Utilize Task Structure**: Methods like linformer and performer perform general approximations on attention itself, while CIFG, SRU, and low-rank decomposition perform general simplifications on recurrent structures. Quantization and sparsity perform general compression on weight representations. None of them utilize a strong prior in the voice assistant interaction protocol: **the position of the sentence relative to the wake word determines which catalog entries may appear in which frames.**

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

Simultaneously satisfying four mutually constraining objectives on a streaming on-device neural transducer (with RNN-T and Conformer-Transducer as two backbones): (1) Improve the detection accuracy (TAR and F1) of user-defined wake words; (2) Maintain or even exceed the proper noun recognition gains brought by neural biasing; (3) Reduce the runtime FLOPs of biasing attention by more than an order of magnitude; (4) Keep parameter overhead within approximately 1%, without retraining the pre-trained backbone of about 80 million parameters. The core challenge lies in: **how to reuse the wake word detection signal that is already running in the system, so that it not only switches encoders but also switches biasing computation paths and catalog content, enabling the small attention network to achieve the accuracy of the large attention network in the lead-in segment.**

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall design is a three-layer combination of "bifocal transducer + dual-attention biasing branch + offline context encoder" (Figure 1(c)). The backbone follows the bifocal neural transducer (RNN-T or C-T, Figure 1(a)): the small audio encoder is responsible for the lead-in segment (audio frames before and within the wake word), the large audio encoder is responsible for the remaining audio, the text predictor encodes the sequence of predicted subwords, and the joint network fuses the two outputs to obtain subword distributions via fully connected layers and softmax. On top of this, three new components are added (blue parts in Figure 1(c)): an offline-running context encoder (BiLSTM) that encodes user-defined wake words and proper nouns into embeddings and caches them; two parallel MHA biasing networks—the small attention network $A_s$ is attached after the small audio encoder to serve the lead-in segment, and the large attention network $A_l$ is attached after the large audio encoder to serve the main body of the sentence after the wake word.

The training strategy is "freeze gray blocks, train only blue blocks": first pre-train the bifocal transducer (gray blocks), then only fine-tune the context encoder and the two MHA models (blue blocks), freezing all other pre-trained weights. The practical reason for this design is that the backbone is trained on 114,000 hours of data for general ASR capability, while the fine-tuning data is only hundreds to thousands of hours. Full fine-tuning极易 leads to catastrophic forgetting of general recognition quality. Freezing also minimizes adaptation costs.

The core of the design motivation is a **perplexity asymmetry argument** (Section 3.1): Content spoken before the wake word is naturally low perplexity—mostly silence, filler words, or very short lead-in words, with a small candidate space. Therefore, a small MHA with fewer hidden units and coarse granularity is sufficient. The main body of the sentence after the wake word is an open-ended arbitrary query with high perplexity, requiring a high-capacity large MHA. This is the "bifocal lenses" metaphor used in the paper: like bifocal glasses, different visual areas use lenses with different degrees, each performing its own function.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

Basic equations of the neural transducer (Section 2.1): The audio encoder acts as an acoustic model, producing high-level representations for each input frame $h_t^{enc} = \text{AudioEncoder}(x_t)$; the text predictor acts as a language model, encoding already predicted subwords $y^{u-1}$ to obtain $h_u^{pred} = \text{TextPredictor}(y^{u-1})$; the joint network fuses both, outputting subword probability distributions via fully connected layers and softmax.

The mathematical form of biasing attention is a catalog retrieval variant of standard scaled dot-product attention: the query is the audio encoding output of the current frame, the key and value are the projected representations of the catalog embeddings. The attention weights $\alpha = \text{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d})$ measure the relevance of the current frame to each catalog entry, and the bias vector after weighted value is fused back into the encoding path. The single-frame overhead grows approximately linearly with "number of catalog entries × projection dimension," and the cumulative cost for the entire sequence results in the quadratic complexity of dot-product attention—this is exactly what the paper aims to cut.

Context Encoder: User-defined wake words $ww_1,\dots,ww_k$ and proper nouns $cn_1,\dots,cn_m$ are tokenized via sentence-piece and fed into a BiLSTM. The final state is taken as the embedding $Ce_{ww}$ or $Ce$ (yellow box in Figure 1(c)). The key point is that this step is **completely offline**—catalog embeddings are generated once and cached for reuse. At runtime, only the attention scoring itself remains, so embedding encoding does not occupy the latency budget.

Definition of Evaluation Metrics (Section 5): Relative Word Error Rate reduction $\text{WERR} = (\text{WER}_B - \text{WER}_A)/\text{WER}_B$; TAR is the proportion of true positives correctly accepted, TRR is the proportion of true negatives correctly rejected, and F1 is the harmonic mean of the two; F1R, TARR, TRRR are the relative improvements of the three, respectively. The cost metric measures the **floating-point operations (FLOPs) required by the MHA layer per frame of lead-in audio** (following the definition in [19][21]). This metric is chosen because the lead-in segment is where the bifocal small encoder works and latency is most sensitive. Separating the MHA FLOPs cleanly isolates the biasing overhead from the backbone overhead.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Wake Word Detection-Driven Dynamic Computation Path Switching (Dual-Attention Branches)

Two mutually exclusive MHA networks are trained (Figure 1(c)): $A_s$ is coupled with the small audio encoder, specifically serving the lead-in segment, with the design goal of boosting the ASR accuracy of custom wake words (wake word count is on the order of 10); $A_l$ is paired with the large audio encoder, serving the main body of the sentence, with the goal of improving the recognition of personalized entity names (catalog can scale to tens of thousands). $A_s$ has fewer hidden units than $A_l$, processing frames faster but coarser; $A_l$ has larger capacity and computes more. At runtime, the wake word detection selects which branch to execute frame by frame—the dynamic pivot of the computation path.

Why this saves resources: The lead-in segment is the latency-critical area (the system must respond immediately to wake words). The expensive large attention $A_l$ is never executed on lead-in frames; the cheap small attention $A_s$ never touches the large catalog. The saved computing power is exactly taken from "the audio segment that cannot wait the most," rather than being uniformly cut from the entire sentence.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Dynamic Catalog Masking

Unlike existing work that statically concatenates all catalog embeddings (Section 3.2), the masking in this paper is dynamically determined by the **frame index marking the end of the wake word**: During inference, $A_s$ only applies wake word embeddings $Ce_{ww}$, dynamically masking out proper noun tokens—the biasing candidates in the lead-in segment are narrowed from tens of thousands (proper nouns) to 6 (only custom wake words); correspondingly, $A_l$ discards the wake word catalog and only applies proper noun embeddings $Ce$. The dual purpose of this is: first, narrowing the candidate set makes attention more focused; second, excluding irrelevant catalogs from incorrect sentence positions (e.g., contact names cannot appear in the initial wake word position). The cached $Ce_{ww}$ and $Ce$ are masked at the tensor level during inference according to frame indices, without re-encoding the catalog.

Additionally, a special **no-bias token** is introduced into the catalog (following [14][34]), allowing the dual-attention system to learn "when not to bias." This detail is crucial for TRR: without it, the model is forced to pick the most similar one from the catalog in every frame, naturally tending to accept more, leading to an increase in false wake-up rate.

It is worth emphasizing that masking and dual-attention are two different mechanisms: masking determines "which subset of the catalog each branch looks at," while dual-attention determines "which branch each frame takes and what capacity of attention to use." The former cuts the number of candidates, the latter cuts the projection dimension and network scale. The multiplicative effect of the two results in the final order-of-magnitude reduction.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Asymmetric Capacity and λ Projection Compression (dual-attn-λ)

The large MHA takes the large audio encoder output as query and proper noun embeddings as key/value, projecting to 128 dimensions; the small MHA takes the small audio encoder output as query and wake word embeddings as key/value, projecting to λ dimensions—the model is thus named dual-attn-λ, experimenting with four levels of λ=64, 32, 16, 8. λ is the "computational knob" of this paper: from 64 down to 8, the FLOPs of the lead-in MHA drop from 95K to 12K (Table 1), until the wake word accuracy collapses at λ=8. Both MHA networks use only 1 attention head—the paper explicitly states that trying 2 and 4 heads yielded no accuracy gains. This negative result is informative itself: catalog matching is essentially coarse-grained retrieval, which does not require the multi-view subspace splitting of multi-head attention. Saving multi-head attention is a free computational saving.

Context Encoder Configuration: A BiLSTM with 64 units in each direction, with input and output both projected to 64 dimensions, **trained from scratch** (not initialized from pre-trained weights), to uniformly generate embeddings for wake words and proper nouns.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Compared to Bifocal RNN-T**: Bifocal only solves "running fast in the lead-in segment," not "recognizing new words"; this paper extends the pivot mechanism of bifocal from the audio encoder to the biasing network, reusing the same wake word detection signal to supplement the ability to adapt to rare words. The essence of the difference is upgrading the "latency optimizer" to a "latency optimizer + accuracy amplifier."
- **Compared to Single-Attention Neural Biasing**: [8][14] use one attention network to serve the entire sentence and catalog; this paper splits the sentence into two attention networks with asymmetric capacity based on sentence segments, and further superimposes dynamic catalog masking, reducing the lead-in biasing FLOPs from 3.3M to 95K (Table 1, dual-attn-64) while maintaining or even exceeding accuracy.
- **Compared to General Efficient Attention Methods like Linformer/Performer**: Those methods approximate the attention mechanism itself, with controllable loss but universality; this paper makes no approximations, the attention is exact, and the source of computing savings is **task structure priors** (the wake word splits the interaction into two semantically inequivalent segments, each requiring completely different biasing views).
- **Compared to Quantization/Sparsity**: These belong to the orthogonal direction of weight interpretation, acting on "how cheap each multiplication is"; this paper acts on "how many multiplications need to be done." The two are theoretically superimposable, but the paper does not perform combined experiments.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

- **Pre-training**: 114,000 hours (114K hours) of de-identified internal voice assistant general data, randomly sampled from real traffic across more than 20 domains (music, communication, smart home, etc.), used to pre-train RNN-T and Conformer-Transducer (C-T) baselines. Footnote 1 of the paper explicitly states: to the best of their knowledge, **there is no large-scale public dataset containing diverse user-defined wake words**. This is the reason for using internal data throughout the paper and the fundamental limitation preventing external reproduction.
- **Dual-Attention Network Fine-tuning**: 290 hours of proper noun data containing named entity mentions, mixed with general data at a 1:2.5 ratio, and 3,600 hours (3.6K) of semi-supervised data generated by a teacher model (containing 6 custom wake words).
- **Testing**: 75 hours of manually transcribed general test set, 20 hours of manually transcribed proper noun test set; wake word true acceptance/rejection rates are calculated using 25 hours of manually annotated data containing 6 wake words. The training and test sets are de-identified and non-overlapping.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **WERR**: Relative Word Error Rate reduction, used to normalize the absolute differences between different backbone baselines. A positive value represents improvement.
- **TAR / TRR / F1**: TAR measures the proportion of wake words correctly accepted by the system when spoken (the opposite of missed wake-ups); TRR measures the proportion of correct rejections when no wake word is spoken (the opposite of false wake-ups); F1 takes the harmonic mean of the two. It is necessary to look at F1 rather than TAR alone because the biasing mechanism naturally trades off between the two rates—the paper states directly in Section 5.1: after the model learns to bias towards the expected wake word, TAR naturally rises, but the model tends to accept more queries, so TRR may degrade. The harmonic mean penalizes lopsided operating points, preventing the false prosperity of "high TAR by accepting everything."
- **FLOPs**: Floating-point operations of the MHA layer per frame of lead-in audio (following the definition in [19][21]). The reason for selection is stated earlier: the lead-in segment is the latency-sensitive area. Measuring the MHA layer separately isolates the biasing overhead from the backbone, directly corresponding to deployment bottlenecks.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Three baselines (Section 4.2): `pretrained-base` is the bifocal transducer without biasing layers (Figure 1(a)), fast inference but poor proper noun performance; `single-attn-base-128` is the single-attention biasing of [8][14] (Figure 1(b)), with key/value projected to 128 dimensions, good proper nouns but slow inference; `single-attn-catalog-mask` is the third control, adding only dynamic catalog masking to single attention.

Backbone Configuration (Section 4.2): Input features are 64-dimensional LFBE, extracted every 10ms with a window length of 25ms, resulting in 192-dimensional features per frame; RNN-T uses a 2.5K subword sub-vocabulary, encoder with 5-layer LSTM, lead-in encoder with 256 units per layer and a temporal reduction layer with 2x downsampling in the 3rd layer, large encoder with 1120 units per layer, prediction network with 2-layer LSTM of 1088 units each; C-T uses a 4K subword sub-vocabulary, encoder consisting of 2-layer convolution (128 kernels of size 3, strides 2 and 1) plus projection to 512 dimensions via fully connected layer, followed by 14 conformer blocks (each containing 1024-unit FFN, 1 transformer layer with 8-head 64-dim attention, 1 convolution module with kernel width 15), prediction network with 2-layer LSTM of 736 units each; convolution and attention are calculated only on current and historical frames to ensure streaming capability; outputs of encoders and prediction networks for both backbones are projected via 512-unit FFN layers.

Regarding TRR, the highest relative degradation is 14% (RNN-T) and 12% (C-T)—this is the inherent cost paid for accuracy by the biasing mechanism; but dual-attention, in RNN-T at λ=64/32/8 and all λ levels of C-T, has TRRR slightly better than the single-attention baseline (check Table 1 one by one: e.g., C-T dual-attn-64 TRRR is only -7.98%, significantly better than single-attn's -12.84%). **ASR Accuracy (Table 2, WERR relative to pretrained-base)**: On the proper noun test set, dual-attention reduces WER by a maximum of 28.6% relative to RNN-T (corresponding to +28.64% for dual-attn-8 in Table 2), exceeding the single-attention baseline's 26.7% (+26.73%); on C-T, the maximum is 30.4% (+30.42% for dual-attn-16), exceeding single-attention's 29.1% (+29.08%). The source of gain is attributed to catalog masking and dedicated MHA "focusing on more relevant catalogs" (Section 5.2). On the general test set, WERR fluctuates slightly between -0.2% and +0.8%, basically neutral—this shows that the strategy of freezing the backbone + only training biasing layers successfully preserved general recognition quality. Compressing λ from 64 to 8 does not cause degradation on the proper noun and general test sets relative to the single-attention baseline (Table 2), meaning reduced computing power does not come at the cost of ASR accuracy.

Summary-Level Total Numbers: Wake word frame computing cost reduced by 90% (conservative estimate; Table 1 dual-attn-64 is actually -96.9%); parameters increased by only 1% (83K, from 400K to 483K, accounting for about 1% of the total 80M parameters, Section 5.3); wake word F1 relative improvement 16% (+16.42% for dual-attn-64 RNN-T); general rare word error rate relative improvement 3%—this last 3% cannot be traced in the main text and Table 2 (Table 2 general column is only +0.2%~+0.8%). It should be understood as rare word subset data in the abstract's scope, which the paper does not report in detail.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

1. **Catalog Masking Alone is a Free Lunch**: Compared to single-attn-base-128, single-attn-catalog-mask reduces FLOPs from 3.3M to 190K (-93.7%), with zero parameter increase (400K→400K), while F1R does not drop but rises—RNN-T from +14.72% to +17.03%, C-T from +14.96% to +16.93% (Table 1). This is the cleanest evidence in the paper: narrowing lead-in candidates from 300 to 6 saves 93.7% computation and makes attention more focused. Saving computing power and improving accuracy are not a trade-off at this design point; they are in the same direction.
2. **λ Scan Marks the Capacity Lower Bound**: From λ=64 to 8, FLOPs drop from 95K to 12K (Table 1). Wake word gains are basically maintained at λ=16 (RNN-T F1R +14.34%), but collapse at λ=8—RNN-T F1R drops to +2.38%, C-T to +9.30% (Table 1; the main text in Section 5.3 states "relative F1 improvement of 1% and 6%", which has an unexplained discrepancy with the Table 1 numbers). Meanwhile, the proper noun WERR for λ=8 is actually the highest among all RNN-T levels (Table 2 +28.64%)—the projection dimension of the small MHA is compressed too severely, hurting wake word discrimination, not the proper noun path (proper nouns go through $A_l$, which does not vary with λ). The sweet spot is in the λ=16~32 range.
3. **Multi-Head is Useless**: Both small and large MHA use only 1 attention head; trying 2 and 4 heads yields no accuracy gain (Section 4.2). Catalog retrieval does not need the diversity of multi-head subspaces, saving a default overhead item for similar biasing designs.
4. **Attention Visualization Validates Mechanism**: Figure 2 provides attention heatmaps for dual-attn-64 (each cell is 60ms). The small MHA has high weights on the true wake word "hey shaq" and the rows for masked proper nouns are completely dark; the large MHA has high weights on the target proper noun "brad pitt" and the wake word catalog is masked. Masking indeed makes each attention look only at what it should look at, and the design intent at the mechanism level is visually confirmed.
5. **Systematic Degradation of TRR**: The TRRR of all biasing variants degrades relative to the baseline (Table 1), confirming the prediction in Section 5.1 that "biasing towards accepting more queries"; the presence of the no-bias token can only mitigate but not eliminate this trade-off.

## Main Contributions

1. **Dual-Attention Neural Biasing Architecture**: For the first time (within the scope cited by the paper), the computational pivot mechanism of bifocal is extended from the audio encoder to the biasing attention network. The wake word detection signal simultaneously switches the encoder branch, attention branch, and catalog subset, achieving a new paradigm of "segment-conditioned biasing."
2. **Dynamic Catalog Masking**: Candidate narrowing driven by the wake word end frame index compresses the lead-in biasing candidates from hundreds (300 in evaluation scope) to single digits (6 wake words). This single item contributes -93.7% FLOPs and simultaneously improves F1 (Table 1).
3. **Pareto Results**: Taking dual-attn-64 as an example, lead-in biasing FLOPs are reduced by 96.9% relative to the single-attention baseline (3.3M→95K), parameters increase by only 83K (about 1% of the 80M backbone), wake word F1 relative to the bifocal baseline is +16.42%, and proper noun WERR is on par with or better than single-attention (Table 1, Table 2), holding true for both RNN-T and C-T backbones.
4. **Engineering Recipe**: A lightweight adaptation process that freezes the 114,000-hour pre-trained backbone and only fine-tunes the context encoder and two MHAs, allowing the new mechanism to be deployed without touching general ASR quality—near-zero fluctuation in the general column of Table 2 is direct validation of this recipe.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Strong Dependence on Wake Word Detection**: Encoder switching, attention branch selection, and catalog masking timing are all hung on the frame index of the wake word detection signal. False positives/negatives of the detector will cascade to the entire biasing link. The paper does not report sensitivity analysis to detector error rates, nor does it discuss hysteresis processing for mask switching when the wake word end boundary jitters—this is a general risk of reusing "existing signals in the system" as control flow.
- **TRR Cost Cannot Be Eliminated**: Biasing mechanism trading TAR inevitably pays for TRR. In Table 1, the relative degradation of TRRR for all dual-attention levels reaches up to -16.63% (RNN-T dual-attn-16). For commercial wake systems, the accept/reject operating point needs to be recalibrated. The paper does not report absolute operating points, so it is impossible to judge whether this degradation is acceptable under production thresholds.
- **Hard Bottom of Capacity at λ=8**: The small MHA projection dimension has an information bottleneck. At λ=8, wake word F1R collapses to +2.38% (RNN-T, Table 1)—exactly in the most latency-sensitive lead-in segment. There is an insurmountable lower limit to saving computing power, rather than a curve that can be extrapolated infinitely.
- **Applicability Boundary of the Low-Perplexity Assumption for Lead-in Segment**: The entire design bets on "speech before the wake word has low perplexity." The performance of the coarse-grained small MHA is not verified when the lead-in is long (e.g., user says "uh, um, hey Shaq") or when the wake word has a heavy accent.
- **Disconnection Between Catalog Scale Evaluation and Design Goals**: Section 3 claims the design goal for the proper noun catalog is tens of thousands, but the lead-in catalog evaluated in Table 1 has only 300 entries (6 wake words + remaining proper nouns). The paper does not report the actual FLOPs and latency of the large attention $A_l$ at the tens-of-thousands catalog scale—the cost story for large-scale scenarios lacks empirical support.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **All Internal Data, Irreproducible**: Footnote 1 admits that no large-scale public dataset containing diverse user-defined wake words exists. The 114,000 hours of pre-training + internal test set make the results irreproducible or comparable by the external community.
- **Too Little Wake Word Diversity**: Evaluation uses only 6 custom wake words and 25 hours of annotated data (Section 4.1). Generalization on wake words with different phoneme compositions and lengths is unknown.
- **Only Relative Metrics**: The paper reports relative changes such as F1R/TARR/TRRR/WERR throughout. Absolute TAR/TRR/WER are not reported—readers cannot know the true operating point of the baseline or the true usability of dual-attention.
- **No On-Device Measurement**: Computing cost uses lead-in MHA FLOPs per frame as a proxy metric. There are no measurements of latency, memory bandwidth, or power consumption on real embedded hardware. The conclusion of "mitigating attention runtime latency" (Section 6) remains at the FLOPs inference level.
- **Untraceable Abstract Numbers**: The abstract claims a 3% relative improvement in general rare word error rate, but there is no corresponding data in the main text tables (Table 2 general column is +0.2%~+0.8%); Section 5.3 "relative F1 improvement of 1% and 6% at λ=8" also has an unexplained discrepancy with Table 1's +2.38%/+9.30%.
- **No Statistical Significance Test**: General WERR differences of ±0.2% magnitude (Table 2) lack confidence intervals or significance support and may fall within the noise range.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Robustness to Detector Errors**: Introduce hysteresis or soft transitions for mask switching, or inject wake word detection noise during training for perturbation augmentation, explicitly modeling the sensitivity of the control flow to upstream signals. Alternatively, explore end-to-end joint training of the detector and biasing branches.
- **Learnable Gating Replacing Hard Masking**: Hard masking by frame index is a discrete decision. Changing to differentiable soft catalog weighting (learning the participation degree of each catalog entry in each segment) may simultaneously preserve focusing gains and boundary robustness.
- **Large-Scale Catalog Empirical Testing**: Truly expand the proper noun catalog to tens of thousands of entries, empirically test the FLOPs/latency curve of $A_l$, and combine it with weight interpretation methods like quantization and sparsity—the two are orthogonal, and theoretically, the gains are multiplicative.
- **Expand Evaluation Dimensions**: More wake words, multi-language wake words, accented lead-in speech, cross-user generalization, and report absolute operating points and on-device measured latency and power consumption.
- **Methodology Transfer**: "Segment-conditioned biasing based on interaction segments" is a transferable pattern—any streaming system with trigger detection priors (hot words in dialogue systems, contact dialing in vehicles, participant names in conference systems) can use trigger signals to gate the computation path of context biasing, spending expensive attention only on frames where it might be effective. This is a universal inspiration from this paper beyond the Alexa scenario.
