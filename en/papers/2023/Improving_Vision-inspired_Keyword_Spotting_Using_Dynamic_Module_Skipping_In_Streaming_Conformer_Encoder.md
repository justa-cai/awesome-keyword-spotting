# Improving vision-inspired keyword spotting using dynamic module skipping in streaming conformer encoder

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Alexandre Bittar, Paul Dixon, Mohammad Samragh, Kumari Nishu, Devang Naik (Apple)
- **Date**: August 2023 (arXiv:2309.00140v1, submitted 2023-08-31)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00140
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, streaming conformer, input-dependent dynamic depth, binary gating, word localization, efficient inference, always-on device

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The classic architecture for on-device speech interfaces is a two-stage cascade of a "lightweight keyword detector and a heavyweight recognition model": a small-parameter KWS model that runs continuously listens for interesting words in the continuous audio stream, and only wakes up the large-scale end-to-end ASR model when a hit is detected. This paper explicitly points out the dual motivations of this architecture—privacy and efficiency: the ASR model should only be triggered by the lightweight KWS on audio segments that are truly needed (Section 1). If the KWS cannot provide precise temporal boundaries for keywords, either the entire audio segment is sent to the large model (wasting compute) or the trigger logic relies on coarse energy detection (leading to false alarms). Therefore, "localization" is not just an auxiliary output but a key variable that determines how much compute the cascaded system can save.

The specific technical lineage of this paper is "vision-inspired KWS": treating an audio segment as a one-dimensional image allows the methodology of object detection to be transferred as a whole. From SpeechYOLO (Reference [9]) to open-vocabulary localization based on transformers (Reference [10]), anchor-free detectors (Reference [11]), and finally the foundational framework of Samragh et al.'s ICASSP 2023 work (Reference [12]), this route has formed a standard pipeline with three heads for "detection, classification, and localization." Reference [12] uses a fully convolutional BC-ResNet (Reference [16]) as the encoder, regressing three sets of outputs simultaneously from the encoded representation. What this paper does is: keep this vision-style pipeline unchanged, replace the encoder with a conformer, and equip it with trainable binary gates so that the network depth varies dynamically with the input.

The pain points can be broken down into three levels. First, resource constraints on always-on devices: the KWS model must run in a streaming manner, have an upper bound on memory usage, and have short user-perceived latency, which rules out directly搬ing large ASR-level encoders. Second, the task difficulty of continuous speech: Librispeech consists of read continuous discourse, where multiple keywords often collide within a single window, and there are a large number of homophones with different spellings (peace vs. piece, night vs. knight, right vs. write). The paper explicitly treats them as different classes (Section 3.1), which is a real test of the encoder's context modeling capability. Third, the energy structure of always-on scenarios: a detector hanging on a device listening all day, for the vast majority of the time, faces non-speech segments. Fixed-depth networks continue to calculate at full load on these "trivial inputs," wasting battery power.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

First, the ceiling of context modeling for fully convolutional encoders. Convolutional backbones like BC-ResNet are good at capturing local correlations but lack explicit global interaction mechanisms. The convolutional receptive field needs to be expanded slowly by stacking layers, while distinguishing homophones or determining whether a phoneme segment constitutes a complete keyword often requires context at the scale of the entire window. This shortcoming is directly reflected in the experiments: the reproduced BC-ResNet-L baseline has a false alarm rate as high as 0.314 per second and a precision of only 0.749 on Librispeech test-other (Table 1), indicating that it heavily misclassifies non-keywords as keywords on hard samples.

Second, end-to-end ASR encoders cannot be directly ported. The conformer (Reference [17]) is indeed the mainstream choice for speech recognition, with convolution modules handling local aspects and self-attention handling global aspects. However, the native setting of the conformer is offline sentence input, unbounded attention context, and model size serving recognition accuracy. Putting it directly into KWS would simultaneously violate streaming constraints (context grows infinitely with time) and memory constraints. The paper must redesign the input window mechanism to "fit" the conformer into the streaming pipeline.

Third, existing dynamic computation work does not cover this scenario. Peng et al.'s I3D (Reference [18]) proved that adding binary gates to transformer-structured ASR can skip modules based on input, reducing average computation while keeping word error rate unchanged. However, it only addressed offline ASR, did not touch conformers (which have four different types of modules), did not address streaming, and did not address the multi-task of KWS detection plus localization. Whether the skipping mechanism still holds on streaming small models, and whether gating training would destroy localization accuracy, are unverified issues.

Fourth, static depth wastes compute. Once a traditional KWS model is trained, the computation is fixed, executing the same deep network regardless of whether the input is quiet noise or a difficult accent. The paper uses experiments with GSC overlaid with noise to illustrate how severe this problem is: pure noise segments can skip 97% of the encoder computation (last column of Table 1), while the computation of fixed-depth networks on these inputs is a completely sunk cost.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

In summary, this paper aims to satisfy four sets of mutually constraining constraints simultaneously: First, install the conformer encoder into the streaming window pipeline, with bounded attention context (1.2-second window), small memory footprint (parameters reduced by more than 16% compared to the baseline), and fixed output rhythm (one prediction every 240 milliseconds); Second, extend the I3D gating from transformer-ASR to conformer, where binary gates can be inserted into the residual connections of all four module types (two feed-forward, multi-head self-attention, convolution), and discrete gate decisions must be trainable end-to-end; Third, skipping computation must not come at the cost of detection and localization accuracy; a gate open-rate regularization must be used to control the balance point between accuracy and compute savings; Fourth, efficiency gains must be quantified by scenario—the skip rates under continuous speech, isolated commands, and pure noise must be reported separately to support the conclusion that it is "suitable for always-on detectors."

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall pipeline follows Reference [12], divided into four stages (Figure 1 provides the flowchart for the training mode). The first stage is features and windows: input audio uses a 25 ms window and 10 ms stride to extract 40-channel Mel-filterbank features; to satisfy both streaming and limited attention context requirements, the encoder input is configured with a 1.2-second window (120 frames) and a 240 ms slide (24 frames), with tensor shape 120×40 (Section 2.1). The streaming mechanism during inference is simple: the model saves the last 960 ms of the current window, waits for new 240 ms of audio, concatenates them to form the next 1.2-second window, and repeats. The benefit of this design is that the attention context is always pinned within 1.2 seconds (memory and computation do not grow with duration), and a batch of predictions is produced every 240 ms—the paper points out that this 240 ms rhythm almost constitutes the entire user-perceived latency (Section 2.5). During training, the sliding window is calculated once from complete sentences and stacked on the batch dimension, naturally completing data organization.

The second stage is the encoder: a sub-sampling convolutional layer compresses the time dimension from 120 to 29, while projecting the feature dimension to hidden width H, followed by $N_z$ standard conformer blocks. Each block contains four modules in sequence: feed-forward, multi-head self-attention, convolution, and feed-forward (Section 2.2). These four modules do not change the tensor shape—this seemingly ordinary property is the structural foundation for the entire gating scheme: shape invariance means a scalar gate can be multiplied on the residual branch without breaking dimension consistency. After compressing the time dimension to 29, each output step corresponds to 40 ms of original duration, which aligns perfectly with the receptive field stride $S=40$ ms in the subsequent label construction, and also significantly reduces the quadratic complexity of self-attention within the window—this is a key step in fitting the conformer into the on-device budget.

The third stage is three output heads: the detection head is a feed-forward layer plus sigmoid, mapping the encoding $z$ to frame-wise keyword existence probability (Equation (4)); the classification head first masks classes with detection probability below 0.5 using a binary mask, then passes through softmax to output class probabilities (Equation (5)), and additionally adds a "no keyword" class (Class $C+1$) to handle blank windows; the localization head uses a feed-forward layer to regress the width and offset of each keyword (Equation (6)), adopting a CenterNet-style (Reference [15]) representation.

The fourth stage is max-pooling and event generation: max-pooling is performed on the classification output over 29 time steps with a kernel width of 24 and stride of 1 (24 steps correspond to 1 second, stride corresponds to 40 ms), resulting in 6 output steps per window. The selected indices are then used to index the detection, width, and offset outputs (Section 2.3). The kernel width of 24 is not chosen arbitrarily—it precisely equals the span of a 1-second receptive field under the 40 ms output stride. The pooling window sweeps over 29 steps to produce 6 positions ($29-24+1=6$), which strictly aligns with the number of output steps $T=6N_w$ in the label construction. The paper follows the explanation in WeKws (Reference [23]) for the motivation: max-pooling ensures that the loss only optimizes the steps with the highest posterior probability, avoiding redundant supervision for multiple adjacent steps of the same event.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The mathematical form of the gating is extremely concise. For any gated module, the residual connection is rewritten as:

$$x \rightarrow x + g(\theta, x) \cdot \text{module}(x) \tag{1}$$

where $g(\theta, x) \in \{0,1\}$ is the binary gate. The gate itself is implemented using a linear layer with weights $W_g \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times 2}$ and bias $b_g \in \mathbb{R}^2$, transforming the time-averaged input $\bar{x}$ of the module and passing it through softmax:

$$p_g(x) = \text{Softmax}(W_g \cdot \bar{x} + b_g) \tag{2}$$

yielding the keep and skip probabilities $[p_{\text{keep}}, p_{\text{skip}}]$, where softmax ensures their sum is one. There are two notable design choices here. First, the gate looks at the time-averaged input $\bar{x}$ rather than frame-wise input, so the decision granularity of the gate is the entire window rather than a single frame—the number of gate parameters is compressed to a minimum (only $2H+2$ parameters per gate), and judging "whether this window needs this module" during inference costs only one dot product. Second, different gate functions are used for training and inference: during training, the Gumbel-Softmax trick (References [21][22]) is used to differentially sample discrete 0/1 values from $p_{\text{keep}}$, allowing binary decisions to backpropagate gradients; during inference, it degenerates to a hard decision $p_{\text{keep}} > \beta$, with the threshold fixed at $\beta = 0.5$. This two-stage approach of "relaxed training, hard decision deployment" is the standard solution for discrete gating; otherwise, non-differentiable binary gates cannot be trained end-to-end.

To make the gates truly learn to "close," a gate open-rate regularization is added during training:

$$L_{\text{gate}} = \lambda \cdot f_{\text{open}}, \quad f_{\text{open}} = \frac{1}{4 N_z B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \sum_{l=1}^{N_z} \sum_{m=1}^{4} g(\theta_{l,m}, x_b) \tag{3}$$

with hyperparameter $\lambda = 1$. This regularization averages the gate outputs over all batch elements, all $N_z$ blocks, and the 4 modules of each block, directly penalizing the "open ratio." Why is it needed: without constraints, the gating network has a famous lazy solution—opening all gates, causing the model to degenerate into static depth, with zero skip rate and a failed compute-saving goal; conversely, if the penalty is too strong, the gates may collectively close, leading to a collapse in accuracy. $\lambda=1$ is the balance point given in the paper, but the paper does not report scanning experiments for this hyperparameter.

The mathematics on the label and loss side also has a self-consistent logic. Detection labels are constructed using the "Intersection over Ground Truth" (IOG) metric: for a keyword $c$ with start and end times $(b, e)$, the overlap ratio of the receptive field window $(tS, tS+R)$ with it is defined as the overlap duration divided by the keyword's own duration $e-b$ (Equation (7)). The reason for choosing IOG instead of the common IoU lies in the denominator—the keyword is the "object," and the window is the "field of view." As long as the window covers most of the keyword, it counts as seeing the object, regardless of how large the window itself is. Accordingly, when IOG is greater than 0.95, the detection label is 1; when less than 0.5, it is 0; when between the two, the label is defined as "undefined" and masked out, producing no gradient updates, trained with thresholded binary cross-entropy loss (Equation (8)). The motivation for masking the middle ground is clear: when the window only covers half of the keyword, forcing a label of 1 or 0 is noisy supervision. Classification labels are similar (Equation (9)): when the IOG for any class exceeds 0.95, it is labeled as that class (taking the one with the highest IOG); only when all classes are below 0.05 is it labeled as Class $C+1$; the middle ground is similarly masked. Localization labels are constructed in the CenterNet manner: the receptive field center is $c_t = t + R/(2S)$, the width label is normalized to $(e-b)/R$, and the offset label is the offset of the keyword center relative to the receptive field center $(b+e)/(2S) - c_t$ (Equation (10)). The localization loss is the L1 distance between these and the predicted values. Width and offset can be inverted to solve for start and end times, which is exactly the source of event boundaries during inference.

During inference (Section 2.5), 6 prediction steps are produced every 240 ms. The score for each step is taken as the maximum of the classification probabilities for all true keyword classes; if the score exceeds threshold $\vartheta$, an event candidate is proposed, with the class taken as argmax, and the start and end times are inverted from the width and offset predictions of that step. Finally, Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS, Reference [24]) is used to select the best non-overlapping proposals based on score and time, suppressing duplicate candidates for the same event.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Windowing Mechanism for Installing Conformer into Streaming KWS

The most fundamental innovation of this work is actually the "streaming-capable conformer encoder" itself. The conformer in ASR defaults to whole-sentence input, while the three numbers here constitute a complete streaming contract: a 1.2-second window defines the attention context (memory upper bound), a 240 ms slide defines the output rhythm (latency upper bound), and 960 ms cache reuse avoids re-computing audio seen in the previous window. The sub-sampling layer compresses 120 frames to 29 frames, establishing the quadratic cost of self-attention on the compressed time axis. The deep value of this design lies in proving that the vision-style KWS pipeline of detection plus localization does not need to be bound to a fully convolutional backbone; global attention mechanisms can benefit under strict streaming and memory constraints.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: I3D Gating Extended to Conformer

The second innovation is extending Peng et al.'s I3D from transformer-ASR to conformer. The extension is not a translation: transformer blocks have only two types of skippable objects (feed-forward and attention), while conformer blocks have four types (feed-forward, multi-head self-attention, convolution, feed-forward) with different properties (convolution handles local patterns, attention handles global alignment, feed-forward handles feature transformation). The paper installs independent local gates on the residual connection of each module, where the gate looks only at the input of that specific module (Section 2.2). This brings an important granularity change: different types of modules can decide to open or close independently based on the input of the same window. For example, a noise window might only retain the convolution modules of early blocks and turn off all attention. With $N_z$ blocks and 4 modules, the model has $4N_z$ gates in total—32 gates for the Librispeech configuration and 12 gates for the GSC configuration. The parameter cost of the gates is almost negligible: the gated model on GSC has only about 1k more parameters than the non-gated one (93k vs. 94k, Table 1).

There is also a pragmatic design in the training strategy: pre-train for several epochs without gates, then enable the gates (Section 2.2). Why this is necessary: the gate looks at the time average of the module input; if the network is not trained well, this statistic is basically noise, and the gate learns a strategy of random opening; training the representation first and then the gate gives the gate meaningful signals to rely on. The paper also points out that this mechanism can be used in reverse to fine-tune and add gates to already-trained non-gated conformers—this is valuable for deployment, meaning there is no need to retrain large models to gain skipping capabilities. The paper does not report the specific number of pre-training epochs or the sensitivity of the gate-opening timing.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Capacity-Equivalent "Skip" vs. "Shrink" Control

The third innovation is in the experimental design: using Ours-S (a shrunken non-gated conformer) as a capacity-equivalent control to prove that "dynamic skipping" is superior to "static shrinking." Ours-L-gated and Ours-S have comparable average computation (the former skips 30% of computation, the latter is smaller overall), but the former is significantly better on all metrics on both Librispeech test sets (Table 1: F1 0.892 vs. 0.870 on test-clean, F1 0.769 vs. 0.737 on test-other). This comparison is the pivot of the paper's efficiency argument—it shows that the correct posture for saving compute is not to uniformly thin the network (making all inputs worse), but to maintain network capacity and let simple inputs take shallow paths (hard inputs still take the full depth).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the baseline framework Reference [12]: the pipeline (features, three-head output, IOG labels, NMS evaluation) is completely inherited, with differences concentrated in the encoder (BC-ResNet replaced by conformer plus gating) and the streaming window mechanism. Therefore, the comparative experiments can cleanly attribute gains to the encoder itself. Compared to the original I3D paper Reference [18]: it migrates from offline ASR (metric is word error rate) to streaming KWS (metrics are detection, localization, false alarm rate family), extends from two types of transformer modules to four types of conformer modules, and adds gate open-rate regularization and the strategy of pre-training before enabling gates. Compared to vision-style KWS literature [9][11] in the SpeechYOLO lineage: this paper explicitly treats "memory upper bound under streaming constraints" and "input-conditioned computation" as first-class design goals, rather than just pursuing offline accuracy. A point of honest emphasis: the paper admits in the introduction that skipping modules does not reduce the model volume that needs to be loaded, nor does it significantly improve user-perceived latency—it improves average computation and power consumption (Section 1). This boundary is drawn clearly, avoiding the common over-promotion of dynamic depth.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Two datasets are used in the experiments, corresponding to the scenarios of "detection and localization in continuous speech" and "isolated commands in noisy streams." The first is Librispeech (Reference [19]): English audiobook corpus sampled at 16 kHz, with a training set of 280k sentences totaling 960 hours. The definition of the KWS task is to take the 1000 most frequent words from the training set to form the vocabulary, with word-level start and end times obtained via alignment using Montreal Forced Aligner (Reference [25]) (Section 3.1). The paper emphasizes that this is a quite difficult task: first, continuous reading leads to frequent collisions of keywords within a single window; second, homophones with different spellings are distinguished as different classes. Results are reported on test-clean and the harder test-other. The second is the 35 command words of Google Speech Commands v2 (Reference [20]), placed in isolation on the noisy human voice stream of the MS-SNSD dataset (Reference [30]), with signal-to-noise ratios between 10 and 40 dB (Section 3.2). This construction is deliberate to demonstrate the efficiency gains of gating: commands appear sparsely, and background noise dominates, perfectly simulating the real input distribution of an always-on detector. The paper does not report the test set scale for both datasets, nor distortion types other than noise (such as reverberation, far-field).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric system covers detection, localization, and retrieval perspectives (Section 3.1). False Rejection Rate (FRR) is defined as FN/(FN+TP), i.e., the proportion of true keywords missed; False Alarm Rate (FAR) is defined as the number of false reports per second—the reason for using "per second" instead of "per word" or "percentage" is clear: in streaming always-on scenarios, false alarms accumulate linearly with time, and the false alarm rate per second is the deployment dimension that directly corresponds to "will it be annoying, will it repeatedly wake up the large model." Precision, Recall, F1, Event-level Accuracy, and Mean IoU follow the standards of SpeechYOLO and Reference [12] (the paper does not provide the formula details for Event-level Accuracy). Mean IoU measures the quality of localization boundaries, a practical metric in cascaded architectures that determines "how much audio to cut and give to the large model." MTWV (Mean Word-Weighted Value) is the standard metric from the NIST 2006 Spoken Term Detection evaluation (Reference [28]), calculated on 20 keywords selected from Reference [29], with independent threshold tuning for each word—it treats KWS as a retrieval task evaluated over the entire operating point scan,弥补ing the blind spots of single-threshold metrics. For judgment details, the NMS score threshold is taken as $\vartheta=0.95$, and a predicted event is recorded as a true positive if it has temporal overlap with a true event of the same class. Finally, the paper reports the proportion of multiply-accumulate operations skipped in the encoder (Skips%) as an efficiency metric—this is a new dimension brought by I3D, with no corresponding object in traditional KWS papers.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The main comparison object is the author's reproduced Reference [12] BC-ResNet baseline (Table 1), which ensures complete consistency in training data, labels, and evaluation standards. On Librispeech test-clean: BC-ResNet-L with 1.54M parameters achieves FRR 0.132, FAR 0.192, F1 0.862, MTWV 0.78; switching to the non-gated conformer (Ours-L) with 1.29M parameters, FRR drops slightly to 0.129, FAR is almost halved from 0.192 to 0.097, precision rises from 0.856 to 0.922, F1 rises to 0.896, and MTWV jumps from 0.78 to 0.87—parameters are 16% fewer, and metrics are almost universally improved. The contrast on test-other is more stark: BC-ResNet-L has a FAR as high as 0.314, precision only 0.749, and MTWV only 0.58; Ours-L presses FAR to 0.146 (more than halved), lifts precision to 0.869, F1 from 0.715 to 0.779, and MTWV from 0.58 to 0.76. The distribution of gains is very informative: recall hardly moves (0.684 to 0.705), and the improvement comes entirely from the false alarm side. Why this pattern—self-attention establishes global context within the 1.2-second window, and the biggest beneficiary is the modeling of "Class $C+1$ (no keyword)" and the discrimination of homophones. In other words, the conformer mainly teaches the model "what is not a keyword," rather than "catching more keywords." For an always-on detector, this is exactly the most valuable direction: the sacrifice of missed detection is limited, but false alarms will repeatedly wake up the large model, consume power, and bring privacy concerns. It is necessary to point out two exceptions where the baseline surpasses: on test-clean, BC-ResNet's Event-level Accuracy (0.872 vs. 0.861) and Mean IoU (0.850 vs. 0.839) are slightly higher than Ours-L, meaning the convolution backbone has slightly better localization boundaries on clean data. The paper is honest about this in its claim of "almost universal metric improvement."

On the GSC noisy scenario (Table 1): BC-ResNet-XS with 139k parameters achieves F1 0.955, IoU 0.762, MTWV 0.84; Ours-XS with 93k parameters (32% fewer) lifts F1 to 0.964, IoU to 0.818, MTWV to 0.89, and presses FAR from 0.004 per second to 0.002. In the task of isolated commands with noise, the conformer improves both discrimination and localization boundaries, and the parameter advantage is more obvious.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

This paper does not have an independent ablation table, but the model matrix in Table 1 serves the ablation function, allowing four sets of findings to be extracted. First, the cost of gating on/off: with the same 1.29M parameters, Ours-L-gated skips 30% of encoder computation on test-clean relative to Ours-L, with minimal changes in metrics (F1 from 0.896 to 0.892, FRR from 0.129 to 0.134), and skips 26% on test-other (F1 from 0.779 to 0.769). The paper claims based on this that the gated version has "metric differences less than 1%"—this statement holds for the family of precision, recall, and F1, but the drop in MTWV in Table 1 is actually larger: from 0.87 to 0.84 on test-clean, and from 0.76 to 0.70 on test-other, which is a 3 to 6 point loss in retrieval performance, exceeding the paper's own expression. Second, capacity-equivalent control: Ours-L-gated completely crushes the smaller non-gated model Ours-S under comparable computation conditions (F1 0.769 vs. 0.737 on test-other, MTWV 0.70 vs. 0.67), proving that input-conditioned skipping is superior to uniform shrinking. Third, the IOU of the gated version on GSC drops from 0.818 to 0.757, which is the largest single-point degradation among all metrics—the damage of skipping modules to localization boundaries is amplified in the short command scenario, because the effective information of isolated commands is concentrated in a few frames, and module skipping is more likely to lose boundary information. Fourth, the input-dependence of efficiency: a 42% skip rate appears in speech-containing regions, and 97% in pure noise regions (last column of the GSC row in Table 1, dual values), differing by more than double, directly verifying the mechanism hypothesis that "the gate learned to distinguish speech from non-speech and adjust computation." Additionally, the paper reports in text that pre-training without gates before enabling gates is better than training with gates from scratch (Section 2.2), but does not provide a numerical comparison. Scans for gate position (which type of module is most worth gating), regularization coefficient $\lambda$, decision threshold $\beta$, and number of blocks $N_z$ are not reported by the paper.

## Main Contributions

First, it provides the first (according to the paper's review scope) complete solution for installing a streaming conformer encoder into a vision-inspired KWS pipeline: 1.2-second window, 240 ms slide, 960 ms cache reuse, four-fold sub-sampling. Under the premise that memory upper bounds and latency upper bounds are controlled, it brings global self-attention into the keyword detection and localization task, and proves via experiments that the gains are concentrated on the false alarm side, which is most relevant to deployment (test-other FAR drops from 0.314 to 0.146, Table 1).

Second, it extends I3D dynamic depth from transformer-ASR to the four types of modules in conformer and the multi-task of streaming KWS. It配套 solves three engineering problems: discrete gate training (Gumbel-Softmax sampling plus hard threshold inference), gate open-rate regularization (Equation (3)), and the strategy of pre-training before enabling gates. Moreover, the parameter overhead of the gates is close to zero (about 1k parameters on GSC, Table 1).

Third, it quantifies the realistic benefits of input-conditioned computation with three levels of skip rates: 30%, 42%, and 97% (corresponding to continuous speech, isolated command speech, and pure noise, respectively, in the abstract and Table 1). In particular, the 97% skip rate for pure noise segments turns the implicit waste of "always-on detectors spending most of their compute on non-speech" into an explicit cost that can be cut.

Fourth, the contribution in experimental design: the capacity-equivalent Ours-S control group places the two compute-saving routes of "dynamic skipping" and "static shrinking" on the same scale. The conclusion (skipping is better than shrinking) has methodological reference value for subsequent on-device efficient inference work.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First are the two hard boundaries admitted by the paper itself: skipping modules does not reduce the model volume that needs to be loaded (memory usage remains unchanged), nor does it significantly improve user-perceived latency (Section 1)—the gains are only reflected in average computation and the resulting power consumption. Second, the decision granularity of the gate is window-level: the gate input takes the time average of the module input (Equation (2)), meaning that all frames within the same 1.2-second window share the same open/close decision, and finer conditional computation cannot be done within the window; if a very short command is buried in long noise within the window, the gate can only make an overall decision. Third, the decision threshold $\beta$ is fixed at 0.5, and the regularization coefficient $\lambda$ is fixed at 1 (Section 2.2), so the operating point between "power-saving mode" and "full-precision mode" cannot be adjusted at runtime during deployment. This is less flexible than schemes that train multiple precision levels at once (such as width-prunable networks). Fourth, accuracy is not zero-cost: MTWV drops by 3 to 6 points after gating, and IoU drops by 6 points on GSC (Table 1), indicating that the conclusion of "maintaining equivalent performance" needs to be discounted on retrieval metrics and localization boundaries. Fifth, the evaluation stops at the skip rate level; the paper does not report absolute computation (MACs values), measured latency, measured power consumption, or energy efficiency dimensions such as detections per joule. "Significant power savings" is currently only a mechanism inference.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

The comparison scope is narrow: the baseline is only the author's reproduced BC-ResNet (Table 1), with no horizontal comparison with other streaming KWS encoders (such as cFSMN, DS-TCN, MatchboxNet, etc.), no comparison with other dynamic computation schemes with early-exit mechanisms, and no side-by-side verification with the original reported numbers of Reference [12]. The noise condition is singular: the noisy experiments only use one type of noisy human voice, with a single SNR interval of 10 to 40 dB (Section 3.2). Common robustness dimensions such as far-field, reverberation, multi-speaker overlap, and music background are all absent. The training configuration only reports details for the Librispeech side (H=80, $N_z$=8, Adam with cosine annealing from 0.001 to 0.0001, 100 epochs, 8 V100s, batch 8 per card, Section 3.1), while the GSC side only gives H=40, $N_z$=3. Ablation is missing: scans for gate position, $\lambda$, $\beta$, pre-training epochs, window length, and sub-sampling ratio are not provided. The variance and significance of the model (multiple random seeds) are not reported by the paper. MTWV is only calculated on 20 keywords, and extrapolating retrieval performance under a 1000-word vocabulary requires caution.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Along the route of this paper, at least four directions are worth advancing. The first is to refine the window-level gate to frame-level or token-level: change the gate to look at input summaries other than pooled statistics (such as time-weighted pooling or learned query vectors), allowing short commands to trigger local deepening, which is expected to repair the degradation of localization IoU on GSC. The second is to add runtime budget control to the gates: replace $\lambda$ with a tunable inference-period constraint (such as constrained decoding under a target skip rate), enabling the same model to switch operating points between sufficient and tight battery levels. Combined with the fixed $\beta=0.5$ scheme, this can be made into multiple power-saving modes. The third is to stack with quantization and distillation: gating reduces dynamic computation but does not reduce memory, while low-bit quantization reduces memory without changing the computation structure. The two are orthogonal, and the benefit space for on-device combinations is large. The fourth is to supplement with measured energy dimensions: measure power consumption per second and detection latency on real terminal chips, verifying how much battery time the 97% noise skip rate actually converts into—this is the final step to turn the qualitative assertion of "suitable for always-on detectors" into a quantitative conclusion. In addition, extending this gated conformer to custom wake words and multi-language vocabularies, and exploring the joint optimization of gate decisions and downstream NMS thresholds, are natural extensions.
