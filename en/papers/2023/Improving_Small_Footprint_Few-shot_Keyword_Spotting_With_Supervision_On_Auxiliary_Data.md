## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Few-Shot Keyword Spotting (FS-KWS) refers to a task where, given only a very small number of samples (one-shot and five-shot as investigated in this paper) for each target keyword in an audio stream, the model must detect the word from continuous speech. The corresponding product scenario is very specific: voice assistants need to support user-defined wake words. Users do not want to be bound by vendor-preinstalled words like "Alexa" or "OK Google"; they can simply speak their own word a few times to complete enrollment, after which the device constructs a new class using these samples and goes online for detection. Since enrollment samples must be contributed by the user on the spot, there are naturally only a few samples, making "few-shot" not just an academic modifier but a hard constraint of this scenario.

To support generalizing from "a few samples to unseen words," the mainstream approach is metric learning: during training, the model does not learn specific words but learns a discriminative embedding space. During inference, the embeddings of the enrollment samples are averaged to form a prototype, and test samples are classified based on their distance to the prototype. The implicit premise of this paradigm is that the quality of the embedding space is entirely shaped by the training data: the richer the training vocabulary and the more phoneme combinations it covers, the better the generalization to unseen words. Therefore, few-shot learning is essentially data-hungry: methods like ProtoNets and MAML rely on transferring prior knowledge from large-scale labeled data or related tasks with similar distributions (citations [8][9][10][11] in the paper).

Coincidentally, the field of keyword detection lacks large-scale labeled data. The paper points out the common flaws of public KWS datasets [12][13][14]: limited scale and few categories. Specifically, for the benchmark splitGSC used in this paper (based on Google Speech Commands), the training set contains only 24,400 samples and 15 keywords (including a silence class, see Section 4.1). Manually collecting "keyword-level" labeled data is an expensive engineering task—finding speakers, recording words, segmenting, and annotating; the cost increases linearly with each expansion of the vocabulary. As a result, even with advanced few-shot learning techniques (D-ProtoNets, citation [15], which is also a previous work by these authors), the baseline performance is only 82.0% closed-set accuracy and 82.9 open-set AUROC (Figure 1(a)), which is far from being usable.

At the same time, edge-side deployment draws another line for model size. Wake word detection is an always-on load, running on low-power DSPs or microcontrollers, so the model must be small—the main backbone BC-ResNet8 in this paper's experiments has only 321k parameters, and the smallest BC-ResNet1 has only 9.2k (Figure 4); even an 8M Res12 is considered a large model in this context. Thus, the contradiction closes: embedding space learning requires big data, small model capacity is insufficient to digest the combination of "big data + unlabeled" (SSL failure on small models detailed below), and labeled keyword big data is too expensive to collect.

The breakthrough this paper抓住es is: there is a type of data that is large in volume, free, and readily available—audiobook reading speech. Corpora like LibriSpeech (about 1,000 hours of read English, 16 kHz) and MLS are library-level stock, but they are prepared for speech recognition: they have only sentence-level transcriptions, no word-level alignment; the content is read speech, not command speech (dominated by function words like "and, said, time, that", see domain-out-of-distribution examples in Figure 2); the word distribution is extremely long-tailed (58,997 word types, Table 1). How to inject the phoneme-level discriminative information hidden in this cheap, "out-of-domain, word-unlabeled, long-tailed" data into a small model with hundreds of k or even 9.2k parameters is the main theme of the entire paper.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper's rejection of existing solutions is not vague but nailed down item by item with experimental data, a point done quite solidly in similar papers:

- **Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) route does not match small models.** The literature consensus is that SSL is only beneficial for models with sufficient parameters (citations [24] Chen et al. NeurIPS 2020, [25] Fang et al. ICLR 2021—"Large self-supervised models are strong semi-supervised learners"). This paper reproduced this conclusion under its own settings (Table 2): pretraining BC-ResNet8 on LibriSpeech using SimCLR and then running D-Proto on splitGSC yielded a five-shot closed-set accuracy of 79.9%, which is lower than the baseline of 82.0% trained directly from random initialization; using BYOL resulted in 82.0,持平 (持平 means equal/flat); combining SSL and D-Proto into a multi-task (MTL with SSL) yielded only 83.0. The authors' explanation is that the small-footprint model has too little capacity; SSL pretraining forces it to learn general information like "augmentation invariance," which instead crowds out and flattens the fine-grained representations required for keyword discrimination—the better the invariance is learned, the blurrier the discriminative boundaries between words. Note that this failure is not due to insufficient tuning: the paper explicitly states that extensive data augmentation and hyperparameter search were performed (Section 4.3).
- **Large model pretraining features (PreT-Big) are effective but violate the small-footprint premise.** Using the 300M parameter wav2vec/Hubert feature extractors provided by torchaudio, freezing them, and only fine-tuning the classifier, the Hubert version achieved a five-shot score of 90.4%/85.2, which is indeed significantly better than the baseline. However, 300M is three orders of magnitude larger than 321K; this solution is meaningless on always-on devices; moreover, the wav2vec version's five-shot AUROC was only 76.7, lower than the baseline of 82.9—large model SSL features are not a free lunch, and changing the pretraining objective can hurt open-set detection.
- **Knowledge Distillation (KD) route has marginal gains.** Using large model feature distillation (FitNets-style feature distillation, citation [38]) combined with D-Proto, KD w/ Hubert achieved only 83.7%/84.4 (Table 2), and the improvement relative to the baseline is completely incomparable to the supervision route. Why does distillation fail while direct supervision works? Distillation transfers the teacher's invariant representations, essentially pouring "SSL-friendly information" into the small model, with the same root cause as the first point.
- **TTS synthetic auxiliary data and audio-text alignment routes each have costs.** Lee et al. [6] used TTS to create synthetic keyword data, where there is a significant domain gap between synthetic speech and real command speech (prosody, channel, pronunciation style); Shin et al. [7] utilized the linguistic correspondence between speech and text sequences for open-vocabulary KWS, requiring text modality support. The differentiated claim of this paper is: using real reading speech as an auxiliary domain, using a supervised multi-task strategy, with the target locked on lightweight models.
- **Naive mixed training (ALL) causes representation skew.** The most intuitive approach is to concatenate LibriWord and splitGSC into a single dataset and train D-Proto with a single loss (called ALL in the paper). The result is that closed-set accuracy jumps to 92.2%, but open-set AUROC is only 86.6 (Table 2, Figure 1(c))—closed-set increased by ten percentage points, but open-set barely moved, and one-shot AUROC even dropped to 75.3 (lower than the baseline of 75.7). The reason is that a single loss directly lets domain-in command words and domain-out reading words share the same feature extractor and the same episode construction; the quantity and distribution of reading words dominate the geometric shape of the embedding space, skewing the prototype structure of command words, with open-set discrimination (judging "this sentence is not any registered word") suffering the most.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

Converging the above analysis into one sentence: How to inject the value of large-scale out-of-domain reading speech into lightweight FS-KWS models without increasing inference model size, without adding any inference time overhead, and without requiring manual annotation. It breaks down into three hurdles—the data hurdle (reading speech has no word-level annotation, long-tailed distribution, out-of-domain), the method hurdle (the unsupervised utilization path of SSL has been falsified for small models), and the training hurdle (direct mixed training with a single loss will cause representation skew and harm open-set performance). The paper's answer is a combination of punches: automatic segmentation and alignment plus filtering and balancing on the data side to create LibriWord, and a split multi-task strategy on the training side to create AuxSL; both are indispensable (ablation studies later will prove this).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire framework consists of two coupled components: the **LibriWord dataset** (transforming the unsupervised problem back into a supervised problem) and the **AuxSL training framework** (injecting out-of-domain supervision signals via split paths). The key to understanding the design motivation is a "problem transformation": since the root cause of SSL failure on small models is that small-capacity models cannot digest unsupervised invariance learning, do not force it—use the Montreal Forced Aligner to automatically cut reading speech into samples with word labels, and the problem immediately retreats from "small model + unsupervised + big data" to the supervised classification that small models are best at. This transformation costs almost zero human effort (the aligner is ready-made, the corpus is public), yet unlocks all subsequent supervised training techniques.

The structure of AuxSL (Figure 2) is very restrained: a shared feature extractor $F_\theta$ and two loss paths. The in-domain path is episode-based metric learning on splitGSC, using the dummy prototype loss $L_{FSL}$ of D-ProtoNets, maintaining the main line of "constructing prototype geometry on the command word vocabulary" without interference; the out-of-domain path is a one-layer fully connected classifier $C_\phi$ attached to the same $F_\theta$, performing cross-entropy classification on one thousand words of LibriWord, with the loss added with weight $\lambda$. The classifier is completely discarded during inference—the enrollment and detection processes are exactly the same as pure D-Proto, with **zero additional inference cost**, which is the hard prerequisite for it to land on edge devices.

Why must it be split rather than trained together like ALL? The authors' expression is "to alleviate the representation skew that may arise from directly sharing the feature extractor between in-domain and out-of-domain data," a思路 inspired by Zhou et al. [34] (concept space meta-learning). Mechanistically, it can be understood this way: the loss shape of metric learning strongly depends on the vocabulary composition of the episode—in ALL, the episode samples from the mixed vocabulary, and reading words (one thousand classes, three hundred samples each) overwhelm command words (a dozen classes) in quantity; the distance structure between prototypes is actually optimized for "reading word separability," and the boundary between command words and open-set classes is just incidental; in AuxSL, the in-domain episode is still the pure command word vocabulary, and the prototype geometry is always optimized for the target task, while out-of-domain gradients only "indirectly enrich features" through the shared encoder, without directly rewriting the geometry of the target task. In other words, the auxiliary classification head acts as a "pressure relief valve" for out-of-domain gradients: it absorbs the discriminative pressure of one thousand reading words in its own fully connected layer, and only returns general low-level features invariant to phonemes, speakers, and channels back to $F_\theta$.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Episode training of prototype networks** (Section 3.1, Equation 1). Each training iteration constructs an N-way K-shot problem: N is the number of classes, K is the number of support samples per class. The prototype for each class is the average of the embeddings of the support samples:

$$c_n = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K} F_\theta(x_{n,i})$$

The prototype loss forces query samples to be close to their own class's prototype:

$$L_{FSL} = -\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} \log p_\theta(y = n \mid x^q_{n,i})$$

$$p_\theta(y = n \mid x^q_{n,i}) = \frac{\exp(-d(F_\theta(x^q_{n,i}), c_n))}{\sum_{n'=1}^{N} \exp(-d(F_\theta(x^q_{n,i}), c_{n'}))}$$

where $d(\cdot)$ can be any distance metric, $x^q_{n,i}$ is the i-th query sample of the n-th class, and M is the number of query samples per class. During inference, new class prototypes are obtained by averaging the embeddings of enrollment samples, and test samples are classified according to the probability in Equation 1—the entire process has no trainable new class parameters, which is the mathematical implementation of "register with a few samples."

**Open-set extension of D-ProtoNets** (Section 3.3). The $L_{FSL}$ used in this paper is specifically the dummy prototypical loss of D-ProtoNets [15]: an additional **learnable open-set prototype** is introduced, trained jointly with various class prototypes, turning the problem into an N+1 way classification, so that both closed-set and open-set query samples are pulled towards their respective prototypes. During inference, if the probability that the test sample belongs to the (N+1)-th class (open-set) $p_\theta(y = N+1 \mid x_t^q)$ exceeds a preset threshold, it is judged as open-set (non-keyword) rejection. The reason for choosing it as the in-domain loss is straightforward: the baseline splitGSC itself is designed for closed-set plus open-set evaluation; a single loss feeds both types of query samples, resulting in high training efficiency.

**AuxSL total loss** (Equation 2):

$$L_{AuxSL} = L_{FSL} + \lambda L_{SL}$$

$L_{FSL}$ is any few-shot learning loss on in-domain data (dummy prototype loss in this paper), $L_{SL}$ is the supervised loss on out-of-domain auxiliary data (standard cross-entropy in this paper), and $\lambda$ is the balancing parameter, fixed at 1.0 in experiments. The simplicity of the formula is deliberate—the "simple but effective" in the paper's title refers to here: all innovations lie in the structural decision of "in what form and through which path the auxiliary data enters the loss," rather than tricks in the loss function itself.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Fully Automatic Construction Pipeline of LibriWord

The complete process from LibriSpeech to LibriWord (Section 3.2, Table 1) is divided into four steps:

1. **Word-level alignment and segmentation.** LibriSpeech has about 1,000 hours of read English (16 kHz), totaling 4,806,675 sentence samples and 58,997 word types, with sentence duration distribution [0.0s, 11.1s], but only sentence-level transcriptions. Use Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA, citations [26][32], a trainable text-speech aligner based on Kaldi) for word-level forced alignment, cutting each sentence into single-word audio segments with word labels—this step transforms "unlabeled" into "machine-labeled without manual effort," which is the reason why the cost of the entire pipeline approaches zero.
2. **Head selection by word frequency.** Count word frequencies and retain the top 1,000 most frequent keywords.
3. **Similar word deduplication.** When one word partially overlaps with another (morphological variants like past tense, plural, negation), randomly keep one. Why: small models have limited capacity; near-duplicate classes waste representation budget and create ambiguous supervision (the model cannot distinguish whether it should learn the boundary between had and have); cutting variants allows each class to occupy non-overlapping phoneme regions, simplifying representation learning.
4. **Class-balanced sampling.** Take 300 samples for each keyword. The final LibriWord has 300,000 samples, 1,000 keywords, word length distribution [0.3s, 2.2s], with word-level labels (Table 1).

The "whys" behind two design decisions are worth emphasizing separately. **Why balanced**: The word distribution of LibriSpeech is extremely long-tailed ("the", "and" appear millions of times, many words appear only a few times); using it directly is equivalent to feeding the long tail to the model. The ablation in Figure 3 proves: using the AuxSL architecture and only changing the data construction method, the balanced curve is significantly higher than the unbalanced curve of the same scale, and also higher than the entire library LibriSpeech (4.8 million samples) which is 16 times larger—this is consistent with the phenomenon in ImageNet long-tail recognition literature [36][37]: balancing the number of samples per class during training helps build robust feature embeddings. **Why only 300k samples**: LibriWord is 16 times smaller than LibriSpeech, with significantly reduced collection burden and storage costs, and performance is actually better (Section 4.2)—for engineering teams that need to land, the answer to the question "how large should the auxiliary dataset be" is compressed to a very friendly scale.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: AuxSL Split Multi-Task Training

The details of the training recipe (Section 4.1) all serve "two paths running in parallel without stepping on each other": 500 episodes per epoch, each episode containing 5 closed-set classes plus 5 open-set classes, 5 support samples per class to build prototypes, and 5 query samples to calculate loss; the auxiliary path of the multi-task uses parallel sampling with batch size 64—i.e., the episode batch of metric learning and the word classification batch of cross-entropy flow synchronously, with the sample streams of the two losses not mixing into each other's vocabulary; $\lambda=1.0$ without fancy weighting; the auxiliary classifier is just a 1-FC layer, deliberately kept to the minimum. The optimizer is Adam with an initial learning rate of 0.001, halved every 20 epochs, for a total of 100 epochs.

Evaluation protocol: 1,000 test episodes, 1 or 5 support samples per class, 15 query samples (including open-set classes), reporting the average closed-set/open-set accuracy and AUROC and standard deviation of three random seeds. Reporting seed-level variance is a necessary integrity operation in FS-KWS few-shot evaluation—episode sampling itself introduces considerable evaluation noise.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Comparing item by item with the four most relevant routes, the differences all fall on "where the auxiliary information comes from and in what supervised form it enters the small model":

- **To Kao et al. [5]** (horizontal comparison of multiple SSL models in a two-stage framework): They accept the SSL paradigm itself, only picking the best model inside it; this paper's conclusion is a paradigm-level rejection—SSL fails overall on lightweight FS-KWS, and should be replaced by supervised learning after automatic annotation.
- **To Lee et al. [6]** (TTS synthetic auxiliary data): Synthetic data introduces synthetic domain differences; LibriWord uses real human voices, incurring only one domain difference ("read speech vs. command speech"), which is explicitly isolated by the split structure of AuxSL.
- **To Shin et al. [7]** (audio-text correspondence, open vocabulary): MFA appears in both papers, but with different purposes—[7] uses text as the alignment modality during inference, while this paper only uses MFA for offline data segmentation; training and inference remain pure audio single-modality, and edge devices do not need a text encoder.
- **To PreT-Big / KD / SSL-MTL** (same-table comparison in Table 2): These three are all routes of "moving large model knowledge to small models" or "forcing unsupervised signals," all failing or having marginal gains under the 321k parameter setting; AuxSL is the only solution that does not rely on any large model or any unsupervised objective.
- **To ALL** (naive mixing): Same data, same model, only differing in "single loss mixed training" vs. "split multi-task," five-shot scores change from 92.2%/86.6% to 95.6%/95.2% (Table 2)—this set of controls is the most convincing evidence in the paper, showing that performance differences cannot be attributed to the data itself, but to the injection method.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

- **In-domain baseline splitGSC** (Section 4.1): Based on Google Speech Commands [12], the standard few-shot baseline [15], with 15, 10, and 10 keywords in the training/validation/test sets, with sample sizes of 24,400, 4,007, and 4,482 respectively; all sets contain a silence class; data augmentation uses GSC official background noise, added with probability 0.8. The test vocabulary is completely invisible to the training set, which is the evaluation premise for "few-shot generalization."
- **Out-of-domain auxiliary data** (Table 1): The entire LibriSpeech library has 4,806,675 samples, 58,997 word types, duration [0.0s, 11.1s]; the processed LibriWord has 300,000 samples, 1,000 words, 300 samples per word, duration [0.3s, 2.2s], with word-level labels automatically generated by MFA. The ablation in Figure 3 additionally constructs six scales of dataset variants: 50,000/100,000/200,000/300,000/500,000/4,806,675.
- **Backbone networks and features**: BC-ResNet8 (321k parameters) is the main force, inputting 40-dimensional log Mel spectrograms, window length 30 ms, frame shift 10 ms; Res12 (8M, from TADAM [35]) also inputs log Mel; DS-ResNet18 (72k) inputs 40-dimensional MFCC. Scale experiments (Figure 4) cover four scales of BC-ResNet1 (9.2k)/3/6/8.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Closed-set Accuracy (Acc.)**: When the registered words and test words are in the same vocabulary, the accuracy of classifying according to the nearest prototype in Equation 1, measuring the discriminativeness of the embedding space.
- **Open-set AUROC**: Open-set detection rate, threshold-independent. The reason for choosing it lies in real product semantics—after user-defined wake words are deployed, the device hears any speech stream, and the vast majority of audio is not registered words; the system must reject them; and the choice of rejection threshold depends on the prior trade-off between false alarms and missed detections; different papers tuning thresholds themselves would make comparisons unfair; AUROC integrates out the threshold, reflecting only the quality of open-set discrimination itself. The separate reporting of closed-set Acc and open-set AUROC exactly exposes the pathological state of the ALL method (closed-set surges, open-set stagnates).
- **One-shot and five-shot two tiers**: Corresponding to user registration cost tiers of "saying it once" and "saying it a few times"; one-shot better exposes the robustness of the embedding space to intra-class variance (accidental nature of the same pronunciation).

Relative to the baseline, five-shot closed-set from 82.0 to 95.6, open-set from 82.9 to 95.2, the paper expresses this as "approximately 16% relative improvement in both closed-set and open-set under the five-shot setting" (Figure 1(d); calculated by absolute value, closed-set +13.6 points i.e., relative +16.6%, open-set +12.3 points i.e., relative +14.8%); the absolute improvement of one-shot closed-set from 65.8 to 87.1 reaches 21.3 points. t-SNE visualization (Figure 1) shows that the embedding cluster spacing of unseen keywords in AuxSL is significantly larger than that of the baseline, SSL, and ALL, corroborating the metrics.

**Cross-backbone validation** (Table 3): The same training strategy comparison is moved to Res12 (8M) and DS-ResNet18 (72k). AuxSL on Res12 is 95.6 (0.3) / 94.1 (0.6), one-shot 85.5 (0.2) / 84.3 (1.4); on DS-ResNet18 is 90.8 (0.3) / 89.2 (0.9), one-shot 77.4 (1.0) / 80.2 (1.8), all significantly exceeding ALL (Res12 93.1/86.9; DS-ResNet18 88.0/82.1) and SSL/KD groups. The improvements of competing methods on these backbones are also marginal (e.g., PreT-SimCLR drops to 59.0 (2.7) on DS-ResNet18, 10 points lower than its baseline of 69.2 (2.9)), proving that "SSL fails, AuxSL works" is not a special case of BC-ResNet8.

**Model scale scaling** (Figure 4): The performance of all methods increases with the increase of the feature extractor, but the SSL series is lower than the baseline on the smallest BC-ResNet1 (9.2k)—small model plus SSL is negative gain; the most counterintuitive finding is that **AuxSL added to the smallest BC-ResNet1 achieves scores exceeding all other method combinations including BC-ResNet8**. The authors interpret this as direct evidence that AuxSL can still learn discriminative representations under extreme low-resource settings: when the model is so small that even the baseline cannot run, the information density provided by auxiliary supervision becomes the decisive factor.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have a separate "ablation table," but three sets of control variable experiments constitute a complete ablation chain:

1. **Data construction ablation (Figure 3)**: Fix the AuxSL architecture, only change the auxiliary data construction—six scales from 50k to 4.8M × balanced/unbalanced two constructions. Two conclusions: First, the balanced curve is significantly higher than the unbalanced curve of the same scale overall; the five-shot accuracy of the balanced curve falls in the range of about 95.3-95.7%, while the unbalanced curve and related curves are only about 86.9-87.5% (Figure 3 data annotation); Second, expanding the data from 300k to the entire library 4.8M (16 times), the performance under unbalanced construction is actually worse than the 300k balanced version—data scale cannot compensate for distribution skew, a phenomenon corroborated by ImageNet long-tail recognition research [36][37]. This ablation also answers "why not directly use the entire LibriSpeech library."
2. **Learning scheme ablation (embedded in Table 2)**: Fix LibriWord (supervised series) or LibriSpeech (self-supervised series), horizontally compare four injection methods: PreT/MTL/ALL/AuxSL. All injection methods of the SSL series fail (as mentioned above); within the supervised series, PreT (two-stage) is better than the baseline but loses multi-task synergy, ALL (single loss mixed training) is strong in closed-set but weak in open-set, AuxSL (split multi-task) is comprehensively optimal—proving that **the injection structure of the supervised signal itself contributes to the gap of about 3.4 closed-set points and 8.6 open-set points between ALL and AuxSL**.
3. **Backbone and scale ablation (Table 3, Figure 4)**: Conclusions as described in the previous section—the effectiveness of the method and the conclusion of "SSL failure" hold consistently across the 9.2k to 8M parameter range and three architecture families (BC-ResNet, Res12, DS-ResNet), and the smaller the model, the more prominent the relative advantage of AuxSL.

It is worth pointing out the restraint in the ablation design: the experiment in Figure 3 explicitly states "only using the final MTL architecture, separately examining the impact of LibriWord construction method," i.e., the architecture and data variables are decoupled in the ablation, and their respective effectiveness is independently verified—this is a cleanliness that many similar papers cannot achieve.

## Main Contributions

1. **LibriWord Dataset**: A fully automatic (MFA word-level alignment + word frequency truncation + morphological variant deduplication + class-balanced sampling) keyword-level data construction pipeline, transforming about 1,000 hours of reading speech into a balanced dataset of 1,000 words × 300 samples, totaling 300,000 samples (Table 1), zero manual annotation, volume only one-sixteenth of the source corpus, and ablation proves that this "small and balanced" version is better than the "large and long-tailed" original library (Figure 3).
2. **Systematic Empirical Evidence of SSL Failure on Lightweight FS-KWS**: Horizontally comparing SimCLR, BYOL, wav2vec/Hubert feature distillation, and multi-task combinations (Table 2, Table 3, Figure 4) under the same baseline, same backbone, and equal tuning investment, landing the literature conclusion that "SSL only benefits large models" onto the specific battlefield of KWS small models, correcting the intuition that "using SSL is always correct."
3. **AuxSL Multi-Task Framework**: The split supervision structure of $L_{AuxSL} = L_{FSL} + \lambda L_{SL}$ (Equation 2), using a discardable 1-FC auxiliary classifier to isolate the pollution of out-of-domain gradients on in-domain prototype geometry, with zero additional overhead during inference, achieving consistent and largest improvements across all backbones and all model scales (five-shot 95.6%/95.2, Table 2).
4. **New Conclusions under Extreme Low Resources**: AuxSL + BC-ResNet1 (9.2k parameters) exceeds the combination of other methods plus the largest model BC-ResNet8 (Figure 4), providing a more optimistic boundary for "how small a model can still do user-defined wake words" than before.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Domain difference is only isolated, not eliminated.** The split structure of AuxSL prevents out-of-domain gradients from rewriting in-domain geometry, but the distribution difference between reading speech and command speech (prosody, channel, speaking style, function-word-dominated word distribution) still exists; the components in the auxiliary signal that conflict with the target domain are absorbed by the classification head rather than transformed. The authors themselves acknowledge this in the conclusion, listing domain adaptation modules like RFN [39] and DSBN [40] as unexplored directions.
- **Implicit dependence on text transcriptions.** MFA forced alignment requires each sentence to have a transcription text—LibriSpeech happens to have it, but when switching to truly "wild" auxiliary corpora (podcasts, video audio tracks), transcription quality will become a new bottleneck; alignment errors will directly become label noise entering the CE loss. The paper does not report MFA's alignment error rate or sensitivity to noisy labels.
- **Generalizability of vocabulary and sampling hyperparameters is unverified.** 1,000 words, 300 samples per word is a single-point choice: Figure 3 changed scale and balance, but did not change the two orthogonal dimensions of vocabulary size (how many words) and samples per entry (how many per word), so it is impossible to judge whether 1,000/300 is a sweet spot or suboptimal; similarly, $\lambda$ is fixed at 1.0 and the paper does not report any sensitivity analysis, making it impossible to judge whether "split weights do not need tuning" is robustness or coincidence.
- **Information cost of balanced sampling.** Cutting off 4.5 million samples to balance discards the prior information of word frequency as a whole; in long-tail literature, "resampling + reweighting" usually preserves more information than "truncation-style balancing"; the paper chose the simplest scheme but did not compare.
- **Open-set deployment still requires threshold definition.** AUROC is a threshold-free metric, but product deployment must select a decision threshold for $p(y=N+1)$; the actual trade-off of false alarm rate/missed detection rate is not touched by the paper.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Single baseline, single language.** All conclusions are built on splitGSC (English, GSC recording conditions); MLS is named as a potential auxiliary source in the introduction but is completely absent from experiments; there is no evidence for multi-language scenarios (whether English reading corpus can assist Chinese wake words).
- **Lack of in-field numerical comparison with similar auxiliary data methods.** Lee et al. [6]'s TTS assistance and Shin et al. [7]'s audio-text alignment are compared in related work and positioning, but their reproduced numbers are not in Table 2; "reading speech is better than synthetic speech" is currently only an argument, not a measurement.
- **Absence of engineering-side metrics.** The paper reports parameter counts (9.2k-8M), but training overhead (per-step cost of episode plus CE dual flow, total training time), inference latency, and memory usage are not reported; "zero additional inference cost" holds structurally, but lacks quantitative support.
- **Slight discrepancies between Figure 1 and Table 2** (Figure 1(b) annotates 82.8/84.2, while the corresponding MTL-SimCLR in Table 2 behaves as 83.0/83.1), suggesting that the variance between runs for individual comparison points is of the same order as the reported standard deviations, and marginal conclusions (e.g., differences within one or two points in the SSL series) should be interpreted with caution.
- **Evaluation is still static episode sampling**, without simulating the real user registration process (the impact of different registration pronunciation quality and registration environment noise on prototype stability), leaving one layer away from product semantics.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

- **Introduction of domain adaptation modules**: RFN (Relaxed Instance Frequency Normalization) [39] and DSBN (Domain-Specific Batch Normalization) [40], pointed out by the authors, are used to explicitly align the reading domain and command domain at the feature level, potentially further suppressing the remaining domain difference; this is the most explicit interface left by the paper itself.
- **Multi-language and more auxiliary sources**: Translate the pipeline to MLS (Multilingual Large-Scale reading corpus) to verify cross-language effectiveness; further attempt auxiliary domains closer to spoken language such as podcasts and audio news to shorten the domain difference.
- **Smarter balancing strategies**: Replace hard truncation with class-balanced resampling, logit adjustment, and decoupled representation and classifier from long-tail learning [36][37], obtaining balancing benefits without losing data.
- **Hybrids with large model supervision**: PreT-Big (Hubert 90.4) and AuxSL (95.6) each have their strengths; combining Hubert features as a third distillation loss for AuxSL (isolated within the split structure) is a natural combination experiment, which the paper did not attempt.
- **Automatic calibration of open-set thresholds**: Combine the distribution of enrollment samples to adaptively set the rejection threshold, converting the AUROC advantage into a deployable false alarm/missed detection operating point.
- **Registration-side enhancement**: The embedding space shaped by auxiliary data ultimately serves "building prototypes with a few samples"; performing speech enhancement or prototype variance modeling on enrollment samples may be the source of the next performance step for one-shot.
