## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Wake Word Spotting (WWS) is a special case of Keyword Spotting (KWS), defined as the task of detecting one or a predefined set of wake words within a continuous streaming audio signal. With the rapid proliferation of hands-free devices such as smartphones and voice assistants, WWS has become the first entry point in the voice interaction chain: devices must listen continuously, waking up subsequent speech recognition and dialogue services only after the user utters the wake word. This persistent attribute determines two engineering characteristics of WWS. First, it does not face clean, near-field speech from a laboratory, but rather the full spectrum of acoustic conditions in real home environments; once deployed, the device must handle any sound it encounters. Second, both types of errors directly harm user experience: a miss means the user shouts the wake word but the device fails to wake up, while a false alarm means the device is randomly awakened by TV sounds, family conversations, or screams from next door. Both types of errors rapidly erode user trust in voice interaction.

The paper immediately identifies this pain point: Deep Neural Network (DNN)-based WWS methods (DNN, CNN, Temporal Convolutional Networks TCN, Transformer) perform well in clean and near-field scenarios but exhibit a significantly higher probability of false alarms in complex acoustic environments. These complex acoustic environments are specifically categorized into three types: background noise (cheering, TV, screaming), reverberation, and multi-speaker dialogue scenarios with significant speech overlap. This set of scenario settings is not generic; it directly corresponds to the task setup of Track 1 of the MISP Challenge 2021 (the first Multimodal Information Speech Processing Challenge), which involves detecting the wake word "Xiao T" spoken by participating speakers in a far-field smart home scenario. Far-field implies low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), heavy reverberation, and reliance on multi-channel signals; smart home implies the coexistence of TV noise, family conversations, and sudden noises. Among similar public tasks, MISP 2021 is one of the testbeds closest to real living room deployment conditions.

The nature of this paper is a post-challenge technical deep-dive: The DKU team achieved second place in Track 1 of the challenge. This paper builds upon their参赛 system (Cheng et al., ICASSP 2022, Reference [13]) to advance three things: replacing the pure 3D CNN backbone with a 3D and 2D hybrid backbone, introducing the parameter-free attention module SimAM, and systematically exploring combinations of data augmentation and multimodal fusion strategies. Ultimately, they reduced the WWS score on the test set from 7.1% to 5.59%, a relative improvement of 21%, surpassing the first-place system's 5.8% (Table 4), becoming the new SOTA on this dataset. That is to say, the value of this paper does not lie in proposing a completely new paradigm, but in treating a competition system as an experimental platform to deconstruct the true contribution of each design choice item by item. This post-challenge deep-dive methodology itself is of no less reference value to engineering teams than its conclusions.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper’s梳理 of the shortcomings of existing methods can be summarized into four points, each precisely corresponding to an improvement action in this paper. This correspondence between shortcomings and actions is noteworthy.

First, audio-only methods exhibit structural fragility in complex acoustic scenarios. All discriminative information in pure audio methods comes from the acoustic signal itself. When noise, reverberation, and speech overlap drown out the acoustic features of the wake word, the model has no auxiliary information to rely on, making an increase in the false alarm rate inevitable. Although there is a wealth of multimodal work in the Audio-Visual Speech Recognition (AVSR) field—such as using the Conformer architecture for end-to-end AVSR, or the V-CAFE module which uses visual context to drive audio feature enhancement—and Momeni et al. verified in BMVC 2020 that simple CNN-based audio-visual KWS performs well on noisy audio, mainstream work in the WWS field still ignores visual information. Human perception provides the same hint: in noisy environments, people stare at the speaker's lips to assist in understanding semantics, because the visual channel is completely unaffected by acoustic noise. The visible phonetic information carried by lip movements is naturally complementary to acoustic information under noisy conditions.

Second, existing attention modules require extra parameters. The SE module in the channel dimension (already used in WWS) learns one-dimensional channel weights, while CBAM calculates two-dimensional and one-dimensional attention weights separately. These modules all require inserting learnable parameters into the network. For tasks like WWS that emphasize lightweight, persistent operation, any extra parameters increase the training and deployment burden. More importantly, parameterized attention requires re-learning the weight distribution for each new modality and dataset, leading to high migration costs.

Third, the official baseline's fusion strategy is too naive. The MISP official baseline simply performs a weighted sum of the posterior probabilities of the two unimodal systems (Equation 5). However, the posterior probability distributions output by the audio and video systems differ significantly—the audio is strong, the video is weak, and their confidence scales are incomparable. A 0.7 audio posterior and a 0.7 video posterior are statistically completely different things; simple weighting cannot handle this distribution mismatch.

Fourth, there is a lack of systematic ablation for data augmentation combinations. Training for far-field WWS relies heavily on augmentation (adding noise and reverberation, beamforming, speed perturbation, spectral masking, etc.), but which augmentations are mutually beneficial, which cancel each other out, and what the non-additivity of combinations looks like, previous work has not provided systematic ablation evidence. Engineering practice basically relies on intuition.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, the key challenges this paper aims to solve can be divided into three layers. The first layer is unimodal robustness: how to make both the audio and video paths sufficiently strong under far-field noise conditions. This is the ceiling for fusion gains; fusion cannot create information out of thin air but can only reorganize the information extracted by each path. If unimodal performance is weak, the fusion upper bound is low. The second layer is asymmetric fusion: the WWS score of the audio unimodal on the test set is about 11%, while the video unimodal is about 19%. The performance disparity is huge, and the posterior distributions differ. How to design a fusion mechanism that allows the weak modality to truly contribute complementary information rather than dragging down performance is the most valuable part of this paper's methodology. The third layer is training strategy: given a training set of approximately 52,000 samples with a positive-to-negative sample ratio of about 9:1, how to reduce far-field generalization error through combinations of data augmentation. The paper provides answers to these three layers respectively: a hybrid 3D/2D backbone with SimAM attention, HMA hierarchical feature fusion, and ablation of combinations of seven audio augmentation methods.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall framework is shown in Fig. 1: the system consists of two symmetric unimodal paths (audio and video) plus a fusion module. Each path outputs two things: the wake word posterior probability (for score-level and cascaded fusion) and hierarchical latent features (for neural fusion). This design of one set of features for multiple fusion strategies allows for a fair comparison of the three fusion strategies on the exact same unimodal systems. Therefore, the subsequent ablation conclusions regarding fusion mechanisms (cascaded is worst, HMA is best) can be cleanly attributed to the fusion mechanism itself, without confounding factors from differences in the unimodal systems.

The primary design of the unimodal paths is input unification. The video input is a raw image sequence with shape $(T, H, W, 3)$, where $T$ is the number of frames, $H$ and $W$ are image height and width, and 3 is the RGB channel. The audio input is specially constructed: first, 2D acoustic features (such as FBank) are extracted from the 1D waveform, with shape $(T', D)$; then, a $(D, D)$ sliding window slices along the time axis with a step size of $T/T'$, reshaping the audio features into a pseudo-video with shape $(T, D, D, 1)$. There are two layers of motivation for this. First, the frame rates of audio and video differ (audio feature frame shift is 10 ms, i.e., about 100 frames per second, corresponding to a roughly 4:1 relationship between $T'=256$ and $T=64$). The sliding window slicing effectively aligns the temporal resolution of the audio features to the video frame rate, making the time dimensions of the two paths comparable. Second, after unifying the input shapes, the two paths can reuse the same network architecture. The subsequent hierarchical feature fusion HMA can then concatenate the embedding vectors of the two modalities at the same layer levels. If the two paths have different structures, feature-level fusion loses its alignment anchor. Additionally, each $(D, D)$ window contains a local time-frequency image, preserving short-term local spatial information, which directly explains the phenomenon in the subsequent experiments where 3D convolution outperforms 2D convolution.

The preprocessing chain for the visual path is also worth expanding: RetinaFace is first used for face detection to output facial keypoints, then ArcFace face recognition is used to select the target speaker from multiple faces in the far-field video, and finally, the lip region is cropped according to the keypoints. Here, the role of ArcFace is not identity authentication, but cross-modal speaker association. Far-field scenes often contain multiple people; it is necessary to attribute lip movements to the person actually speaking. Otherwise, the visual path learns false associations such as "anyone in the frame moving their mouth," leading to massive false alarms in multi-speaker overlapping dialogue scenarios (which is the core difficulty of MISP). The cropped lip region video is uniformly scaled to a resolution of 112×112 and sampled at 64 frames, resulting in a video sample shape of $(64, 112, 112, 3)$. On the audio side, 80-dimensional log-Mel filter bank features (FBank) are extracted with a 25 ms window length and 10 ms frame shift, resulting in an audio feature shape of $(256, 80)$. Then, an $(80, 80)$ sliding window with a step size of 4 slices the features to form an audio sample of shape $(64, 80, 80, 1)$.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The most mathematically complete part of this paper is the derivation of the energy function for the SimAM attention module (Equations 1 to 4).

SimAM starts from the phenomenon of spatial inhibition in neuroscience: neurons with the most information usually exhibit discharge patterns significantly different from surrounding neurons. Translating this intuition into an optimizable form involves defining an energy function for each target neuron in the feature map (Equation 1): within a single channel of a 3D feature map $X \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$, let the output of the target neuron be $t$, and the outputs of the remaining $M-1$ neurons ($M=H \times W$) in the same channel be $x_i$. The energy function is $e_t(w_t, b_t, y, x_i) = (y_t - \hat{t})^2 + \frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}(y_o - \hat{x}_i)^2$, where $\hat{t} = w_t t + b_t$ and $\hat{x}_i = w_t x_i + b_t$ are linear transformations. The geometric meaning of this equation is: to find a set of $(w_t, b_t)$ such that the output of the transformed target neuron approximates the label $y_t$, and the outputs of the remaining neurons approximate the label $y_o$. The minimum energy is achieved when $\hat{t}=y_t$ and $\hat{x}_i=y_o$; since spatial inhibition requires the target neuron to be more prominent than its surroundings, it should be that $y_t$ is greater than $y_o$. To simplify the solution, the paper takes binary labels $y_t=1$ and $y_o=-1$, and adds an L2 regularization term to $w_t$ (Equation 2). Directly optimizing Equation 2 is computationally expensive, but this problem has a fast closed-form solution for $w_t$ and $b_t$. Substituting back yields the analytical form of the minimum energy (Equation 3): $e_t^* = \frac{4(\hat{\sigma}^2+\lambda)}{(t-\hat{\mu})^2 + 2\hat{\sigma}^2 + 2\lambda}$, where $\hat{\mu}$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2$ are the mean and variance of all neurons in that channel. The final SimAM module is defined as $e = \sigma(1/E) \odot X$ (Equation 4), where $E$ organizes all $e_t^*$ into a tensor with the same shape as the feature map along the channel and spatial dimensions, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function, and $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication.

This closed-form solution is worth reading item by item, as it explains all behaviors of SimAM. The term $(t-\hat{\mu})^2$ measures the degree to which the target neuron deviates from the channel mean, and the term $\hat{\sigma}^2$ measures the dispersion of the surroundings: the more a neuron deviates from the mean and the more uniform the surroundings are, the smaller its energy $e_t^*$, the greater its importance $1/e_t^*$, and the higher the attention weight it receives. In other words, SimAM automatically amplifies neurons that stand out in a uniform background. For the WWS task, this corresponds exactly to the transient discriminative regions of the wake word on the time-frequency map (short-term energy mutations in specific frequency bands) and frames with vigorous lip opening and closing in the video. These positions are precisely where neurons with discharge patterns significantly different from the surroundings are clustered. For the 4D feature map $(C, T, H, W)$ output by the 3D CNN, the energy is calculated jointly along the time and spatial dimensions, with $M=T \times H \times W$. The entire module has no learnable parameters ($\lambda$ is fixed at 0.001, serving to prevent denominator blow-up when variance approaches zero). This is the essential difference between it and SE, CBAM: attention weights are determined instantly by the statistics of the current feature map, introducing no training degrees of freedom.

Regarding the training objective, the system uses weighted binary cross-entropy loss, with a negative-to-positive sample weight ratio of 5:1, to counteract the approximately 9:1 positive-to-negative sample imbalance in the training set. If weighting is not applied, the loss surface will be dominated by negative samples, and the model will prioritize learning the trivial solution of predicting negative for any input. On imbalanced data, the loss for this solution is already low, but the False Rejection Rate (FRR) would be disastrous.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: 3D/2D Hybrid Convolution Backbone

This paper splits the previous 3D-ResNet34 into cascaded 3D-ResNet18 and 2D-ResNet18. The design basis comes from classic analysis in the action recognition field (Tran et al., CVPR 2018, Reference [18]): low-level 3D convolutions focus on short-term spatial modeling, while high-level 2D convolutions are more conducive to extracting semantic and temporal information. Applied to the WWS task, this division of labor corresponds very naturally: low-level input features (local time-frequency textures, lip appearance details) require fine-grained spatiotemporal filters; while high-level features need to organize local pattern sequences into a semantic judgment of whether it is a wake word. 2D convolutions can undertake this task by sliding with a large receptive field along the time axis. There is a third engineering benefit: 3D convolution kernels are cubes, and the parameter count grows with the cube of the kernel size. Replacing high-level layers with 2D convolutions significantly reduces the parameter count—the paper explicitly states that converting 3D to 2D can reduce model parameters, but does not report the specific amount of reduction. For structural connection, after the feature map passes through 3D-ResNet18, average pooling is performed along the spatial axis, changing the shape to $(C, T)$, which is then treated as a single-channel latent image $(1, T, C)$ and fed into 2D-ResNet18. Both audio and video inputs follow this hybrid backbone, keeping the two paths isomorphic.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Introducing Parameter-Free Attention SimAM to WWS

The paper migrates SimAM from the speaker verification domain (where two ASV works have already adopted it) to WWS. The rationality of this migration lies in the similarity of signal structures between the two tasks: both involve discriminative sparse targets embedded in long-term backgrounds (the wake word occupies only a small portion of frames in a segment, and the target speaker's voiceprint information occupies only a specific subspace of features). The mechanism of parameter-free 3D attention to amplify a few unique neurons is isomorphic in these two tasks. The performance of the audio path conforms to expectations: after adding SimAM to 3D-ResNet18+2D-ResNet18, the WWS on the test set dropped from 11.24% to 11.03% (A3 vs A4 in Table 2), and on the development set from 9.71% to 9.54%. This is a stable, small gain exchanged for zero parameters, accumulating over time. The performance of the video path is more intriguing (Table 3): V2 adding SimAM yields V4, where the development set FRR drops from 9.78% to 6.89%, at the cost of FAR worsening from 6.64% to 9.09%; the overall test set score of 22.93% vs 24.03% is still an improvement. This indicates that the role of parameter-free attention on the weak modality is to rebalance the error structure rather than simply compress the total error. It makes the model more sensitive to true wake words while relaxing constraints on some negative samples. The paper does not elaborate on this asymmetric gain phenomenon, but it is valuable for understanding the true behavior of attention modules.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Combination Ablation of Seven Data Augmentations

Seven augmentations are examined on the audio side: offline noise and reverberation addition (NR), MVDR beamforming enhancement (BE), negative sample sub-segmentation (NS), volume perturbation (VP), speed perturbation (SP), slight cropping (TS), and SpecAugment (SA). The motivations for two of these are worth expanding separately. NR uses the pyroomacoustic tool to generate room impulse responses, simulating the official near-field data into mid-to-far-field data, and mixing it with the official noise library at random SNRs from -15 to 15 dB. Essentially, this bridges the distribution gap between the training set (mixed near/mid/far) and the test set (far-field only) using indoor acoustic simulation. NS targets a very specific risk: the duration distribution of positive and negative samples differs greatly, and the model might guess the label with low accuracy based solely on audio length. Cutting negative samples into short segments destroys this shortcut. This insight comes from adversarial thinking about task shortcuts (shortcuts), making it the most task-specific augmentation in the entire set. General augmentation libraries will not think of task-specific vulnerabilities like negative sample duration leaking labels. BE incorporates the enhanced audio after multi-channel signals undergo MVDR beamforming into the training set, both expanding the data volume and allowing the model to see signal forms processed by front-end enhancement, aligning with the real deployment pipeline. Video-side augmentations follow the previous work, including speed perturbation, per-frame rotation, horizontal flipping, frame-level cropping, color jittering, and grayscale conversion.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Three Fusion Strategies and HMA Hierarchical Modality Aggregation

The paper systematically compares three fusion strategies. Score-level fusion (Equation 5) $P_{av} = \alpha \times P_a + \beta \times P_v$ is the approach of the official baseline, where this paper takes $\alpha=\beta=0.5$. Cascaded fusion leverages the performance difference between the two systems: the weaker video system first screens candidates with a low threshold $th_l=0.1$, and then the stronger audio system makes the final decision with a high threshold $th_h=0.4$. The intuition is that the weak system performs coarse screening and the strong system performs fine judgment. Neural fusion adopts the HMA (Hierarchical Modality Aggregation) proposed in the previous work: global average pooling is performed on the latent feature maps of residual blocks at each layer of each unimodal network to obtain hierarchical embeddings; embeddings of the same level from the two modalities are concatenated to form multimodal embeddings $c_l$ ($l$ being the level); and then the HMA network aggregates all levels of $c_l$ to output the final decision. The essential difference between HMA and the other two lies in the information level: score-level and cascaded fusion only exchange the final posterior, a single scalar, while the intermediate representations of the visual path are compressed away before fusion occurs. HMA allows sound features and lip movement features at each level to dialogue directly, aligning discriminative information at the representation level. The paper's experiments will prove that this representation-level path is the key to extracting the value of the weak modality.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared with the previous competition system [13], this paper has three differences: the backbone changes from pure 3D CNN to 3D/2D hybrid, the parameter-free attention SimAM is added, and the fusion side expands from a single scheme to a three-strategy systematic comparison. Compared with the official baseline [12], the differences cover the entire chain of unimodal backbone, attention, augmentation, and fusion, reducing the WWS on the test set from 25.1% to 5.59% (Table 4). Compared with contemporaneous Transformer end-to-end schemes (Xu et al. [14]), this paper adheres to the modular route of convolution backbone plus explicit fusion module, leading the test set WWS by about 3.5 percentage points (5.59% vs 9.1%). Compared with parameterized attention like SE and CBAM, SimAM has zero parameters. Compared with general AVSR schemes, this paper retains WWS-specific task customizations: negative sample sub-segmentation to counteract duration shortcuts, weighted BCE to counteract positive-negative imbalance, lip region cropping plus ArcFace speaker association for the visual front-end, and lip-reading database pre-training to supplement visual representation.

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

The experiment uses the MISP Challenge 2021 dataset, with the task goal of detecting the wake word "Xiao T" spoken by participating speakers in a far-field smart home scenario. The data composition is: a training set of 47k+ negative samples and 5k+ positive samples (positive-to-negative ratio approx. 9:1), covering near, mid, and far field distances; a development set of 2k+ negative samples and 600+ positive samples, also spanning field distances; and a test set of 8k+ samples. Labels were not provided during the competition for the test set, and it contained only far-field data. After the competition, the authors obtained labels from the MISP committee for fair comparison with other teams. The fact that the test set contains only far-field data is key to interpreting all experimental results: all final comparisons occur at the most difficult field distance tier, and this also explains the distribution difference between the development and test sets. For the visual side, pre-training uses the CAS-VSR-W1k (LRW-1000) lip-reading database—a large-scale natural scene lip-reading benchmark. The backbone network is pre-trained on it and then fine-tuned on MISP. Training configuration: single RTX 3090 GPU, batch size 64, Adam optimizer. Learning rate for unimodal models is 0.001, and for the HMA fusion model is 0.0001. SimAM hyperparameter $\lambda=0.001$. Cascaded fusion thresholds are $th_l=0.1$ and $th_h=0.4$. For fusion experiments, the optimal models of the two modalities are combined (Audio A4 + Video V5) and then the three fusion strategies are compared.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metrics follow the official challenge definitions (Equations 6, 7): FRR equals the number of missed detection samples divided by the total number of samples containing the wake word; FAR equals the number of false alarm samples divided by the total number of samples not containing the wake word; the final WWS score equals the sum of FRR and FAR. Choosing the sum of two error rates rather than a single error rate is behind the bidirectional cost symmetry of the wake word task: a miss directly prevents the user from waking the device, while a false alarm causes the device to wake up randomly; the harm to user experience from both is of the same magnitude. And it is not difficult to push any single error rate to zero (predicting all positive leads to high FAR, predicting all negative leads to high FRR). The real difficulty is finding the balance point between the two. The WWS score compresses this bi-objective balance problem into a single-objective ranking metric, which is convenient for horizontal comparison between teams and naturally penalizes unbalanced systems. This is reflected very specifically in the comparison between score-level fusion and HMA in this paper.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Table 4 provides a panoramic comparison of audio-visual systems (all far-field). Test set WWS scores: Official Baseline 25.1%, Transformer End-to-End Scheme (Xu et al.) 9.1%, Previous Second-Place System (Cheng et al.) 7.1%, Competition First Place 5.8%, This Paper's Score-Level Fusion 6.36%, This Paper's Cascaded Fusion 10.79%, This Paper's HMA 5.59%. The HMA system finally achieved FRR 2.15% and FAR 3.44%, with the two error rates being unusually balanced; score-level fusion had FRR 1.54% and FAR 4.82%, a total score of 6.36% and obvious imbalance; cascaded fusion had FRR as high as 7.29% and FAR 3.5%, with error quality severely biased towards misses. The 21% relative improvement over the previous work's 7.1% comes from this, simultaneously surpassing the first place's 5.8% to become the new SOTA. Additionally, the final model is obtained by averaging the top-3 models with the lowest loss on the development set, which is a common method to exchange model averaging for stability and generalization.

Two groups of comparisons are worth digging into. The first group is the ranking reversal between the development and test sets: on the development set, score-level fusion (5.29%) is better than HMA (5.59%), but on the test set, HMA overtakes (5.59% vs 6.36%). The authors selected models based on development set loss; HMA generalizes more stably on the more difficult test set. The actual reason is that feature-level fusion has a naturally higher tolerance for score distribution mismatch than posterior weighting. This is the experimental echo of the judgment in the motivation section that the posterior distributions of the two modalities differ greatly, and it is also the most powerful set of evidence in the entire paper. The second group is the failure of cascaded fusion: the paper's explanation is that the score distributions of the two modalities are inconsistent, leading to limited screening effect of the video level, and the two thresholds require fine-tuning. More bluntly, the video system's low-threshold coarse screening intended to cheaply filter out obvious negative samples, but the two posterior scales are incomparable—the video's 0.1 and the audio's 0.4 are not markings on the same ruler. It fails to filter out many negative samples, and instead, the audio side's high threshold rejects many true wake words. Thus, the cost of narrowing FAR to 3.5% is FRR skyrocketing to 7.29%. This negative result has warning value for all teams wanting to do cascaded fusion: the prerequisite for cascaded fusion is that the scores of the first level have predictive power for the decision of the second level. When there is distribution mismatch, this prerequisite does not hold.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Data augmentation ablation (Table 1, based on Audio 2D-ResNet34, far-field development and test sets) is the table with the highest information density in the entire paper. Reading row by row: D1 with no augmentation has a test set WWS of 21.36%; D2 adding NR and BE drops to 20.01%, attributed by the paper to increased training sample volume (simulating mid-to-far field plus beamforming enhancement is equivalent to expanding far-field corpus); D3 adding NS on top is 20.2%, and D4 adding VP on top is 20.54%—both NS and VP individually cause slight degradation, and VP pushes the test set FAR up to 7.42%; switching to adding SP, D5 drops significantly to 16.25%, D6 adding TS is 17.14%, and D7 adding SA is 17.2% (its development set 12.57% is acceptable, but the test set FAR worsens to 10.15%, indicating that SpecAugment alone increases false alarms on the test set); D8 with all six fully open achieves the optimum, with a test set score of 11.69%, a relative drop of about 45% compared to the no-augmentation D1; D9 removing NS is worse than D8 on both the development set (10.51% vs 10.21%) and test set (12.3% vs 11.69%), indicating that NS indeed has an auxiliary role when combined with other augmentations. The paper's final conclusion: SP, TS, and SA contribute the most; VP and NS are harmful individually, but NS is beneficial in combination. The optimal combination D8 achieves 11.69% on the test set. The engineering value of this table lies in demonstrating the non-additivity of augmentation effects: VP, an augmentation that helps in no combination, is directly removed; NS, which is harmful individually, is retained in combination. If one only does naive ablation by adding and subtracting items one by one, one will never see such interaction effects.

Backbone ablation is divided into audio and video sides. Audio side (Table 2): 2D-ResNet34 (A1) test set 11.69%, 3D-ResNet34 (A2) 11.34%, 3D-18+2D-18 hybrid (A3) 11.24%, hybrid plus SimAM (A4) 11.03%. A4 is the optimal audio path and introduces no extra parameters. The paper's explanation for 3D being better than 2D is short-term temporal feature modeling—after audio input is reshaped to $(T, D, D, 1)$, each window is a local time-frequency image, and 3D convolution of the window sequence along the time axis captures short-term temporal dynamics; 2D convolution treats each window as an independent image, losing the temporal evolution information between windows. Video side (Table 3): 3D-ResNet34 (V1) test set 27.54%, 3D-18+2D-18 (V2) 24.03%, V2 plus lip-reading pre-training (V3) 21.34%, V2 plus SimAM (V4) 22.93%, V5 with both added 19.13%. This table provides two pieces of information. First, the degradation from development to test set on the visual side is much more severe than on the audio side (V1 worsens from 16.85% to 27.54%, while audio side A2 only goes from 10.05% to 11.34%). This confirms that far-field vision (small faces in the picture, low lip region resolution) is the root cause of the weak modality, and also explains why fusion must be carefully designed to extract the value of vision. Second, the gain from lip-reading pre-training (V3 improves by 2.69 percentage points relative to V2) exceeds that of SimAM (V4 improves by 1.1 percentage points relative to V2), indicating that the bottleneck of the visual path is in representation learning rather than feature weighting. The general lip movement representation learned on LRW-1000 is directly migrated here, compensating for the innate deficiencies in the quantity and quality of MISP video data. This is a typical strategy of using external large-scale data to exchange for performance under resource-constrained conditions.

## Main Contributions

First, a new SOTA on the MISP 2021 dataset is provided: the HMA fusion system achieves FRR 2.15%, FAR 3.44%, and WWS 5.59% on the test set, a 21% relative improvement over the previous work, surpassing the competition first place (Table 4).

Second, the 3D/2D hybrid backbone is introduced to audio-visual WWS and verified to be superior to pure 2D and pure 3D: the division of labor where low-level 3D convolutions handle short-term spatiotemporal modeling and high-level 2D convolutions handle semantic and temporal aggregation holds on both the audio (Table 2) and video (Table 3) sides.

Third, the SimAM parameter-free attention is migrated to WWS, achieving improvements on both audio and video sides without introducing any parameters (A4 in Table 2, V5 in Table 3), providing a lightweight alternative to parameterized attention.

Fourth, a combination ablation of seven data augmentations is completed (Table 1), revealing the non-additivity of augmentation interactions (VP is harmful individually and removed, NS is harmful individually but beneficial in combination), providing a directly reusable augmentation recipe for far-field WWS training.

Fifth, the three fusion strategies of score-level, cascaded, and HMA are systematically compared. Experiments prove that cascaded fusion fails when posterior distributions are mismatched (VA6 in Table 4) while feature-level fusion is the most robust, leaving behind the evidence of ranking reversal between development and test sets, which is valuable for analysis.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, computational and latency costs are completely unreported. 3D convolution plus dual-modal paths plus top-3 model averaging imposes hard constraints on inference cost for persistent wake detection, a low-power consumption scenario. The paper does not report parameter count, computational load, or real-time metrics. Even the parameter reduction benefit of converting 3D to 2D is not quantified, remaining only at the qualitative statement that it can reduce parameters. Second, the visual front-end is a single-point dependency: RetinaFace detection failure, ArcFace target association selecting the wrong person, side faces, and occlusion will all cause the visual path to cut off or introduce erroneous supervision. The paper does not report the failure rate of the visual front-end and its impact on system-level metrics, whereas far-field small faces are precisely the conditions most likely to trigger these failures. Third, cascaded fusion is sensitive to manual thresholds ($th_l=0.1$, $th_h=0.4$ are manually fixed values). The paper stops at the conclusion that it doesn't work well, without exploring adaptive thresholds or learnable gating. Fourth, HMA requires two-stage training (first train two unimodal systems, then train the fusion network). The training process is more complex than end-to-end schemes, and the capacity and structural details of the fusion network are not elaborated in this paper (pointing to Reference [13]). Fifth, SimAM causes FAR deterioration on the video side development set (V4 vs V2 in Table 3, 9.09% vs 6.64%). The reason for this unstable gain is not analyzed in the paper.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First, data augmentation ablation is only done on 2D-ResNet34 (the paper's original words: "for convenience, we use 2D-ResNet34 for experiments here"). However, the final system is 3D/2D hybrid plus SimAM. The optimal combination D8 is not re-verified on the final architecture—the possibility of interaction between augmentation and architecture is ignored, and directly moving D8 into the final system carries a risk of sub-optimality. Second, the combination search in Table 1 is incomplete: D5 to D7 only add one augmentation each on the basis of NR+BE+NS. VP appears only once in D4 and is then completely dismissed. There is no exhaustive search of the combination space, nor multiple random seed repetitions and significance tests. Fluctuations in single experiments cannot be ruled out. Third, the comparison with the first place is only the total WWS score (5.8% vs 5.59%). The decomposition of FRR and FAR is not reported, making it impossible to compare error structures. Two systems with similar total scores may have completely different error compositions, which has different implications for productization decisions. Fourth, the specific composition of the top-3 model averaging (which three models, how loss is calculated, how weights are averaged) is not reported, affecting reproducibility. Fifth, video-side augmentations completely follow the previous work [13]. This paper does not perform video augmentation ablation, meaning optimization of the visual side's training strategy is actually in an unexplored state. Sixth, the development set spans field distances while the test set is only far-field. The ranking reversal where score-level is better on the development set and HMA is better on the test set indicates a deviation between the standard of selecting models based on development set loss and the final metric. The paper does not discuss the robustness of the model selection criterion.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

First, adaptive fusion: Replace the fixed $\alpha=\beta=0.5$ and fixed threshold cascaded fusion with dynamically adjusted fusion weights or learnable gating based on input conditions (SNR estimation, number of speakers, visual quality), turning intuitions like "increasing visual weight under strong noise" into mechanisms rather than hyperparameters. Second, stronger visual representation: Larger-scale lip-reading pre-training, generative augmentation for far-field low-resolution lip regions, or directly introducing dual-stream visual encoding of lip keypoint geometric features and appearance features. The goal is to first narrow the performance gap of about 8 percentage points between visual and audio unimodal (19.13% vs 11.03%), so that the ceiling of fusion can continue to be raised. Third, lightweight and streaming: Perform knowledge distillation and 3D convolution decomposition on the hybrid backbone. Evaluate whether the gain exchanged for top-3 model averaging is worth three times the inference cost. Provide a complete account of parameter count, latency, and power consumption for edge-side persistent deployment. Fourth, more thorough augmentation combination search: Replace manual combinations with automatic augmentation search or Bayesian optimization, and re-verify the optimal recipe on the final architecture. Fifth, upgrade the multi-channel front-end from training-time enhancement to inference-time input, exploring joint optimization of beamforming and back-end networks.
