## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the task of identifying target keywords within a continuous audio stream. It serves as the trigger entry for voice assistants ("Hey Siri", "Alexa", "Okay Google") on devices such as smart speakers, smartphones, and headphones (as stated in the paper's introduction). The unique nature of this entry point dictates that it consumes two types of resources simultaneously: on one hand, it is a resident module that must run on edge-side hardware with extremely limited computing power, memory, and power consumption, with the model size constrained to tens of thousands of parameters; on the other hand, it faces acoustic conditions with high variance—near-field and far-field scenarios, speaker differences, accents, environmental noise, and reverberation. Supervised models must cover all these combinations to generalize, and coverage relies on massive amounts of labeled data.

Thus, the paper explicitly defines the challenges of low-resource KWS as two-fold (from the abstract): **limited labeled data** and **limited available device resources**. These two are intertwined: labeling is expensive, leading to insufficient data; models are small, leading to limited capacity, making small models more prone to overfitting with less data. The introduction also highlights a third practical pain point: data distribution shifting between different recording environments causes model mismatch—models deployed in production lose performance when moved to new environments, and the cost of re-labeling for new environments circles back to the first pain point. These three pain points collectively point to the same proposition: **labeled data is the bottleneck, while unlabeled speech data is cheap and abundant. Can unlabeled data be used to inject priors into a small model intended for deployment?** This is the fundamental motivation for choosing "representation learning" as the entry point in this paper—it is precisely the standard method for converting unlabeled data into model priors.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper analyzes the deficiencies of two existing routes in the KWS scenario:

The first route is the edge-side small model itself. Recent KWS methods (cited [4]-[10]) have advanced in "improving accuracy" and "reducing computational cost." CNN-based methods (TC-ResNet, DS-CNN, etc.) offer both good accuracy and efficiency, but have structural issues: (1) **Heavy reliance on large amounts of labeled data**—the introduction states, "those methods require large volumes of labeled data, which is time-consuming and expensive"; high labeling costs are a real pain point in the industry; (2) **2D CNNs need to be stacked very deep to capture dependencies across frequency bands and frames**—this conflicts with the goal of small model size, as depth equals size; (3) Fragility to environmental drift, lacking cross-domain robustness.

The second route is introducing self-supervised pre-trained models into downstream tasks. The introduction reviews two existing usage patterns: one is using them as feature extractors fine-tuned on small amounts of labeled data (cite [13]), and the other is using teacher models to generate pseudo-labels for student models (cite [16][17], e.g., HuBERT). The benefit of the pseudo-label route is that it can filter out low-quality ground truth, preventing the student model from being fed dirty data, and can label unlabeled data when initial annotations are insufficient. However, each has a fatal flaw when applied to KWS: **the fine-tuning route requires the deployed model itself to be that large model**—the introduction directly cites [18] (a pre-trained embedding scheme for few-shot KWS) stating "the pretrained model is too large to deploy on low-resource devices"; wav2vec 2.0 base has tens of millions of parameters and cannot fit on edge devices; **the pseudo-label route requires the teacher to output labels aligned with the downstream label space**—general self-supervised speech models lack a keyword classification head and cannot provide pseudo-labels for "is it a wake-word"; fine-tuning the teacher first returns to the first flaw.

Furthermore, directly transferring contrastive learning/siamese networks from computer vision (cite [19] SimCLR, [20] SimSiam) to KWS has known problems: without constraints, the siamese structure's outputs will "collapse" into a constant—the network finds it easiest to output a constant vector, rendering the representation completely ineffective. This is a risk explicitly named in the introduction.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Condensing the above contradictions into a core problem: **Under the dual constraints of "few labels + small edge-side computing power," how to use unlabeled data to pre-train a small model of ~65K parameters to learn useful acoustic representations.** The paper breaks this down into three sub-challenges: (1) How to transfer knowledge from wav2vec 2.0 to a small student encoder that can be deployed on the edge, without inheriting its size—i.e., "using the large model but not deploying the large model"; (2) How to design a self-supervised contrastive structure that does not collapse, effectively constraining both sentence-level and frame-level representations—i.e., "how to construct positive and negative sample pairs"; (3) How to combine multiple unsupervised losses and supervised CE losses in stages and with weights to mutually enhance rather than conflict. The first two challenges correspond to the paper's two modules (WVC, LGCSiam), and the third challenge corresponds to the training pipeline design.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system (Figure 1) consists of three parts: the self-training WVC module (self-training with pretrained Wav2Vec 2.0 as constraint), the self-supervised LGCSiam module (local-global contrastive siamese networks), and the lightweight TCANet model itself.

The most notable point in the design philosophy is: **the object being pre-trained is the small model itself that will be deployed.** It is not a two-stage process of "pre-training a large model and then distilling it into a small model," but rather letting the TCANet in its deployed form act as the student, pulling it into a good representation space using two constraints on unlabeled data first, and then fine-tuning with a small amount of labeled data. Why is it designed this way: the training form and deployment form are completely consistent, avoiding secondary accuracy loss from distillation/compression steps; simultaneously, the two representation learning modules (teacher network, siamese branches) only exist during the training phase and are removed entirely during inference, resulting in zero edge-side overhead—this is the concrete implementation of "using the knowledge of the large model, not the size of the large model."

TCANet's data path (Figure 1(b)): Input waveform is converted into a 40-dimensional log-Mel spectrogram $x \in R^{B \times T \times 40}$ (B is batch, T is max frames); the encoder $E(\cdot)$ structure is similar to TC-ResNet, consisting of 7 convolutional layers—the first layer has kernel=3, stride=2 (compressing time domain from T to T/2), the remaining 6 layers have kernel=9, with 64 output channels per layer; BatchNorm and ReLU follow each layer. The encoder transforms $x$ into $e \in R^{B \times T/2 \times 64}$. The decoder $D(\cdot)$ is a multi-head self-attention block; the output undergoes global average pooling (compressing the time dimension into one frame), a dense layer, and a softmax layer to obtain $p \in R^{B \times C}$, where C=12 classes (10 command words plus unknown, silence), optimized using cross-entropy loss (Equation 2).

The division of labor between "convolutional encoder + attention decoder" has clear motivation. The encoder follows TC-ResNet-style temporal convolutions because temporal convolutions are arithmetic-intensity friendly and parameter-efficient on edge devices; the decoder switches to multi-head self-attention precisely to address the issue pointed out in the introduction that "2D CNNs need to be stacked very deep to cover cross-frequency-band and cross-frame dependencies"—attention establishes a global receptive field in one step, exchanging depth for a very small parameter budget. To keep the total volume at 65K (Table 3), the paper made a key trade-off in the encoder: apart from the first layer, all layers use separable conv1d instead of standard conv1d, reducing single-layer parameters from $9 \times 64 \times 64 = 36864$ to $9 \times 64 \times 1 + 1 \times 64 \times 64 = 4672$, an approximately 8-fold reduction. The saved parameter budget just supports the attention decoder. Additionally, the first layer's stride=2 halves the sequence length, so subsequent convolutions and attention are computed at T/2 resolution—since attention's computational cost grows quadratically with sequence length, halving the time domain is equivalent to cutting the attention overhead by about 4 times. This is the arithmetic prerequisite for "small models daring to use attention."

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The entire paper contains 11 equations, constituting four loss functions and two sets of combined weights.

**Supervised Classification Loss (Eq. 2)**: Standard cross-entropy $Loss_{CE} = -\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j=1}^{C} y_{ij}\log(p_{ij})$, where $y \in R^{B \times C}$ is the one-hot ground truth.

**Attention Decoder (Eq. 1)**: $Q = e \cdot W_Q$, $K = e \cdot W_K$, $V = e \cdot W_V$, where $W_Q, W_K, W_V \in R^{64 \times 64}$; each head $head_i = Softmax(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{64/n}})V_i$ (n is the number of heads, each head dimension is 64/n); decoder output $D(e) = Concat(head_1, ..., head_n)W_o$, where $W_o \in R^{64 \times 64}$. The paper does not report the specific value of n.

**WVC Loss (Eq. 3, 4)**: The Wav2Vec 2.0 base model (the paper's footnote provides the official checkpoint wav2vec_small.pt) consists of a feature encoder, context network, quantization module, and pre-training contrastive loss; this paper uses it to process input waveforms to obtain context representations $e_0 \in R^{B \times T/2 \times 768}$ (12 transformer blocks, dimension increases from 512 to 768, frame shift 10ms). The output of the KWS encoder $e_1 \in R^{B \times T/2 \times 64}$ is transformed to the teacher space via an MLP projection head: $e_1' = W_2\sigma(W_1 \cdot e_1)$, where $W_1 \in R^{64 \times 128}$, $W_2 \in R^{128 \times 768}$, and $\sigma$ is ReLU. The loss is MSE regression: $Loss_{WVC} = \|e_1' - e_0\|^2$.

**LGCSiam Loss (Eq. 5-9)**: Two random augmentations of the same waveform are converted into log-Mel spectrograms $x_1, x_2 \in R^{B \times T \times 40}$, passed through the **shared-weight** encoder and decoder to obtain $d_1 = D(E(x_1))$, $d_2 = D(E(x_2))$, where $d_1, d_2 \in R^{B \times T/2 \times 64}$; then passed through an MLP projection head $d_1' = W_4\sigma(W_3 \cdot d_1)$ ($W_3 \in R^{64 \times 128}$, $W_4 \in R^{128 \times 128}$). The construction of positive and negative sample pairs (Figure 2) is the core: **Local positive pairs** are feature vectors at the same frame position for two augmentations of the same sample, e.g., $[d_1'(0,0,:), d_2'(0,0,:)]$, $[d_1'(0,1,:), d_2'(0,1,:)]$ up to $[d_1'(B,T,:), d_2'(B,T,:)]$; **Global positive pairs** are sentence-level vectors after time-average pooling; **Negative pairs** are any combination of "different samples or different timestamps" $[d_1'(j_1,i_1,:), d_2'(j_2,i_2,:)]$, where $i_1 \neq i_2$ or $j_1 \neq j_2$. Two loss components: Global consistency loss (Eq. 7) is the MSE of sentence-level vectors after time averaging, averaged over the batch; Local contrastive loss (Eq. 8) is in InfoNCE form—the numerator is the similarity of positive pairs at the same position, the denominator sums over all candidates in the batch at all timestamps, with temperature parameter $\tau = 0.5$, and the similarity function is cosine similarity. Finally, $Loss_{LGCSiam} = L_{global} + L_{local}$ (Eq. 9).

**Multi-stage Combination (Eq. 10, 11)**: Pre-training is divided into two steps—the first step optimizes the encoder on unlabeled data using only $Loss_{WVC}$; the second step jointly trains the encoder and decoder using $L = \lambda_1 Loss_{LGCSiam} + \lambda_2 Loss_{WVC}$ ($\lambda_1 = 0.1$, $\lambda_2 = 0.9$). In the supervised fine-tuning stage, the classification head is added, and the total loss is $L = \gamma_1 Loss_{CE} + \gamma_2 Loss_{LGCSiam} + \gamma_3 Loss_{WVC}$, where $\gamma_1 = 0.9$, $\gamma_2 = \gamma_3 = 0.05$.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: WVC Module — Using wav2vec 2.0 as a "Constraint" Rather Than a Feature Extractor or Pseudo-Label Machine

WVC (Wav2Vec 2.0 as Constraint) is the most effective design in the paper (ablation in Table 1: +4.06 points alone at 5% labeling). There are three layers of "why" behind its technical choices:

First, **why align representation space rather than label space**. The pseudo-label route (cite [16][17]) requires the teacher to output labels aligned with KWS categories; general self-supervised models cannot provide keyword judgments; whereas frame-level regression of teacher hidden representations only requires the teacher to provide dense targets at the frame level. **Any unlabeled audio can serve as training data**, requiring no labels, no task alignment, and no prior fine-tuning of the teacher. This directly bypasses the deadlocks of the two existing routes analyzed in the introduction.

Second, **why add a 64→128→768 MLP projection head**. The student backbone has only 64-dimensional output, while the teacher is 768-dimensional; if forced to output 768 dimensions directly, the last few layers of the backbone would expand, causing edge-side size to spiral out of control. The projection head absorbs the burden of "dimension transformation" into a module dedicated to the training phase, keeping the student backbone at a small 64-dimensional overhead. The projection head is removed along with the entire WVC module during inference. This is isomorphic to the projection head idea in SimCLR: the projection head handles spatial transformation, while the backbone learns transferable representations.

Third, **why use MSE instead of cosine similarity or KL**. MSE performs element-wise regression, constraining both direction and magnitude, which is a stronger constraint than cosine similarity which only constrains direction; for frame-level dense alignment, the form of "teacher vector as regression target" is most direct for MSE. The cost is sensitivity to the absolute scale of teacher representations, but this is not an issue with the same-source checkpoint.

A mechanism detail worth emphasizing: WVC constrains the **encoder output** ($e_1$), while LGCSiam constrains the **encoder+decoder overall output** ($d = D(E(x))$). The two modules act at different depths of the network, and pre-training proceeds sequentially (Section 3.4.2: TCANet+WVC+LGCSiam first pre-trains with WVC, then trains LGCSiam, and finally fine-tunes the whole thing), forming a progressive structure of "pull encoder first, then pull the whole path, finally fine-tune with labels."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: LGCSiam Module — Local-Global Dual-Scale Contrastive Anti-Collapse

Siamese networks without additional constraints will collapse into constant outputs (cite [20]), which is the starting point for designing LGCSiam in this paper. There is more than one technical route to prevent collapse—SimSiam uses stop-gradient, SimCLR uses large batch negative samples plus projection head—the paper chooses a combination of **negative sample repulsion + dual-scale positive pairs**, and makes two modifications tailored to the temporal structure of speech:

First, **the negative sample pool comes from two dimensions: "cross-sample" and "cross-timestamp"**. In image contrastive learning, negative samples can only come from other images within the batch, and the pool size is limited by the batch; whereas this paper includes all other frame vectors in the same batch (frames from different samples, frames from different moments of the same sample) in the denominator of Eq. 8, expanding the negative sample pool to the magnitude of $B \times T/2$, which is equivalent to **freeing negative samples along the sequence dimension without increasing the batch size**, which is particularly cost-effective for small-card training. Why can "different timestamps of the same sample" serve as negative samples: adjacent frames in speech have extremely strong correlations; if adjacent frames are also treated as positive pairs, the network only needs to learn a "smooth low-pass" degenerate solution to fool the loss; setting different timestamps as negative pairs forces the representation to retain discriminative power along the time axis.

Second, **global sentence-level positive pairs and local frame-level positive pairs are used together** (Figure 2(a)). Local positive pairs ensure the encoder learns fine-grained acoustic event representations (what happens in which frame), while global positive pairs ensure the entire frontend outputs stable sentence-level summaries for two augmentations of the same waveform. They use different loss forms: global uses MSE (Eq. 7, directly pulling together), local uses InfoNCE (Eq. 8, contrastive repulsion), complementarily supporting the representation space.

The premise of this structure is guaranteed by data augmentation (Figure 3)—"two augmentations have the same semantics" must hold for contrastive learning to make sense. The seven augmentations selected by the paper are all **channel-level/noise-level perturbations that preserve keyword identity**: pre-emphasis (first-order autoregressive filtering $y[n] = y[n] - coef \cdot y[n-1]$, coefficient 0.95~0.99), de-emphasis (inverse operation), pitch shifting (-5 to +5 semitones), notch filtering and peak filtering (suppression or enhancement of random frequency bands), adding background noise (SNR -5dB to 15dB), frequency masking (SpecAugment, masking up to 10 frequency bands), Cutout (up to 10 in frequency domain, up to 10 in time domain). Why choose these instead of speech content-level augmentations: they exactly simulate environmental drift during KWS deployment (frequency response of different recording devices, different background noise, speakers with different pitches), making the representations learned during pre-training naturally robust to the distribution drift problem mentioned in the introduction.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: TCANet — Lightweight Marriage of Temporal Convolution Encoder and Attention Decoder

As the carrier model, TCANet's contribution lies in squeezing three things into 65K parameters (Table 3): a 7-layer temporal convolution encoder (including separable conv reducing parameters by ~8x, first layer stride=2 halving the time domain), a multi-head self-attention decoder (Eq. 1), and a global average pooling plus dense/softmax classification head. At the same parameter count (65K vs 66K), it outperforms TC-ResNet8 by 0.75 points (96.85 vs 96.1, Table 3), indicating that the combination of "convolution for local features + attention for global context" is indeed more parameter-efficient than pure convolutional residual structures. The root of this design motivation lies in the introduction: "2D CNNs need very deep models to capture dependencies between different frequency bands and different frames," while attention turns the "dependency modeling" from an architecture depth issue into something that can be done with a $64 \times 64$ projection.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the few-shot KWS pre-trained embedding scheme in cite [18]: [18] deploys the large pre-trained model itself, whereas in this paper, the large model only appears during the training phase, and the edge side only has the 65K student. This is the fundamental difference. Compared to the HuBERT/pseudo-label distillation route (cite [16][17]): this paper does not generate pseudo-labels, does not require the teacher to have task-aligned output heads, and instead uses hidden representation regression, relaxing data requirements from "domains where pseudo-labels can be generated" to "any speech audio." Compared to direct transfer from SimCLR (cite [19])/SimSiam (cite [20]): this paper transforms the image's "one image, one instance representation" into "one speech, T/2 frames of frame-level + sentence-level dual positive pairs, cross-sample cross-time negative pairs," expanding the negative sample dimension from one axis (batch) to two axes (batch + time), adapting to the temporal structure of speech. Compared to edge-side baselines like TC-ResNet, DS-CNN, etc.: Transformer-style self-attention (cite [21]) is introduced in the decoder for the first time (in KWS models of this parameter magnitude), while retaining the efficiency advantages of temporal convolutions. Compared to the conventional "pre-training → fine-tuning" two-stage approach: Eq. 11 retains two unsupervised losses as regularization terms during the fine-tuning stage ($\gamma_2 = \gamma_3 = 0.05$), preventing the student's representations from drifting too quickly toward the 12-class decision boundaries on small labeled sets and losing the general structure learned during pre-training—this is essentially treating "representation preservation" as a continuous constraint during fine-tuning, rather than a one-time initialization.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

- **Speech Commands Dataset** (cite [22], Warden 2018): 65,000 one-second English speech commands, 12 classes ("yes", "no", "up", "down", "left", "right", "on", "off", "stop", "go" plus unknown and silence), with training/validation/test sets split according to the official implementation of [22]; background noise is generated by randomly selecting and clipping noise segments and superimposing them. The low-resource setting takes a 5% subset from the labeled set for fine-tuning (columns "Acc. (5% SC)" in Table 1, Table 2).
- **AISHELL Dataset** (cite [23], i.e., the unlabeled use of the AISHELL-1 Mandarin reading corpus): Used for cross-domain pre-training experiments (Table 2); the paper does not report the actual number of hours or utterances used.
- Pre-training data scale: Both experiments state "100% unlabeled" (Table 1 uses 100% unlabeled SC for pre-training, Table 2 uses 100% unlabeled AISHELL for pre-training); the paper does not report the data-volume-gain curve for smaller amounts of unlabeled data.

Implementation details (Section 3.2): PyTorch implementation, mini-batch 128, SGD momentum 0.9, weight decay 0.0001, initial learning rate 0.1, decayed by multiplying 0.333 if validation accuracy does not increase for 3 consecutive epochs.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Classification Accuracy (Acc.)**: The only accuracy metric. The rationale is direct and pragmatic—Speech Commands is a 12-class classification benchmark, and accuracy can be directly aligned with literature numbers from DS-CNN (cite [8]), TC-ResNet (cite [11]), ensuring horizontal comparability in Table 3.
- **Parameter Count (Params)**: The scale metric in Table 3, used to support the "small-footprint" claim.
- **Not Reported by the Paper**: False Alarm Rate / Missed Detection Rate (FA/FR), DET curves, AUC, inference latency, FLOPS, running memory, real hardware power consumption—these are exactly the metrics most concerned with KWS engineering deployment, all missing (see Limitations section). The implicit reason for choosing only accuracy is that the paper's experimental positioning is a "comparative study of representation learning methods" rather than a "product-level KWS system release," but this positioning also limits the extrapolation range of the conclusions.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

The main comparison is in **Table 3** (Speech Commands dataset):

| Model | Acc. | Params |
|---|---|---|
| DS-CNN-S | 94.4 | 24K |
| DS-CNN-M | 94.9 | 140K |
| DS-CNN-L | 95.4 | 420K |
| TC-ResNet8 | 96.1 | 66K |
| TC-ResNet8-1.5 | 96.2 | 145K |
| TC-ResNet14 | 96.2 | 137K |
| TC-ResNet14-1.5 | 96.6 | 305K |
| TCANet | 96.85 | 65K |
| TCANet+WVC+LGCSiam | 97.5 | 65K |

Three readings: First, **a positive victory at the same parameter magnitude**—TCANet (65K) vs TC-ResNet8 (66K) is 96.85 vs 96.1, higher by 0.75 points, indicating that the attention decoder indeed traded for accuracy at the same volume; Second, **a victory in cost-effectiveness**—after adding representation learning, it reaches 97.5, which is 0.9 points higher than the largest baseline TC-ResNet14-1.5 (96.6, 305K parameters), while the parameters are only about 21% of its size, equivalent to "buying 0.9 more points with one-fifth the volume"; Third, the entire DS-CNN series (94.4~95.4) is significantly suppressed by the temporal convolution route (96.1+) in this accuracy range, and DS-CNN only reaches 95.4 even at 420K parameters (DS-CNN-L), showing that the gap in architecture routes is greater than the gap in scale.

It should be noted that the comparison in Table 3 is under the full-label setting (100% SC fine-tuning); the true power of representation learning lies in low-label settings (see ablation section); the paper does not report these baselines' numbers under 5% labeling, so the question of "how much it leads baselines in low-resource settings" strictly speaking has no comparative answer.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

**In-domain Pre-training (Table 1)**—Pre-trained on 100% unlabeled SC, fine-tuned on 100% and 5% labeled SC respectively:

| System | Acc. (100% SC) | Acc. (5% SC) |
|---|---|---|
| TCANet (no pre-training) | 96.85 | 88.24 |
| TCANet+LGCSiam | 96.97 | 89.92 |
| TCANet+WVC | 97.45 | 92.3 |
| TCANet+WVC+LGCSiam | 97.5 | 92.52 |

**Finding 1: The fewer the labels, the greater the gain from representation learning.** Under the 5% setting, the gain of the complete system is 92.52 − 88.24 = **+4.28 points**, while under the 100% setting, it is only 97.5 − 96.85 = **+0.65 points**, the former being about 6.6 times the latter. This directly verifies the paper's core proposition—the value of representation learning is realized when labels are scarce; when labels are sufficient, the supervised signal itself is already enough, and the marginal value of unsupervised priors decays rapidly. Why does bare training under the 5% setting only reach 88.24 (dropping 8.6 points): small model plus small data, overfitting dominates; pre-training is equivalent to initializing parameters to a manifold that is acoustically reasonable, significantly narrowing the search space.

**Finding 2: WVC dominates, LGCSiam is marginal.** LGCSiam alone gains +1.68 under 5% (88.24→89.92), WVC alone gains +4.06 (→92.3), and their combination only gains an additional +0.22 (→92.52). This indicates that the frame-level dense teacher regression signal (WVC, T/2 frames of supervision per sample) has far more information than the self-built contrastive structure (LGCSiam's supervision is essentially this one weak constraint of augmentation invariance); LGCSiam mainly supplements the magnitude of fine-tuning on top of WVC and cannot independently support the overall picture. This ablation has direct implications for resource allocation in subsequent work: **if only one module can be implemented, do WVC first.**

**Finding 3: Cross-domain pre-training is almost lossless (Table 2)**—Pre-trained on 100% unlabeled AISHELL (Mandarin reading corpus), fine-tuned on SC (English commands):

| System | Acc. (100% SC) | Acc. (5% SC) |
|---|---|---|
| TCANet (no pre-training) | 96.85 | 88.24 |
| TCANet+LGCSiam | 96.96 | 89.09 |
| TCANet+WVC | 97.32 | 92.13 |
| TCANet+WVC+LGCSiam | 97.45 | 92.33 |

The complete system drops only 0.05 points (97.45 vs 97.5) and 0.19 points (92.33 vs 92.52, comparing Table 1 and Table 2) in cross-domain relative to in-domain. This is the result with the greatest engineering value in the paper: **pre-training data does not need to be in the same language, domain, or recording conditions as the target task**—representations pre-trained on Chinese unlabeled corpus can still be transferred to English keyword classification. The deployment implication is that labeling budgets can be spent entirely on target-domain fine-tuning, and pre-training corpus can be "whatever is available."

**Finding 4: The two modules have different sensitivities to cross-domain settings, exposing mechanistic differences.** Looking at the differences between Table 1 and Table 2 separately: when LGCSiam is used alone, it drops 0.83 points in cross-domain under 5% (89.92→89.09), while WVC drops only 0.17 points (92.3→92.13). Why: LGCSiam learns "augmentation invariance," and this invariance is anchored in the distribution of the pre-training data itself; when switching domains, the distribution-specific structures learned under augmentation will partially fail; whereas WVC distills the hidden representations of wav2vec 2.0—this model is pre-trained on ultra-large-scale multi-domain data, and its representations are inherently language-independent. The student inherits this cross-domain stability through regression. This also explains why the complete system is robust in cross-domain settings: the dominant component (WVC) is inherently cross-domain.

**Ablation Gaps**: The paper does not report the necessity of the two-stage pre-training order (WVC first, then LGCSiam, Section 3.4.2)—there is no control for reverse order or single-step joint pre-training; no sensitivity analysis is reported for the weights ($\lambda_1=0.1$, $\lambda_2=0.9$, $\gamma_1=0.9$, $\gamma_2=\gamma_3=0.05$) and temperature $\tau=0.5$ in Eq. 10, Eq. 11; no progressive curve is reported for unlabeled data volume from 0 to 100%.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed a third route of using wav2vec 2.0 as a constraint module (WVC) rather than a feature extractor or pseudo-label teacher**: Does not deploy the large model, does not fine-tune the large model, does not require label space alignment, and transfers knowledge solely via frame-level hidden representation regression, bringing a separate +4.06 points under the 5% labeling setting (Table 1), which is the main source of validity in the paper.
2. **Proposed the LGCSiam local-global contrastive siamese structure**: Dual positive pairs at frame and sentence levels, cross-sample and cross-timestamp negative pairs, complementary MSE and InfoNCE dual losses, preventing siamese network collapse without needing tricks like stop-gradient, and utilizing the speech sequence structure to free-expand the negative sample pool to the magnitude of $B \times T/2$.
3. **Proposed the lightweight model TCANet**: Temporal convolution encoder (separable conv reduces parameters by ~8x, first layer stride=2 halves the time domain) plus multi-head self-attention decoder, reaching 96.85 with 65K parameters, exceeding TC-ResNet8 by 0.75 points at the same parameter magnitude; after adding representation learning, it reaches 97.5, exceeding all baselines (Table 3).
4. **Provided evidence for the effectiveness of cross-language and cross-domain pre-training**: Pre-training on Chinese unlabeled corpus is almost lossless on English KWS fine-tuning (Table 2), forming a low-resource deployment recipe of "take any unlabeled data, spend labels only on target-domain fine-tuning," and proving that the gain is larger when labels are fewer (+4.28 under 5% setting vs +0.65 under 100% setting, Table 1).

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **The training cost of WVC is shifted and not quantified**. Each pre-training iteration requires a forward pass of the 12-transformer-block wav2vec 2.0 base teacher; the pre-training computational cost is far greater than fine-tuning; the paper does not report the time/compute ratio between pre-training and fine-tuning, nor does it discuss whether teacher forward passes can be cached or accelerated via quantization. Edge-side inference saves costs, but the overhead on the training side is real.
- **Offline decision-making form is misaligned with streaming KWS requirements**. TCANet's supervision path uses global average pooling to compress the entire sentence into one frame for classification, which is naturally an "input whole segment, one decision" offline form; real wake-up systems require per-frame low-latency triggering and false alarm suppression. The attention decoder itself also tends toward global context. The paper does not report any streaming transformation schemes (causal attention, frame-level decision heads, cached inference); there is a clear untraveled path from this paper's form to a product-level wake-up engine.
- **There is an inherent tension in the supervision semantics of LGCSiam**. The abstract claims the goal is to "learn similar sentence-level representations for similar audio samples," but the negative pairs in Eq. 8 push apart different samples in the same batch (including samples of the same keyword from different speakers)—the actual optimization is for instance discrimination rather than semantic similarity; "similar audio" is only guaranteed in the sense of augmentation. This tension is同源 (homologous) to known problems of contrastive learning in the visual field; the paper does not discuss it nor conduct controls for supervised contrastive variants.
- **The rationality of MSE alignment of hidden representations depends on teacher representation quality**. Transformer hidden representations have issues such as anisotropy and redundancy; the paper does not perform any analysis or selection on wav2vec 2.0 intermediate layer representations (e.g., which layer to align with, whether whitening is needed), nor does it report cases of alignment failure.
- **Hyperparameters are hand-tuned fixed values**. Temperature $\tau = 0.5$, loss weights $\lambda_1 = 0.1$, $\lambda_2 = 0.9$, $\gamma_1 = 0.9$, $\gamma_2 = \gamma_3 = 0.05$ are not given sensitivity analysis; their robustness when transferred to other datasets or model scales is unknown.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Single dimension of metrics**. Only classification accuracy and parameter count are reported; the paper does not report FA/FR, DET curves, AUC, latency, FLOPS, memory, or power consumption. The argument for "small-footprint" rests solely on 65K parameters (Table 3), without edge-side empirical support.
- **Single downstream benchmark**. All downstream experiments are completed on the 12-class command word classification of Speech Commands, which differs from the real "single wake-word + unknown word + silence" open-set task form; no second dataset (e.g., Google Speech Commands v2's other language versions, internal wake-word sets) is used for validation.
- **Incomplete reproduction details**. The paper does not report the number of attention heads n, input frames T, Mel window length/frame shift (only frame shift 10ms is mentioned for the Wav2Vec 2.0 side), number of pre-training epochs, specific sampling method for unlabeled data; it is also unclear whether the DS-CNN, TC-ResNet baseline numbers in Table 3 are reproduced or cited from literature.
- **Incomplete ablation**. Missing controls for two-stage pre-training order, missing curves for different unlabeled data volumes (only the 100% tier), missing controls for baselines under 5% labeling (leading to no horizontal external evidence for low-resource settings), cross-domain has only one direction (Chinese pre-training → English fine-tuning), missing symmetric validation for English → Chinese or third languages.
- **Baselines lack similar self-supervised methods**. No experimental comparison is made with routes analyzed in the introduction such as "fine-tuning wav2vec 2.0 then distilling" or "HuBERT pseudo-labels," nor are there controls with different teacher models (WavLM, HuBERT); the sensitivity of the WVC framework to teacher selection is unknown.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

- **Streaming transformation and completion of product-level metrics**: Replace global average pooling with frame-level decision heads, change attention to causal/restricted window forms, add FA/FR and DET curves, and answer "how many points does this representation learning add in a real wake-up engine."
- **Cost reduction on the teacher side**: Use smaller self-supervised models as teachers, or perform one-time representation compression (layer pruning, quantization, whitening) on wav2vec 2.0 before using it as a regression target, reducing pre-training costs; multi-teacher/multi-layer alignment can also be explored.
- **Semantic upgrade of the contrastive structure**: Introduce keyword-aware positive pairs in LGCSiam (samples of the same keyword from different speakers serve as positive pairs for each other, i.e., supervised contrastive ideas), bridging the tension between instance discrimination and "similar audio, similar representations"; perform hard negative mining for negative pairs.
- **Systematic characterization of data volume-gain**: Report 2D curves for unlabeled data volume from zero to full, and labeling ratio from 1% to 100%, providing a basis for labeling budget planning for low-resource deployment; symmetrically validate cross-domain conclusions on more languages and more domains (far-field, telephone channels).
- **Sensitivity research on teachers and frameworks**: Horizontally compare the effects of wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, and WavLM as WVC teachers, and test whether the recipe of "frame-level hidden representation regression" itself is superior to pseudo-label or feature fine-tuning routes.
