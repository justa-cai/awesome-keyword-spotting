## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points
Speech keyword spotting is a core technology for implementing always-on voice interfaces on consumer electronic devices. Its value lies in its simple structure, low latency, and the ability to be implemented with relatively small neural networks, avoiding the massive computational overhead associated with full speech recognition. The paper explicitly anchors the application scenarios in wearable devices and smart home IoT devices: such devices are equipped only with low-power microcontrollers, with flash storage in the hundreds of KB range and RAM in the tens of KB range (as stated in the Related Work section of Chapter 2). Furthermore, due to the small physical size of these devices, it is difficult to arrange multiple microphones like smart speakers. Representative smart speakers, such as the Google Speaker series, trade accuracy for a quad-core application processor and more than two microphones. Yu et al.'s multi-microphone noise-robust keyword spotting scheme uses even six microphones and a model with over 5.1M parameters (cited in Chapter 2 of the paper). These hardware conditions do not hold for earbuds, watches, or sensor nodes.

The pain points are thus clear: single-microphone, no noise-reduction frontend, and the model must be small enough to fit into a microcontroller, while environmental noise is inevitable. Environmental noise degrades the performance of the acoustic model, directly harming the usability of the voice interface—a wake-word function that cannot be awakened in a noisy environment is effectively non-existent. The strong noise scenarios focused on in this paper are quantified by Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR): SNR below 0 dB indicates that background noise power exceeds speech power, and -10 dB indicates that noise power is ten times that of speech (definition in Equation (1) and explanation in Chapter 3). Maintaining keyword spotting accuracy under these conditions is the watershed for whether voice interfaces on wearable devices can be successfully deployed.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods
The paper systematically outlines the gaps in existing methods using a positioning table (Table I). The core contradiction is that the two lines of "noise robustness" and "small footprint" have never truly intersected:

- **Capacity Bottleneck of Noise-Mixed Training**: Injecting background noise into clean training samples is the most promising data augmentation route, as it artificially creates difficult samples to make the model more robust. However, the paper repeatedly emphasizes an empirical fact—tiny neural networks struggle to learn loud noise situations. Insufficient capacity causes small models to fail directly when facing louder noise.
- **Curriculum Learning Cannot Save Small Networks**: Curriculum learning divides the training process into stages based on sample difficulty (starting with clean speech, then moving to strong noise), mimicking the rhythm of human curriculum learning. It is widely used for noise-robust keyword spotting. However, the paper's key observation is that this strategy is effective for large networks but basically ineffective for small networks. The data in Table III is striking: for a small network with 27k parameters (BC-ResNet-2), random noise-mixed training without curriculum yields a clean accuracy of 95.7%, while applying a traditional ConvMixer-style curriculum drops it to 91.7%. At the strong noise end of MUSAN (-12.5 dB), the difference is only 0.5 percentage points (69.4% vs. 68.9%). Traditional curriculum learning not only provides no benefit to small networks but also hinders overall accuracy.
- **Existing Robust Methods Do Not Meet Footprint Constraints**: ImportantAug uses an augmentation agent to decide where to inject noise into the less important regions of speech, but related work uses large models with 24,000k (24M) parameters (Table I), and the test accuracy at -12.5 dB is only 56.5%. ConvMixer has 119k parameters and achieves 71.9% at -10 dB. PKD (Prototype Knowledge Distillation) has 102k parameters and only reports 82.3% at 0 dB, with no results reported for stronger noise (Table I).
- **Existing Compression Methods Do Not Consider Noise Robustness**: Compression research aimed at small-footprint keyword spotting (e.g., Song et al.'s lightweight encoder distillation, Tucker et al.'s distillation plus quantization, Kim et al.'s PQK pruning-quantization-distillation trilogy) all focus solely on size and accuracy. Noise robustness is not included in their objective functions.
- **Joint Frameworks Are a Blank in Acoustic Fields**: Joint frameworks of curriculum learning and knowledge distillation have appeared in computer vision and natural language processing (e.g., Zhu et al. for long-sentence training in dialogue generation, Panagiotatos et al. for visual robust distillation). However, such research is very scarce in acoustic modeling. Although Guimaraes et al. used both curriculum learning and distillation, the purpose of distillation was to extract knowledge from large self-supervised models (DistilHuBERT), not to compress the keyword spotting model itself.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper
In one sentence: Under the constraints of a single microphone and model parameters compressed to the 27k level (deployable on microcontrollers), let the keyword spotting model maintain usable accuracy in unseen noise environments with SNR as low as -10, -12.5, or even -15 dB. Broken down, this involves three intertwined sub-problems: First, curriculum learning requires sufficient model capacity to digest strong noise samples, but the deployment end lacks exactly that capacity—there is a mismatch between capacity and constraints. Second, even if a large network learns noise robustness, how to transfer this property to a small network with minimal loss (ordinary single-teacher distillation suffers significant loss at strong noise ends). Third, ensemble distillation can improve teacher quality, but simple averaging of multiple teachers often leads to conflicts, and independently training multiple teachers is costly. The paper's answer is to string these three sub-problems into a two-stage pipeline, allowing the weakness of each module to be compensated by the strength of another.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation
The overall framework is a two-stage joint pipeline (Fig. 1): In the first stage, a sufficiently large teacher network (BC-ResNet-8, 321k parameters, see Experimental Setup section; recorded as 313k in Table III) is subjected to the paper's proposed noise-mixed curriculum learning, allowing it to fully learn various noise conditions, especially strong noise conditions. In the second stage, the paper's proposed weighted stage ensemble distillation compresses this large teacher into a small student network (BC-ResNet-2, 27.3k parameters) that can be deployed on a microcontroller. The base network selected is BC-ResNet (Broadcast Residual Learning Network from Interspeech 2021, a state-of-the-art small-footprint keyword spotting architecture at the time). The input is a 49×40 two-dimensional feature obtained by MFCC transformation of 1-second raw audio, and the output is 11 classes (10 predefined keywords plus 1 negative class, where the negative class includes non-keyword words and background audio).

The motivation for this architecture stems from the paper's core finding: **curriculum learning on a large teacher first, followed by distilling a small student, is superior to applying curriculum learning directly to the small student** (Fig. 4: compared to curriculum learning training the small network directly, the joint framework improves average accuracy by approximately 8 percentage points and reduces model size by an average of 4 times). The logical chain is: Noise robustness is essentially a property distilled by the model from data, and distillation requires capacity; the deployment end does not need the "distillation process," only the "distillation result," and knowledge distillation is exactly the pipeline for transporting properties. Thus, capacity requirements and deployment constraints are split to be satisfied in two separate stages—the teacher stage releases capacity to learn robustness, and the student stage only performs property transport.

The selection of the teacher scale also has data support: Fig. 14 shows that at MUSAN -12.5 dB, a 120k parameter teacher achieves only 75.1%, a 350k teacher rises to 79.7%, and a 700k teacher reaches 80.9%. The gains slow down significantly after 350k. Based on this, the paper selects a teacher of approximately 320k magnitude (accuracy close to the 79.7% tier), striking a balance between teacher accuracy and training/distillation costs. It explicitly states that larger teachers and more teacher numbers yield higher accuracy, but the appropriate scale must be selected according to computing power limits.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms
**Noise Quantification Basis (Equation (1))**: SNR is defined as the decibel value of the ratio of average speech power to average noise power, $SNR = 10 \cdot \log_{10}(P_{speech} / P_{noise})$. When synthesizing training samples, given a target SNR value $r$, the noise scaling factor $\omega$ is inversely solved to obtain the mixed sample $d_{Mix} = d_{Speech} + \omega \cdot d_{Noise}$ (Algorithm 1, lines 06-07), ensuring the synthesized sample falls precisely on the expected signal-to-noise ratio.

**Standard Knowledge Distillation (Equations (2)-(6))**: The teacher and student outputs are logits $z_T = f(\theta_T, x)$ and $z_S = g(\theta_S, x)$, respectively. The student probability $p_S$ is obtained via SoftMax (Equation (2)). The student first minimizes cross-entropy $L_{SCE}$ with respect to the true label $y$ (Equation (3)). Simultaneously, the teacher output is softened using temperature $\tau$ to get $p_T = \sigma(z_T, \tau)$ (Equation (4)). The distillation loss takes the temperature-scaled KL divergence $L_{KD} = \tau^2 \cdot KL(p_T, p_S)$ (Equation (5)). The total loss is the weighted combination of the two: $L_S = (1-\lambda) \cdot L_{SCE} + \lambda \cdot L_{KD}$ (Equation (6)). The $\tau^2$ coefficient is to compensate for the scaling of gradient magnitude caused by temperature softening, which is standard practice in the Hinton distillation framework.

**Traditional Ensemble Distillation (Equations (7)-(9))**: The logits of $M$ independently trained teachers (same architecture, same data distribution, different random seed initialization) are averaged and passed through temperature SoftMax to obtain the average softened output $P_E$ (Equation (7)). The ensemble distillation loss $L_{ED} = \tau^2 \cdot KL(P_E, p_S)$ (Equation (8)) replaces $L_{KD}$ in Equation (6) to obtain the total loss (Equation (9)).

**Simple Snapshot Averaging and Its Problems (Equation (10))**: Curriculum learning produces $M \times N$ snapshots ($M$ independent networks $\times$ $N$ curriculum stages per network). The most direct approach is to average all of them: $\tilde{P}_E = \sigma( \frac{1}{M \times N} \sum_m \sum_n z_{Tm,n} , \tau )$ (Equation (10)). The paper explicitly points out that this indiscriminate averaging "can often cause conflict"—snapshots from different stages specialize in different noise intensities, and the soft labels they provide for the same sample may cancel each other out.

**Weighted Stage Ensemble (Equations (11)-(12))**: The inner summation is grouped by stage. The sub-ensemble $\sum_n z_{Tm,n}$ of the $n$-th stage is multiplied by weight $w_n$, and then the $M \times N$ snapshots are normalized as a whole: $\hat{P}_E = \sigma( \frac{1}{M \times N} \sum_m w_n \sum_n z_{Tm,n} , \tau )$ (Equation (11)). The weight $w_n$ is determined by whether the SNR of the training sample falls within the main range $R_n$ of the $n$-th stage: it takes $\alpha$ if within the main range, and $\beta$ otherwise, with $\alpha > \beta$ (Equation (12)). In this paper, $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=0$ are finally chosen (determined by grid search, parameter settings in Chapter 3), which is equivalent to hard-selecting the subset of snapshots participating in the ensemble based on the sample's SNR.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Noise-Mixed Curriculum Learning (Proportional Increment Strategy)
The approach of traditional curriculum learning (used by ConvMixer) is to gradually widen the noise loudness range: training is divided into five progressively difficult stages. The first stage uses only clean speech, and subsequently, noise sets are added in steps of -5 dB ({clean, 0 dB}, {clean, 0 dB, -5 dB}, {clean, 0 dB, -5 dB, -10 dB}, description of ConvMixer in the Experimental Setup section). The paper's strategy goes the opposite way (comparison explanation in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2): **From the first stage, clean and noise samples are trained simultaneously, but the proportion of noise samples is increased stage by stage**, causing the training distribution to gradually focus on strong noise conditions.

The specific mechanism is driven by the SNR distribution $R_n$, which consists of a sampling range and a main range: the sampling range is fixed at [-15, 50] dB (-15 dB is the lower bound of the loudest noise, 50 dB is close to the upper bound of clean samples); each sample draws an SNR from the current stage's main range with probability $\rho$, and from the sampling range outside the main range with probability $1-\rho$ (to maintain exploration and prevent forgetting the weak noise end, $\rho$ is set to 0.9). The main ranges of the five stages narrow successively to [-15, 50], [-15, 10], [-15, 5], [-15, 0], [-15, -5] dB—note that the lower bound is always pinned at -15 dB, and it is the upper bound that narrows, meaning the proportion of louder noise samples increases (distribution illustration in Fig. 2). In the first stage, the main range coincides with the sampling range, equivalent to uniform sampling. Algorithm 1 gives the complete process: an outer loop of $N$ stages, $E_n$ epochs per stage. Within each epoch, for each clean speech, one noise is randomly selected, an SNR is drawn according to $R_n$, and a weight factor $\omega$ is inversely solved to synthesize a new sample—**samples are resynthesized per epoch**, data does not repeat within the same stage, equivalent to a continuously refreshed data augmentation stream. After training, the teacher snapshot $\theta_{T_n}$ of that stage is retained, yielding $N$ snapshots in total.

This design has two complementary "whys." First, why sacrifice weak noise performance for strong noise performance: Table III shows that the large network's clean accuracy with the paper's curriculum is 97.4%, lower than the 97.9% of random noise mixing and the 98.0% of traditional curriculum. However, at MUSAN -10 dB, the paper's curriculum achieves 83.9% (compared to 82.2% and 82.0% for the other two), and at -12.5 dB, it achieves 79.7% vs. 77.4% and 77.4%—in the later stages, pressing the training distribution towards strong noise comes at the cost of slight degradation at the weak noise end. Second, who covers this degradation: it is covered by the ensemble distillation in the second stage. The snapshots from each stage of the curriculum have natural division of labor—networks trained in early stages perform well under weak noise, and networks trained in late stages perform well under strong noise. During distillation, the corresponding snapshots are called according to the sample's SNR, and the student inherits the weak noise capability of early snapshots and the strong noise capability of late snapshots (mechanism description at the beginning of Chapter 3). This is where the two modules in the paper truly "joint" rather than simply serial: the stage snapshots produced by curriculum learning happen to be the ensemble materials for division of labor by noise intensity in the distillation stage.

General speech enhancement is superimposed throughout training (conditions consistent across stages): volume randomly varies between 40%–180%, speech position randomly shifts between 25%–125%, random resampling between 90%–110% changes speech rate and pitch, and SpecAugment performs masking in the range of 0–5 in the frequency and time domains (speech enhancement settings in Chapter 3).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Weighted Stage Ensemble Distillation
Traditional ensemble distillation requires $M$ independently trained complete teachers, with costs growing linearly with $M$. The paper's entry point is that curriculum learning naturally produces snapshots for free: one $N$-stage curriculum training yields $N$ snapshots with different parameters, and $M$ independent trainings yield $M \times N$ snapshots (in this paper $M=5, N=5$, totaling 25 snapshots, total parameters 7.5M, Table IV). The diversity of these snapshots is not parameter jitter caused by random initialization, but **division of labor** caused by systematic shifts in training distribution—the main noise range focused on by the $n$-th stage snapshot is explicitly defined by $R_n$, providing an interpretable basis for scheduling teachers based on samples (mechanism illustration in Fig. 3).

The details of weight scheduling (Algorithm 2) are worth expanding: for each training sample, all $w_n$ are initially set to $\beta$; if the sample's SNR falls within the main range $R_n$ of the $n$-th stage, then $w_n$ is set to $\alpha$; subsequently, the distillation loss is calculated using the weighted $\hat{P}_E$ to train the student. Since the five main ranges are nested and narrowing ([-15, 50] $\supset$ [-15, 10] $\supset$ … $\supset$ [-15, -5]), a sample with an SNR of 2 dB falls within the main ranges of the first three stages, activating only the snapshots of the first three stages; a sample with an SNR of -12 dB falls within all five main ranges, activating all 25 snapshots. This precisely implements the property claimed by the paper—"the dirtier the data, the more knowledge is accepted from snapshots of later stages; the cleaner the data, the more knowledge comes from early stage snapshots": clean samples are taught only by early snapshots (weak noise experts), and strong noise samples are taught by a larger set including late snapshots. Compared to the indiscriminate averaging of Equation (10), this subset selection based on sample SNR avoids soft label conflicts where "weak noise experts and strong noise experts disagree on the same sample."

Hyperparameter settings (Chapter 3): Temperature $\tau=5$, loss weight $\lambda=0.1$ (both common values in distillation obtained via grid search); number of stages $N=5$ (consistent with other curriculum learning research); number of independent teachers $M=5$ (larger $M$ yields higher accuracy but also higher learning costs); $\alpha=1, \beta=0$. The student training in the distillation stage is of the same specification as training from scratch: Adam optimizer, learning rate 0.001, batch size 256, total 4000 epochs (Experimental Setup section).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods
- **Vs. ConvMixer (Traditional Curriculum)**: The curriculum philosophy is opposite—ConvMixer starts with clean speech and gradually widens the loudness range, while the paper starts with mixed training and gradually narrows the main range to increase the proportion of strong noise. Data speaks (Table III large network): traditional curriculum has no gain at the strong noise end compared to random noise mixing (both 77.4% at -12.5 dB), while the paper's curriculum achieves 79.7%.
- **Vs. ImportantAug**: The route is completely different. ImportantAug uses a trained augmentation agent to estimate the importance of speech regions and injects noise only into unimportant regions, with a supporting model capacity of 24M parameters (Table I). The paper does not pick injection locations but relies on curriculum-style scheduling of training distribution + distillation compression, achieving 78.6% vs. 56.5% (Table II MUSAN column) with 27k parameters at -12.5 dB.
- **Vs. PKD**: The distillation signal is different. PKD constructs prototype distributions for distillation using distances between class centers and embedding vectors, solving the problem of network bias towards easily trained samples. The paper's distillation signal is still the logit-level temperature-softened distribution; the innovation lies on the teacher side—constructing a weighted teacher set with division of labor by noise intensity using curriculum stage snapshots, rather than modifying the distance metric of the loss.
- **Vs. Traditional Ensemble Distillation**: Both the teacher source and scheduling method are different. Traditional methods require independently training $M$ teachers and then averaging (Equation (7)). The paper treats the intermediate products of curriculum learning as a free teacher pool and does not use fixed-weight averaging (Equation (10)), but dynamically selects subsets based on the training sample's SNR (Equations (11)(12)).
- **Vs. Curriculum-Distillation Joint Frameworks in Vision and NLP**: Zhu et al. and Panagiotatos et al. proved the feasibility of the joint route in their respective fields. This paper is the first joint framework in acoustic modeling, specifically for noise-robust small-footprint keyword spotting (original statement of the paper's first contribution), and it makes "noise intensity" an explicit variable for teacher scheduling, a dimension unique to acoustics.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale
- **Speech Data**: Google Speech Commands (GSC) version 2, approximately 100k word audio clips, 35 different words (Experimental Setup section). The network is trained on the training set and evaluated on the test set; noise and clean samples are randomly selected and mixed at different SNRs to construct noise environments. In the 11-class output, the number of negative class training samples is more than 10 times that of a single keyword, so the negative class weight is set to 0.1 to prevent the model from biasing towards the negative class.
- **Training Noise**: FSD50k (an open-source dataset of human-labeled sound events), used only for synthesis during training, unseen during testing.
- **Test Noise (Four Public Unseen Noise Sets)**:
    - **MUSAN**: Uses the GSC-MUSAN construction method from related work. Only the first 1 second of each MUSAN recording is taken, and recordings shorter than 1 second are discarded (limit of sample length to 1 second). Then, 175 random noise audio files are selected and mixed with test set samples.
    - **QUT**: Takes the 40-minute living room background noise recording named HOME-LIVINGB-1.wav, randomly cuts segments, and mixes them with clean test samples. The sampling rate is changed from 48 kHz to 16 kHz to align with GSC.
    - **UrbanSound8K**: 8,732 city sound files not exceeding 4 seconds, 10 classes (air conditioning, car horn, children playing, dog barking, electric drill, engine idling, gunshot, handheld electric drill, siren, street music). Sources are field recordings from freesound.org. Usage is the same as QUT.
    - **WHAM**: i.e., WSJ0 Hipster Ambient Mixtures, composed of wsj0-2mix two-speaker mixed data plus real environmental noise. Noise was collected from public places such as cafes, restaurants, and bars in the San Francisco Bay Area. Usage is the same as QUT.
- **Training Scale**: Curriculum learning runs for a total of 4000 epochs, with the five stages sequentially running 2000, 500, 500, 500, 500 (the first stage takes half the budget, covering the full SNR range to lay the foundation). The baseline without curriculum is also trained for 4000 epochs to ensure fairness. Optimizer Adam, learning rate 0.001, batch size 256.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics
- **Accuracy by SNR Tier (%)**: Test noise is mixed at tiers of 40/30/20/10/0/-10/-12.5 dB (MUSAN, QUT) or 20/15/10/5/0 dB (UrbanSound8K, WHAM), and accuracy is reported tier by tier. The rationale directly corresponds to the research goal—noise robustness must be viewed through the accuracy curve under noise intensity scanning; a single average number would mask the collapse at the strong noise end. Furthermore, all evaluations are on **unseen noise** (except for ImportantAug on MUSAN, which is a noise condition observed during its training, i.e., more favorable to it, as noted under Table II). This tests generalized robustness rather than memorization.
- **Noise-Averaged Accuracy and F1**: Table III and Table IV report both the "average of accuracy across noise tiers" and its F1-score version. The necessity of F1 stems from the class structure—the negative class samples are more than 10 times those of the keyword classes, so accuracy would be dominated by the negative class. F1 reflects the balance of recall and precision on the keyword classes.
- **Model Parameters**: A direct proxy variable for deployment constraints (Tables I, II, and IV all use parameter count as the size column).

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods
Main comparison (Table II, student network 27k parameters, MUSAN column): The paper's joint framework achieves clean accuracy 97.1%, 40 dB 97.0%, 30 dB 96.8%, 20 dB 96.4%, 10 dB 95.2%, 0 dB 91.1%, -10 dB 82.3%, -12.5 dB 78.6%. Comparison objects: Directly training the small network (27k) with curriculum learning yields 93.1/93.1/92.9/92.3/90.7/86.1/74.5/70.7; ConvMixer (119k) only reports clean 93.2%, 20 dB 90.8%, 0 dB 83.0%, -10 dB 71.9%; ImportantAug (100k) yields 95.0/94.9/94.8/94.3/92.6/86.7/65.0/56.5. The gap at the strong noise end widens drastically: at -12.5 dB, the paper's 78.6% is 22.1 percentage points higher than ImportantAug's 56.5% and 7.9 percentage points higher than the 70.7% of directly training the small network with curriculum learning; at -10 dB, it is 82.3% vs. 65.0% (ImportantAug) and 71.9% (ConvMixer). QUT column (Table II): The paper achieves clean 97.3%, 0 dB 87.8%, -10 dB 68.5%, -12.5 dB 64.1%, while ImportantAug at the same tiers achieves only 76.5%, 38.7%, 28.0%—leading by 36.1 percentage points at -12.5 dB. UrbanSound8K column: The paper achieves clean 97.1%, 20 dB 96.4%, 10 dB 94.9%, 0 dB 89.2%, compared to PKD (102k)'s 97.7%, 96.7%, 93.8%, 82.3%—with only about one-quarter of the parameters, slightly lower than PKD at clean and weak noise ends, but surpassing it by 6.9 percentage points at the 0 dB strong noise end. WHAM column (Table II reports four tiers): The paper drops from 96.4% to 91.1%, while PKD drops from 95.9% to 79.4%, leading by about 11.7 percentage points at the last tier. Overall conclusion (Fig. 4): Compared to advanced methods, the joint framework improves average accuracy by about 8 percentage points and reduces model size by an average of 4 times; the text specifically points out 20 dB MUSAN 96.4%, 0 dB MUSAN 91.1%, 20 dB UrbanSound8K 96.4%, 0 dB UrbanSound8K 89.2%, compared to 92.3%, 86.1%, 92.5%, 82.5% when using curriculum learning alone.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments
**Curriculum Learning Ablation (Table III, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, MUSAN)**: Comparison of three training methods $\times$ two network scales. Random noise mixing (no curriculum, i.e., the approach of ImportantAug/PKD): Small network clean 95.7%, noise average 86.5%, -12.5 dB 68.9%; Large network 97.9%, 91.4%, 77.4%. Traditional curriculum (ConvMixer-style): Small network clean 91.7%, -12.5 dB 69.4%; Large network 98.0%, 77.4%. Paper's curriculum: Small network 93.1%, 70.7% (F1 drops from 0.82 to 0.81); Large network 97.4%, 91.4%, 79.7% (-10 dB 83.9%). Three findings: First, traditional curriculum also provides no gain for large networks (77.4%持平 random mixing), and is even more conservative at the weak noise end. Second, the paper's curriculum achieves 83.9%/-10 dB and 79.7%/-12.5 dB at the strong noise end of large networks, higher than the other two, at the cost of being 0.5 percentage points lower at the clean end than random mixing (97.4% vs. 97.9%)—confirming the design orientation of "sacrificing weak noise for strong noise." Third, even with the paper's curriculum, the small network only achieves 74.5% (-10 dB, Table III; text Fig. 5 paragraph writes 74.6%) and 70.7% (-12.5 dB), far lower than the large network—the capacity bottleneck is structural, and curriculum learning alone cannot fix it; distillation transport is necessary. Fig. 6's training curves supplement convergence observations: random noise mixing converges to 77.4% (-12 dB) at 3000 epochs, traditional curriculum converges slower and finally持平s, while the paper's curriculum continues to climb to higher accuracy.

**Ensemble Distillation Ablation (Table IV, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, MUSAN)**: Comparison of four distillation methods, each reporting teacher ensemble and student scores before and after distillation. Ensemble distillation without curriculum (5 independent teachers, 1.5M): Teacher ensemble -12.5 dB 80.1%, student 76.1%. Ensemble distillation with paper's curriculum (1.5M): Teacher 82.1%, student 78.7%. Stage ensemble (25 snapshots, 7.5M, Equation (10) average): Teacher 80.8%, student 76.9%. Weighted stage ensemble (7.5M, Equations (11)(12)): Teacher ensemble clean 98.4%, noise average 92.9%, -10 dB 85.8%, -12.5 dB 82.0%; Student 27k clean 97.1%, noise average 91.1%, -10 dB 82.3%, -12.5 dB 78.6% (F1 0.95/0.86). Three conclusions: First, any distillation is significantly better than training the small network from scratch (student 78.6% vs. small network's own training 70.7%/-12.5 dB). Second, curriculum learning raises both the teacher ensemble and the student (teacher 82.1% vs. 80.1%, student 78.7% vs. 76.1%)—curriculum and distillation have a multiplicative superposition relationship. Third, weighted stage ensemble is the only method among the four that is optimal in all noise conditions (teacher 82.0%, student 78.6%). The cost of distillation can also be quantified: the student is 3.4 percentage points lower than the teacher ensemble (78.6% vs. 82.0%), but this buys a parameter compression of about 275 times from 7.5M to 27k.

**Hyperparameter Exploration (Fig. 9–Fig. 14, all on MUSAN)**: Main range sampling probability $\rho$—the selected 0.9 achieves 79.7% at -12.5 dB, other values achieve 79.5% or 79.6%, the gain is small but consistent (Fig. 9). Main range scheduling scheme—the selected [-15, 50]/[-15, 10]/[-15, 5]/[-15, 0]/[-15, -5] is superior to alternative schemes (e.g., [-15, 50]/[-15, 5]/[-15, 0]/[-15, -5]/[-15, -10]), 79.7% vs. 79.1% (Fig. 10). Speech enhancement—superimposed, -12.5 dB rises from 79.4% to 79.7% (Fig. 11). Distillation weight constants—$\alpha=1, \beta=0$ raises the teacher ensemble from 81.7% to 82.0% (Fig. 12). Number of teachers—no ensemble 78.8%, five-teacher ensemble 80.1% (-12.5 dB, Fig. 13). Teacher scale—120k teacher 75.1%, 350k teacher 79.7%, 700k teacher 80.9% (Fig. 14). Overall picture: Except for teacher scale and number, the individual gains of each hyperparameter are in the range of 0.3–0.6 percentage points, indicating that the main gains of the framework come from the structural design (curriculum + weighted ensemble distillation), and hyperparameters are just fine-tuning.

**Extended SNR Evaluation (Fig. 15)**: The SNR of the four noise sets is pushed down to -15 dB. The paper's joint framework achieves MUSAN 66.8%, QUT 54.1%, UrbanSound8K 59.6%, WHAM 53.3%, compared to 57.2%, 43.2%, 50.2%, 43.9% for directly training the small network with curriculum learning—still stably leading by 9 to 11 percentage points under the worst conditions.

## Main Contributions
1. **First Joint Framework in the Field**: According to the paper, this is the first research in the direction of noise-robust small-footprint keyword spotting to jointly combine curriculum learning and knowledge distillation, providing the core finding—curriculum learning on a large teacher first, followed by distilling a small student, is superior to applying curriculum learning directly to the small network (average accuracy approx. +8 percentage points, size reduced by an average of 4 times, Fig. 4).
2. **New Noise-Mixed Curriculum Strategy**: Subverting the traditional curriculum design of "gradually widening the loudness range from clean," it changes to full mixed training, gradually narrowing the main range stage by stage to increase the proportion of strong noise, combined with a fixed [-15, 50] dB sampling range and exploration probability $\rho=0.9$, achieving 83.9% (-10 dB)/79.7% (-12.5 dB) at the strong noise end of large networks, superior to random mixing and traditional curriculum (Fig. 5, Table III).
3. **Weighted Stage Ensemble Distillation**: Treating snapshots from each stage of the curriculum as a free division-of-labor teacher pool, dynamically scheduling ensemble weights based on whether the training sample's SNR falls within the main range of each stage ($\alpha=1, \beta=0$), teacher ensemble 82.0%, distilled student 78.6% (-12.5 dB, Fig. 7, Fig. 8), all superior to ensemble distillation without curriculum and indiscriminate stage averaging.
4. **Advanced Comprehensive Results**: 27k parameters, single microphone, unseen noise conditions, -12.5 dB MUSAN 78.6% (Table II), leading state-of-the-art noise-robust keyword spotting methods (ConvMixer, ImportantAug, PKD) at all noise tiers across four public noise datasets.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method
- **Training Cost Transfer**: The framework transfers capacity requirements from the deployment end to the training end, at considerable cost—$M=5$ independent 4000-epoch teacher curriculum trainings plus 4000-epoch student distillation, maintaining 25 snapshots on the teacher side, totaling 7.5M parameters (Table IV). The paper acknowledges that larger teacher numbers and scales yield higher accuracy, but only gives qualitative advice to "select according to computing power limits," without providing an analytical trade-off between cost and benefit.
- **Significant Loss in Distillation at Strong Noise Ends**: Teacher ensemble -12.5 dB 82.0%, student 78.6%, a loss of 3.4 percentage points; the distillation signal is only the logit-level temperature-softened distribution, without intermediate feature alignment. The paper itself admits in the third point of the conclusion that new loss functions and knowledge transfer methods need to be studied to improve performance at strong noise ends.
- **Weight Scheduling Depends on Known SNR of Synthetic Samples**: Equations (12) and Algorithm 2 require the signal-to-noise ratio of training samples to be known (since samples are synthesized according to target SNR, which is naturally true during training). This mechanism cannot be directly migrated to scenarios where SNR is unknown. Moreover, $\alpha=1, \beta=0$ is a hard switch. The nested main ranges cause strong noise samples to activate all 25 snapshots while clean samples activate only 5, causing uneven changes in the number of teachers with noise intensity. The paper does not discuss the impact of this imbalance on student convergence.
- **Not Jointly Optimized with Quantization**: The 27.3k parameter student is still a floating-point model. The paper's target platform is microcontrollers with hundreds of KB of flash and tens of KB of RAM, but it does not report accuracy loss after quantization, actual RAM usage, inference latency, and power consumption—parameter count is just a proxy variable for deployment constraints. A floating-point 27k model may not be deployable on microcontrollers without an FPU.
- **Distillation Hyperparameters Taken as Generic Values**: $\tau=5, \lambda=0.1$ are directly taken as common values from distillation literature (confirmed by grid search), without specific search for strong noise tasks. For a task so sensitive to the strong noise end, the interaction between these two parameters and noise robustness is not ablated.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design
- **Inconsistent Internal Numbers**: Teacher parameters are written as 321k in the Experimental Setup section and 313k in Table III; the -12.5 dB MUSAN result is 78.6% in both Table II and Fig. 8, but written as 79.1% in the Conclusion chapter; the small network -10 dB in the Fig. 5 text paragraph is 74.6%, while Table III shows 74.5%. Although these are all last-digit differences, they indicate flaws in the rigor of experimental records.
- **Narrow Coverage of Hyperparameter Ablation**: The six explorations of $\rho$, main range scheduling, speech enhancement, $\alpha/\beta$, teacher number, and teacher scale are all done only on the MUSAN noise set (Sections A and B of Chapter 5 explicitly state "used MUSAN for the comparisons/exploration"). Whether the conclusions generalize to QUT, UrbanSound8K, and WHAM is unverified; the specific candidate ranges for grid search are not reported by the paper.
- **Uneven Coverage of Strong Noise Tiers**: In Table II, MUSAN and QUT are tested down to -10/-12.5 dB, while UrbanSound8K and WHAM only go to around 0 dB. The SNR grids of the four columns are not aligned, and cross-dataset strong noise comparisons rely on the extended experiments in Fig. 15, which only provide a single point at -15 dB.
- **Lack of Real Deployment and Real Wake Word Verification**: All experiments are based on GSC word-level data, with the negative class suppressed by a class weight of 0.1. There is no evaluation of false wake-up rate/missed wake-up rate on continuous audio streams, nor any end-to-end verification on actual hardware. For a paper targeting microcontrollers, this is a significant gap in the evidence chain.
- **Baseline Reproduction Conditions Not Fully Transparent**: The comparison numbers for ConvMixer, ImportantAug, and PKD are partially cited from the original paper settings (e.g., ImportantAug on MUSAN is an observed noise condition, while the other methods are unseen noise conditions, as noted under Table II). It is not stated whether the training budget aligns with the paper's 4000 epochs.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement
The paper lists three self-stated directions: First, adaptive operation for devices with different performance and microphone counts, incorporating hardware conditions into the joint framework, drawing on the ideas of hardware-aware neural architecture search—the current framework produces a single 27k student, which cannot scale according to device tiers. Second, evolving from "treating everything except speech as noise" to noise scenario understanding, e.g., identifying kitchen-related noise to judge cooking situations, thereby improving the recognition rate of cooking-related commands or making context recommendations, turning noise from pure interference into a context signal. Third, introducing more advanced distillation losses and teacher-student matching methods to compress the 3.4 percentage point distillation loss at the strong noise end. Beyond the paper, from an engineering perspective, there are several natural extensions: changing the weight scheduling from hard switching of $\alpha/\beta$ to soft weights that decay continuously with SNR distance, eliminating teacher number jumps under nested ranges; jointly optimizing with post-training quantization or quantization-aware training to provide truly integer-quantized models deployable on microcontrollers; adding false wake-up rate metrics on continuous audio streams; and making the number of curriculum stages $N$ and the main range scheduling itself learnable parameters (similar to the data-parameterization approach of dynamic curriculum learning), replacing the current manual design.
