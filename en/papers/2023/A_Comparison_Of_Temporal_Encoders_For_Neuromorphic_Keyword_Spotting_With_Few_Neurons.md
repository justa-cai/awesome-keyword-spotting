# A Comparison of Temporal Encoders for Neuromorphic Keyword Spotting with Few Neurons

- **Date**: January 2023 (arXiv:2301.09962v1, submitted 2023-01-24)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09962
- **Keywords**: neuromorphic computing, spiking neural network, keyword spotting, temporal encoding, time-difference encoder, disynaptic E-I element, device mismatch

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Speech is becoming the mainstream interface for human-computer interaction. The proliferation of voice assistant devices has made "always-on" keyword spotting (KWS) the first gatekeeper of the entire speech pipeline. The engineering context of this gatekeeper is unique: full speech recognition requires identifying thousands of words, resulting in high model complexity and computational overhead. In contrast, KWS only needs to recognize one or a few specific words, naturally reducing computational demands by an order of magnitude. Therefore, the industry standard approach treats KWS as a "wake-up switch"—it remains in a low-power listening state, and only wakes up the resource-intensive full recognition system upon detecting the keyword. This positioning dictates that the core metric for KWS is not peak accuracy, but rather "how many hours of continuous listening can be sustained per joule of energy."

Traditional KWS runs on MCUs or DSPs. Even when models are compressed to tens of thousands of parameters, frame-based computation (forward pass per frame, every 10-30 ms) remains clock-driven with continuous power consumption, creating a structural gap with the ideal state of "sparse events." Next-generation brain-inspired neuromorphic processors (Loihi, DYNAP-class chips cited in the introduction) offer an alternative physical foundation: in-memory computing, massive parallelism, and event-driven operation—neurons only activate when receiving spikes, with near-zero power consumption when no events occur. The sparsity of Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) on such hardware aligns perfectly with the always-on listening requirements of KWS.

However, for SNNs to truly excel at KWS on neuromorphic hardware, a deep problem cannot be avoided: **physical time is an intrinsic variable in the computation of such systems**. Frame-based deep models slice time into frames and rely on cache windows to move history into the network. In contrast, mixed-signal neuromorphic hardware operates in a streaming manner, where the sequence and interval of signal arrivals are themselves information. How to encode the temporal structure of speech using the fewest neurons and spikes—the design of the "temporal encoder"—has become the core bottleneck for resource-constrained SNN keyword detection. The introduction explicitly states that a meaningful goal for building resource-efficient neuromorphic KWS is to **minimize both the number of neurons and the number of spikes required for successful classification**, as spike generation and routing constitute the main portion of dynamic power consumption on processors like DYNAP-SE (Section III-C).

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper criticizes existing work for the mismatch between temporal encoding mechanisms and mixed-signal hardware. Existing neuromorphic KWS studies (references [3]-[6]) rely on different temporal encoding methods—buffering, transmission delays, finely tuned time constants—but these methods are **not optimal** for real-time implementation on efficient mixed-signal neuromorphic hardware. A breakdown of "why they don't work":

- **Buffering mechanisms**: Require dedicated storage units and clock-driven read/write access logic, which contradicts the asynchronous, event-driven philosophy of hardware. Buffering essentially forces streaming problems back into frame-based processing, incurring extra overhead for storage and addressing.
- **Dedicated delay lines**: On hardware, these are either simulated by cascading synaptic/neuronal stages (consuming physical units for every millisecond of delay) or implemented with dedicated delay circuits, incurring high area and power costs. The section on E-I elements (Section II-C) directly points out that dedicated delay mechanisms or dendritic simulations are "expensive to implement" on neuromorphic hardware, which is precisely what the E-I element aims to replace.
- **Finely tuned time constants**: Analog circuits suffer from device mismatch, causing natural dispersion in time constants for circuits of the same model. Requiring extremely precise time constants is akin to fighting against the statistical nature of physical devices, raising issues with yield and robustness.

A more critical methodological gap is that **these works lack controlled comparisons**. Each paper uses different datasets, different input features, and different readout methods, making it impossible to attribute performance differences to the "temporal encoding mechanism itself." The TDE and E-I encoding elements, proposed in recent years and both claiming to target low-power hardware, have never been directly compared under identical input and readout conditions. Engineers have no way to judge which to choose or what their true benefits and costs are. Furthermore, the time constants and weights of these encoders are largely set manually or via grid search. The parameter space for 180 TDE neurons or 174+156 E-I neurons involves hundreds of dimensions, making manual tuning infeasible—a lack of training/learning protocols is a common shortcoming for both methods (explicitly acknowledged in Section III-C).

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

This paper addresses a clean, controlled experimental question: under identical inputs (the same batch of formant spikes) and identical readouts (the same $\ell_2$-regularized logistic regression linear classifier, same spike count features), (1) Can TDE layers and E-I layers improve the linear separability of keyword categories beyond the level of the original formant channels? (2) Can this improvement generalize to held-out test speakers? (3) What is the spike activity overhead (a proxy for power consumption) for each encoder? (4) If effective on the training set but ineffective on the test set, where is the missing link (parameter heterogeneity, learning methods)? Essentially, this is an empirical study that moves "temporal encoder selection" from self-referential claims to an attributable, comparable framework, and honestly reports negative results.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall architecture (Fig. 1) is a single-input, multi-branch parallel comparison pipeline: spoken digit recording → formant extraction (spiking) → 32 input frequency channels → parallel entry into three encoding branches—one layer of TDE neurons (180 neurons), two cascaded layers of E-I elements (first layer 174 neurons, second layer 156 neurons) → each connected to a linear classifier (LC). Each classifier is a logistic regression model fitted using the LIBLINEAR solver in scikit-learn with $\ell_2$ regularization. The input is the **spike count** of each utterance on the neurons of each layer. Five classifiers read out: the original formant channel (LCft), the TDE layer (LCtde), the E-I first layer (LCei1), the E-I second layer (LCei2), and the full network (LCall).

The "why" behind several design decisions is worth expanding:

- **Why use formants instead of full spectrum**: Following literature [3], the paper extracts four "first" formants (the four spectral maxima with the highest amplitude) from 32 frequency bands, implemented using the MATLAB sine wave speech analysis tool. Formants are spectral maxima and are the primary acoustic features for distinguishing vowels in human hearing perception. Using four channels to replace the full spectrum performs a physically sound dimensionality reduction on the input information, allowing subsequent encoders to process only the "trajectory of spectral peaks," the most compact representation. The design motivation for 32 frequency bands comes directly from the design analysis of the neuromorphic cochlea (silicon cochlea) AER EAR (literature [17]), ensuring frontend compatibility with hardware targets.
- **Why 1 ms time bins, with one spike per bin per channel**: The formant trajectory is converted into an event stream—one spike is generated in the input channel corresponding to the current frequency position of each of the four formants within every 1 ms time bin (Fig. 2 provides an example, where green marks true positives correctly classified by all single-neuron systems, and red marks false negatives, intuitively demonstrating the inter-class similarity of the input data). This encoding preserves the millisecond-level temporal structure of the formant trajectory, which is exactly the object that temporal encoders like TDE and E-I can act upon.
- **Why three parallel branches instead of serial comparison**: The same data is fed simultaneously to the TDE layer and the two E-I layers, ensuring that any performance differences stem solely from the encoding mechanism itself, not from confounding factors in data or readout flow. The figure caption for Fig. 6 further explains the "bypass" design: each classifier receives the spike counts of the indicated layer **as well as all preceding layers** (e.g., the "1 E-I layer" configuration uses counts from 32+174 neurons, "1 TDE layer" is 32+180, "2 E-I layers" is 32+174+156). This ensures the readout always sees all accumulated information up to that layer, making the comparison between layers a fair incremental comparison.
- **Why stack a second E-I layer**: Each E-I neuron is a small spatiotemporal feature detector. The purpose of the second layer is to test the impact of longer effective time constants brought by **depth** (Section II-C)—a direct test of the design hypothesis of whether "delays can be accumulated through hierarchy."
- **Why use spike counts for readout instead of spike times**: Count readout is the easiest to implement on mixed-signal hardware (a single counter suffices) and minimizes the capacity of the readout itself, preventing the encoder's contribution from being overshadowed by a powerful readout. This is an intentional "weakest readout" strategy to isolate encoder quality.
- **Why tune hyperparameters on a different dataset**: TDE parameters were obtained via grid search on the Speech Commands dataset (literature [21]) (Table I), while the final classifier was trained on TIDIGITS. The paper explains this simulates a real product development process—feature extractors are tuned during R&D, and classifiers are trained on end-user data. This also avoids information leakage from tuning features and evaluating generalization on the same small dataset.

The simulation toolchain is separated by hardware affinity: the TDE layer is simulated using Nengo (literature [20]); the E-I layer runs on the Brian 2 simulator (literature [24]) using the teili toolbox (literature [23]), which provides simulated neuron and synaptic circuit models for the DYNAP-SE processor—Adaptive Exponential Integrate-and-Fire (AdEx) neuron models and Differential Pair Integrator (DPI) synaptic circuits. That is, the E-I branch simulation directly models the behavior of real analog circuits, including device mismatch, bringing simulation conclusions closer to hardware implementation.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**TDE (Time-Difference Encoder)** originates from the fundamental spiking motion detector in neuromorphic vision (literature [10], Milde et al. 2018). Each TDE unit (Fig. 3a) is a current-type leaky integrate-and-fire neuron (CuBa LIF), augmented with a "gain" compartment and two input synapses: a facilitatory synapse and a trigger synapse. The working mechanism is multiplicative gating: after the facilitatory synapse receives a spike, the gain compartment produces an exponentially decaying gain factor, written as $g(t) \propto \exp(-(t - t_{fac})/\tau_{fac})$, where $\tau_{fac}$ is the facilitation time constant; when the trigger synapse receives a spike, it generates an excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC), the amplitude of which is proportional to the instantaneous value of the gain factor at the trigger time. Thus, the current integrated by the neuron is essentially $w_{trig} \cdot \exp(-\Delta t/\tau_{fac})$ ($\Delta t = t_{trig} - t_{fac}$ is the time difference between the two inputs): if $\Delta t$ is small (two spikes almost simultaneous), the gain is still high, resulting in a strong response; if $\Delta t$ is large, the gain has decayed, resulting in a weak response; if $\Delta t$ is negative (trigger precedes facilitation), there is almost no response (Fig. 3b-d show responses for small, large, and negative time differences, respectively, and Fig. 3e provides the time difference-spike response curve). The key is that time difference information is encoded into a **burst of spikes**: the number of spikes in the burst and the interspike intervals both vary with $\Delta t$. This is a multiplicative gate that can be implemented with pure analog circuits—using physical charging/discharging to replace subtractors and multipliers in digital circuits. The cost is that **each pair of input channels requiring time difference encoding needs one TDE neuron**. Table I gives the parameters: $\tau_{fac} = 8$ ms, $\tau_{trig} = 2$ ms, two weights $w_{fac} = w_{trig} = 50,000$ (Nengo simulation units).

**E-I Disynaptic Element** comes from the same team's previous work on synaptic delays (literature [11]). The current for each presynaptic connection is the sum of two dynamic synaptic currents: one excitatory DPI synapse (time constant $\tau_e$, weight $w_e$) plus one inhibitory DPI synapse ($\tau_i$, $w_i$). The values in Table I are $\tau_e = 1.5$ ms, $\tau_i = 1$ ms, $w_e = 105$ nA, $w_i = -147$ nA—the inhibitory term exceeds the excitatory term in magnitude but decays faster. The resulting postsynaptic current exhibits a biphasic waveform: first net hyperpolarization (inhibition), followed by the inhibition decaying faster than excitation, flipping the net current positive, forming a **delayed excitation** (current curves I1 and I2 in Fig. 4b), phenomenologically mimicking postinhibitory rebound in neurobiology. Thus, "delay" no longer requires a dedicated delay line but is synthesized by the differential of a pair of time constants—replacing a delay line with two existing synaptic circuits. The time constants are set to produce an effective delay of approximately 2 ms, motivated by the auditory model in literature [3] (original description in literature [26]). Weights are tuned so that "at least four temporally coincident input spikes trigger one output spike" (Section II-C), meaning each E-I neuron is a four-event coincidence detector. What truly gives this layer representational power is **device mismatch**: the paper uses teili's mismatch function to apply Gaussian perturbations to the time constants and weights of all E-I synapses, with the standard deviation set to 50% (teili's default is 20%; the paper specifically strengthened it to 50% to "strongly emphasize" this effect). Thus, the 174 structurally identical neurons become a family of diverse spatiotemporal feature detectors due to varying parameters (I1 and I2 in Fig. 4b show different delays and amplitudes due to circuit non-uniformity).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Controlled Comparison Paradigm with Identical Inputs and Readouts

The paper's greatest methodological contribution is not a new element, but placing two existing elements into an experimental framework that allows for clean attribution: identical inputs (same batch of formant spikes), identical readouts (same logistic regression family, same $\ell_2$ regularization, same spike count features), identical data splits, with the only variable being the intermediate encoding layer. Coupled with "bypass cumulative readout" (each layer's classifier can see all preceding layers) and cross-layer unified permutation importance analysis (see Experimental Results), any accuracy differences can be attributed to the encoding mechanism itself. Prior to this, the numbers in neuromorphic KWS studies were incomparable—changing features, readouts, and data splits made it impossible to locate the source of performance differences. This comparison paradigm is no less valuable for the replicability of future selection studies than a new structure.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Treating Device Mismatch as a Source of Representational Diversity Rather Than an Enemy

The digital circuit approach is to eliminate mismatch (larger transistors, calibration). The approach for mixed-signal neuromorphic hardware in this paper is reversed to **exploit mismatch**: the E-I layer does not pursue parameter consistency but actively expands the dispersion of synaptic time constants and weights to $\sigma = 50\%$, allowing randomly non-uniform synaptic unit groups to spontaneously form diverse feature detectors. This corresponds to the judgment in neurocomputing literature that "neuronal heterogeneity promotes robust learning" (literature [12]). The accompanying topological design is also sophisticated: the maximum horizontal input distance $d_{max} = 3$ (each E-I neuron connects to adjacent 4 input channels, first layer 29 positions $\times$ 6 copies = 174 neurons; second layer repeats the connection pattern for each copy, $26 \times 6 = 156$). The TDE layer also adheres to $d_{max} = 3$, yielding 180 neurons (ordered channel pairs within 32 channels with distance $\le 3$). The motivation for the $d_{max}$ constraint is clearly explained in Section II-B: TDE aims to capture temporal changes **within a single formant trajectory**, and must avoid pseudo-activation caused by spikes from different formant trajectories triggering TDEs—limiting horizontal distance limits cross-trajectory contamination. The design of 6 copies is to make the "search space scale" of E-I roughly equivalent to the 180 units of TDE, ensuring comparability.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Spike Economics Evaluation with Power Consumption as a Proxy

The paper elevates "average number of spikes per speech" (Fig. 7b) to a primary metric alongside accuracy, for very engineering reasons: spike generation and routing constitute the main portion of dynamic power consumption in neuromorphic processors like DYNAP-SE, and thus a significant portion of total power. That is, even if an encoder has good accuracy, if it burns hundreds of extra spikes per speech, it is unacceptable in an always-on listening scenario. Incorporating a power proxy metric into encoder comparison is uncommon in KWS literature and directly shapes the paper's conclusion tendency (TDE has higher activity).

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

Compared to literature [3] (Coath et al.'s robust auditory perception model): that method relies on explicit delay mechanisms for temporal processing in SNNs. The E-I element in this paper synthesizes delay using disynaptic currents, and TDE encodes time differences using gain gating. Both are "delay-line-free" alternatives, and this paper performs a quantitative comparison between the two that [3] lacks. Compared to literature [15] (Wu et al. 2018 biological plausibility SNN speech recognition on the same TIDIGITS data): Wu et al. used 4, 16, and 36 feature extraction neurons to achieve classification accuracies of approximately 9%, 27%, and 61%, respectively. This paper re-examines the upper limit of this route using two completely different encoding mechanisms, formant inputs, and linear readouts, at an equivalent "few neurons" scale. Compared to KWS works running on digital neuromorphic chips (e.g., Loihi-class): this paper explicitly targets mixed-signal analog hardware (DYNAP-SE and its AdEx/DPI circuit models), so the evaluation incorporates device mismatch into simulation rather than treating it as noise to be eliminated. Compared to the same team's previous single-neuron spatiotemporal pattern recognition work (dSTC network in literature [14]): this paper expands the object from a proof-of-concept experiment for a single neuron to a population comparison of a whole layer and two mechanisms, supplementing the systematic evaluation under a linear readout framework.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The main dataset is TIDIGITS (literature [9]), following the setting of literature [15]: taking single-digit speech from all adult speakers ("oh", "zero" to "nine", 11 classes in total), including 111 male and 114 female speakers. The default training-test split of the dataset is used, divided by speaker group (male/female). Each digit's test/training subset contains 224 and 226 samples, respectively. The task is set as one-vs-rest binary classification: for each keyword digit, all keyword samples are used, and the number of non-keyword digit samples is divided by the total number of non-keyword digits (10) and rounded down for class balancing. Finally, the training set for each keyword consists of 224 positive samples against $10 \times 22 = 220$ negative samples, totaling 444 (Section III-B explicitly writes "444 samples in total"); the test set consists of 226 positive samples against $10 \times 23 = 230$ negative samples, totaling 456. Hyperparameter tuning uses spoken digits from the Speech Commands dataset (literature [21]), with speakers completely different from the final evaluation dataset. It should be noted that this scale is very small relative to modern KWS standards, and the acoustic differences among the 11 digit words (especially pairs sharing phonemes like "nine" and "five") are much smaller than in real wake-word scenarios, so the generalizability of the experimental conclusions is questionable.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **Accuracy**: Average classification accuracy on the training and test sets, with a random guessing baseline of 50%. Rationale: Accuracy is an unbiased core metric for balanced one-vs-rest binary classification; training accuracy specifically measures "to what extent the encoder linearizes category information," while test accuracy measures generalization.
- **Permutation Importance (Fig. 5)**: Defined as the decrease in model score caused by randomly permuting the values of a single feature (here, the spike count of a single neuron) (method from Breiman's random forest literature [27]). Rationale: It provides the relative amount of category information encoded by each neuron, directly answering "which neurons are worth reading out"—for a deployer who wants to minimize the number of hardware readout channels, this is precisely the neuron-level information value ranking. The paper evaluates permutation importance on the test set, using a logistic regression model fitted on the training data of all network layers.
- **True Positive/True Negative Proportion (Fig. 7a)**: The proportion of true positives and true negatives among all correctly classified samples. Rationale: To test if errors are biased toward one side (e.g., a degenerate classifier that only says "not a keyword"), ensuring that the accuracy number is backed by balanced discriminative power.
- **Average Number of Spikes per Speech (Fig. 7b)**: The average number of spikes generated by neurons in each layer processing a single keyword speech, with standard deviation error bars. Rationale: As mentioned, this is a direct proxy metric for dynamic power consumption.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**The baseline is direct linear classification of original formants.** Fig. 6 shows that without any encoding processing, using the spike counts of the 32 formant channels directly for logistic regression, the average accuracy for different keywords on the training set ranges between 79%-96% (random guessing 50%). This indicates that formants themselves are already quite informative features, but there remains residual linear inseparability—this is the space the encoders are meant to solve. After adding any encoder, all 11 keywords reach 100% accuracy on the training set (444 training samples are completely linearly separable, Section III-B)—no exceptions for the TDE layer, single-layer E-I, or two-layer E-I configurations. This is a positive result of the paper: **both types of temporal encoders elevated the category information to a degree of complete linear separability on the training distribution**.

**But the positive results disappear on the test set.** Fig. 7a uses single neurons for test set classification (neurons selected based on highest training accuracy): compared to single formant channels, individual components of the TDE and E-I layers show **no significant advantage**, and adding a second E-I layer brings no systematic improvement. The proportion of true positives and true negatives is roughly balanced, indicating that errors are not biased toward one side as a degeneration. Fig. 8 extends the readout neurons to a maximum of 10 (the first selected by highest training score, the rest by highest permutation importance), covering four keywords: "one", "four", "seven", "eight". Selecting a subset based on permutation importance does show slight improvement over single neurons, but TDE or E-I still show **no significant advantage** compared to using a subset of formant channels directly. At the conclusion level, the paper is unambiguous: complete separability on training data did not translate to generalization on test data, and neither encoder outperformed the "no encoding" baseline (Abstract and Section IV conclusions).

**External Reference**: The paper cites another SNN keyword detection work on the same dataset (literature [15]): when the feature extraction layer used 4, 16, and 36 neurons respectively, classification accuracies were approximately 9%, 27%, and 61%. This comparison illustrates both the overall difficulty scale of "KWS with few neurons" and highlights that the accuracy interval reached by this paper's formant frontend plus linear readout (Fig. 7a single neurons mostly fluctuate between 0.5-1.0 depending on the keyword) is not lagging on this research line.

**Spike Overhead Comparison (Fig. 7b)**: On almost all keywords, TDE generates approximately 100 more spikes per speech than other neurons. Looking at the coordinate magnitude in Fig. 7b (y-axis up to approximately 600), this is a considerable additional dynamic power cost. The paper cautiously points out that both TDE and E-I spike counts have room for compression through parameter tuning, but the directional difference is clear: given equal accuracy, TDE's power proxy metric is worse.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

This paper does not have traditional ablation tables, but the experimental design embeds three sets of ablation tests:

- **Depth Ablation (one layer vs. two layers of E-I)**: Fig. 7a and Fig. 8 consistently show that the second E-I layer brings no systematic benefit. This refutes the hypothesis that "accumulating effective time constants by stacking depth improves discriminative power"—without parameter heterogeneity and learning, depth merely repeats homogeneous detectors. This is a direct counter-evidence to the design motivation in Section II-C and part of the paper's "lack of generalization capability" diagnosis.
- **Readout Scale Ablation (1 neuron vs. 10 neurons)**: Fig. 8 shows that selecting a neuron subset based on permutation importance yields slight improvement over single neurons (selected by training score), indicating that permutation importance ranking does carry valid information; but the improvement is limited and does not change the major conclusion of "no encoder advantage."
- **Information Content Ablation (Permutation Importance, Fig. 5)**: In the TDE layer and the E-I second layer, the highest-importance encoders have importance values 3 to 4 times that of formant channels. This result is subtle: it proves that both types of encoders **are indeed extracting spatiotemporal pattern information from the input** (the expression in Section III-A is "affirming the intuition that these computational elements are useful for extracting spatiotemporal pattern information from input data"), but this information is concentrated in a few neurons and failed to translate into better classification on the test set—information is present, but generalization is not. This triad of "training separability $\uparrow$, permutation importance $\uparrow$, test accuracy $\rightarrow$" points the finger at overfitting caused by parameter homogeneity, rather than the failure of the encoding mechanism itself.
- There are two unreported ablation gaps in the paper: the impact of mismatch intensity $\sigma$ (50% vs. default 20%) lacks comparative experiments; the difference between cross-dataset tuning (Speech Commands for TDE parameters) and in-domain tuning is also not quantified.

## Main Contributions

1. **First controlled comparison of two hardware-oriented temporal encoders**: Under identical inputs (formant spikes), identical linear readout ($\ell_2$-regularized logistic regression, spike count features), and identical data splits, this paper directly compares the performance of TDE and disynaptic E-I elements in keyword detection, pulling previously isolated and non-attributable literature into the same comparable framework (Fig. 1, Fig. 6-8).
2. **An honest negative result and its attribution**: Both types of encoders can achieve 100% linear separability on the 444 training samples (compared to 79%-96% for direct formant classification), but show no significant advantage over the formant baseline on the test set. The paper attributes this to parameter homogeneity (TDE all share the same parameters, E-I parameters drawn from a Gaussian distribution with fixed mean) and the lack of a learning protocol, rather than the invalidity of the encoding mechanism itself—permutation importance (Fig. 5, head neurons of TDE and E-I second layer are 3-4 times that of formants) proves that information extraction did occur.
3. **Spike economics evaluation dimension**: Incorporating the average number of spikes per speech as a power proxy into the comparison (Fig. 7b), identifying the activity disadvantage of TDE generating approximately 100 more spikes per speech than other neurons, providing hard constraints beyond accuracy for selection on event-driven hardware like DYNAP-SE.
4. **Hardware-aligned simulation methodology**: The E-I branch uses teili in Brian 2 to directly simulate DYNAP-SE's AdEx neuron and DPI synaptic circuit models (including device mismatch with 50% standard deviation). The TDE branch uses Nengo for grid search parameter tuning (Table I) and completes tuning on a cross-speaker dataset—providing a reusable toolchain combination for "simulation conclusions as close to hardware as possible."
5. **Design experience of topological constraints**: Details such as the $d_{max} = 3$ horizontal connection constraint (preventing pseudo-triggering across formant trajectories), 6 copies to balance search space, and bypass cumulative readout (Fig. 6 caption) are engineering templates that can be directly inherited for subsequent similar comparative experiments.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **No learning, only setting**. All time constants and weights of TDE take the same set of grid search values, and E-I parameters are randomly drawn from a Gaussian distribution with a fixed mean; there is no gradient-based training in the network. The paper itself breaks this in Section III-C: to produce selectivity for the spatiotemporal structure of features for each keyword, "both types of encoders likely require non-uniform distributions of parameter values," and hundreds of parameters cannot be tuned by hand or grid search—learning methods must be introduced. This explains the core pathology of the lack of advantage on the test set: 180 TDEs sharing one set of parameters is equivalent to a family of completely isomorphic filters scanning all channel pairs, with inherently limited expressive power.
- **Temporal structure is lost at the readout end**. TDE encodes time differences in the **number and interspike intervals** of spike bursts (Section II-B), while the readout only counts spikes per speech—the temporal information carefully preserved by the encoder is flattened into a single number at the readout end. There is a representational mismatch between TDE's mechanistic advantage and count readout; the paper did not attempt time-sensitive readout schemes (e.g., utilizing first spike time or interspike interval statistics).
- **Input dimensionality reduction is too aggressive**. Only retaining the four highest-amplitude formants per millisecond and 32 frequency bands (Section II-A) discards discriminative information such as broadband transients and energy envelopes of consonants at the frontend. Information that encoders cannot handle cannot be recovered at the readout end—the upper limit of test set accuracy is partly locked by the frontend.
- **Boundaries of scale and task**. Conclusions only hold under the TIDIGITS digit word, one-vs-rest setting with approximately 444/456 samples. Whether the ranking of the two encoders remains unchanged under real wake words (arbitrary phrases, far-field, noise) is not touched upon.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **100% accuracy on the training set is itself an overfitting alarm**, but the paper does not report regularization strength scans, cross-validation, or learning curves to quantify the degree of overfitting, nor variance under multiple seeds/multiple mismatch samplings (mismatch sampling randomness was done only once, variance not reported).
- **Mismatch intensity, a key independent variable, lacks ablation**: The impact of $\sigma = 50\%$ vs. teili's default 20%, and the curve of $\sigma$ vs. accuracy are absent, while "E-I relies on heterogeneity" is one of the paper's core mechanistic claims.
- **Cross-dataset tuning introduces domain shift**: TDE parameters were grid-searched on Speech Commands and evaluated on TIDIGITS. The recording conditions and speaker distributions of the two datasets are different, so the tuned parameters may not be optimal, which could be a confounding factor for TDE's weaker performance.
- **Power assertions remain at the proxy metric level**: All power-related discussions are based on spike counts (Fig. 7b) and qualitative common sense from literature, without actual DYNAP-SE energy consumption numbers (not reported by the paper), nor benchmarking of Nengo/teili simulations against hardware behavior.
- **Narrow baseline selection**: No comparison with any non-neuromorphic small KWS models (e.g., DS-CNN class) or digital SNN frameworks, leaving readers unable to place the results in the broader KWS accuracy coordinate system.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The main direction pointed out by the paper's conclusion is **heterogeneity plus learning**: replacing homogeneous settings with heterogeneous weights and time constants, and optimizing these parameters using training methods to maximize detection accuracy for specific keywords (Section IV). Literature [12] (neuronal heterogeneity promotes robust learning) and [13] (impact of spiking neuron leakage and network recursion) are named as trainable paths to draw from. Specifically for the E-I element, the improved form is to upgrade from "sampling from a large number of randomly non-uniform units" to "optimizing the selection of non-uniform neurons and synaptic circuits for each E-I neuron"—i.e., moving from buying mismatch lottery tickets to directed orchestration of mismatch. For TDE, a natural extension is hierarchical optimization: which channel pairs are worth building TDEs for (connection sparsification), and setting $\tau_{fac}$ for each pair according to the dynamic range of target formants. Time-sensitive decoding (spike times, interspike intervals) is worth exploring at the readout end to fulfill TDE's mechanistic promise in temporal encoding. At the system level, directly interfacing the 32-band formant frontend with a silicon cochlea (AER EAR class) and measuring energy consumption and accuracy on DYNAP-SE is the final defense for this "few-neuron always-on KWS" route. Until then, the value of this paper lies in marking the starting point—both types of encoders are worth continued investment, but the more fundamental question of "where do parameters come from" must be solved first.
