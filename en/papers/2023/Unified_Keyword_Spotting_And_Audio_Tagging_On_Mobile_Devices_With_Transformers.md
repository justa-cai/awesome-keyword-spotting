- **Authors/Affiliations**: Heinrich Dinkel†, Yongqing Wang†, Zhiyong Yan, Junbo Zhang, Yujun Wang (Xiaomi Corporation, Beijing; † Co-first authors)
- **Date**: March 2023 (arXiv:2303.01812v1 [cs.SD])
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01812
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, audio tagging, vision transformer, bottleneck attention, on-device inference, mobile latency benchmark, pseudo strong labels

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the entry task for modern intelligent voice assistants; the entire interaction flow is triggered only after the user utters the wake-word. Its engineering nature imposes three hard constraints: First, the model resides on the device side, so the parameter count (i.e., model size) is directly limited. Second, KWS is an "always on" task; inference speed and floating-point operations (FLOPs) must be small because it consumes battery every second in the background. Third, wake-up latency must be as low as possible; users expect the assistant to respond immediately after saying "Xiao Ai Tong Xue." These three constraints have been repeatedly studied within the KWS community, but the Audio Tagging (AT) community is a completely different landscape—the paper points out at the beginning: to the authors' knowledge, no previous research has focused on the lightweighting and on-device computation of AT models. Mainstream work in the AT community revolves around achieving state-of-the-art performance on the Audioset benchmark. Models like CNN14 (76M parameters), AST, and AudioMAE (80M parameters) require GPU-level compute for fast inference and simply cannot enter always-on on-device scenarios.

This paper is the direct follow-up to the authors' team's Interspeech 2022 paper, UniKW-AT. The conference version first proved that KWS and AT can be modeled jointly: treating keywords as a special class of sound events, taking the union of the label sets (527 + 10 = 537 classes), and training jointly with binary cross-entropy. A single MobileNetV2 (2.9M parameters) achieved 97.53% accuracy on GSCV1 and 33.42 mAP on Audioset, while significantly improving rejection rates on out-of-domain negative sets such as Musan and VL106. However, the conference version had a fatal gap: the abstract explicitly admitted that "previous work did not consider the real-world deployment of UniKW-AT models, where model size and inference speed are more important than pure performance." The full conference paper reported no latency, memory, or speed numbers; "on-device feasibility" was merely an inference at the parameter level—which is precisely the first principle of KWS. What this paper aims to do is fill this shortcoming: it provides three truly deployable unified models on mobile phones (Unified Transformers, UiT) and measures latency on four mobile chips.

The engineering value of the unified model itself is also worth reiterating (discussed specifically in the paper's introduction): the output of the AT branch can be passed down to the Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) pipeline, with the potential benefit of enhancing noise robustness. At the very least, a UniKW-AT model can double as a Voice Activity Detector (VAD), producing two semantic outputs from one computation. This explains why it is worth paying the extra architectural design cost for "unification" rather than simply deploying two independent models.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

**The backbone of the conference version UniKW-AT is unsuitable for on-device use.** MobileNetV2 has 2.9M parameters; the paper measures its wake-up latency at 320 ms and single inference at 8.0 ms on Snapdragon 865 (Table 3). In contrast, the pure KWS model TC-ResNet8 has 0.06M parameters and 0.4 ms inference (Table 3). The size and speed differ by an order of magnitude. 2.9M is considered lightweight in the AT community, but under the standards of the KWS community, it is significantly too large. If the unified framework can only be deployed at this cost, it will never replace existing pure KWS deployments.

**Pure KWS models cannot backward-compatible with AT.** The paper used TC-ResNet8 for baseline experiments: it performed excellently on GSCV1 (96.72%, even higher than the publicly reported 96.10%), but failed completely on AT, achieving only 8.67 mAP (Table 2). The reason lies in the label structure, not capacity—the output space of pure KWS models contains only target words and a vague non-target bucket, and it was never taught to understand sound event semantics. This shows that "unification" must be solved at the task representation level; simply adding a head to a small model is not enough.

**The complexity of transformers in the AT community is unbearable.** ViT-like models are typically trained on a 10-second scale in Audioset, with the number of patches reaching N = NT × NF = 1212 (paper citations [17][20]). The computational complexity of a single transformer block is O(N²D + D²N), which is quadratic with respect to both the number of patches N and the model dimension D—the attention matrix for 1212 patches is itself a memory disaster. This is the structural reason why AT transformers cannot go on-device; it cannot be bypassed by simply shrinking the model.

**Transformers on the KWS side lack deployment validation.** Transformer KWS models such as the KWT series (0.6M–5.3M) and LETR (0.6M/1.1M) have good accuracy on GSCV1 (97.05%–97.85%, Table 2), but they only do KWS, have no AT capability, and this paper does not report the latency of these models on mobile devices (not reported in the paper), so their on-device feasibility is also questionable.

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

The core challenge in one sentence: How to compress the computational and memory overhead of a ViT-like transformer to a level that can be sustained in real-time on mobile CPUs at float32 precision, while ensuring that the dual-task performance of the UniKW-AT framework does not collapse. Specifically, this breaks down into four sub-problems: (1) The two quadratic terms of attention complexity O(N²D + D²N) (N and D) must be tamed simultaneously; (2) The 1-second training duration of KWS and the 10-second evaluation duration of Audioset are naturally mismatched; cropping the context will hurt AT performance, requiring a pseudo strong label (PSL) mechanism to partially alleviate this; (3) Wake-up latency has a hard floor—the patch size determines how many frames the model must accumulate at least before making a decision; (4) All component selections (activation functions, MLP expansion ratios, positional encodings) must be re-examined with inference speed and peak memory as the primary criteria, rather than following the default configurations of academia.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall design philosophy of UiT is "first find the root cause of the overhead, then apply the specific remedy." The paper explicitly points out: the embedding dimension D and the number of patches N are the main reasons for the excessive computational overhead of transformers (the two quadratic variables in O(N²D + D²N)). Therefore, the entire method revolves around two main lines: patch reduction to compress N, and bottleneck attention (BN-A) to compress D.

The architecture family is shown in Table 1 (Table 1): three models UiT-XS / UiT-2XS / UiT-3XS, with layers L = 12 / 6 / 4, unified embedding dimension D = 128, unified MLP hidden layer 384 (i.e., 3D), parameter counts 1.5M / 0.8M / 574k, 34 / 18 / 13 MFLOPs per second inference, and peak memory 7.59 / 4.10 / 3.15 MB (MFLOPs and Mpk are both calculated based on a 1-second input). Patch size P = 256 = 16 × 16 (time × frequency), with 2 attention heads per self-attention head. The benefit of this family design is that the three tiers of models only change the number of layers, not the width, sharing the training pipeline and components completely, making it convenient to select the deployment tier according to device档次.

The training pipeline (Figure 1) fully inherits the conference version's UniKW-AT framework: sampling from Audioset (AT side) and GSCV1 (KWS side), with Audioset samples randomly cropped to the target duration t = 1 s to align with KWS; a pre-trained machine annotator (MobileNetV2) estimates soft labels for the cropped segments, i.e., pseudo strong labels; each batch is composed of a 50% AT + 50% KWS ratio during training; the input Mel spectrogram (F = 64, T = 100) is mapped to D = 128 dimensions via convolutional patchify and subsampling, added with learnable independent positional encodings, passed through several layers of "bottleneck attention + MLP" encoders, mean-pooled, and then into a 537-class classification head, optimized with binary cross-entropy. That is, the task representation layer (label union, Speech mapping, BCE) is inherited exactly as in the conference version, and all innovations are concentrated in the backbone network and inference overhead layer—this is precisely the positioning of this paper relative to the conference version: the conference version answers "can we unify?", this paper answers "can we run it on a mobile phone after unification?".

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

Multi-head attention layer calculation (paper Equation 1):

$$A = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{D}}\right)V$$

Where K, Q, V ∈ R^{N×D} are obtained from input X ∈ R^{N×D} via linear transformations W_j ∈ R^{D×D} (j ∈ {K, Q, V}). The complexity of a single transformer block is O(N²D + D²N)—the first term is the overhead of the attention matrix QK^T and its multiplication with V, and the second term is the overhead of linear projections and MLPs—both quadratic with respect to the number of patches N and the model dimension D. This complexity formula is the starting point for all design decisions in the paper.

Patch geometry: The input is a 64-dimensional log-Mel spectrogram with a 10 ms frame shift (1 second = 100 frames). A 16 × 16 patch cuts out NF = 64/16 = 4 blocks on the frequency axis, and cuts out NT = 6 blocks per second on the time axis (100/16 ≈ 6.25, rounded to 6). Thus, one second of audio has only N = 6 × 4 = 24 patches—compared to the N = 1212 for standard 10-second training in Audioset, this compresses N by about 50 times, and the attention overhead of the N² term collapses by more than four orders of magnitude. The physical meaning of the 16-frame patch is that it pins the model's decision latency to 16 frames = 160 ms (under a 10 ms frame shift), which is the time resolution floor inherent to the architecture.

The training objective is multi-label binary cross-entropy (inherited from the conference version), where each output node is an independent sigmoid probability. Before calculating KWS accuracy, single-label post-processing is required: the model may output "keyword + Speech" simultaneously; a threshold γ = 0.2 is used to determine if a keyword exists, and only if it exceeds this threshold is argmax taken within the keyword vocabulary. Note that this γ is different from the conference version's 0.4 (adjusted on a new validation set in this paper, details of tuning not reported).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Patch Reduction — Time-axis Cropping plus Pseudo Strong Labels

Since time-frequency information is crucial for AT, the paper did not cut the frequency axis (NF = 4 remains fixed), but chose to compress the time axis: directly feeding 1-second cropped segments, letting NT drop from about 72 at the 10-second scale to 6. Why is this possible? Because KWS is inherently a 1-second task, cropping aligns the duration of both datasets simultaneously—this inherits the conference version's "random cropping" strategy. But the cost is clearly stated in the paper: limiting the context will hurt AT performance on Audioset, because the training duration (1 s) and evaluation duration (10 s) are mismatched, and the model has never seen long contexts. The mitigation method is pseudo strong labels (PSL, citing the authors' team ICASSP 2022 work [21]): using a pre-trained teacher model (MobileNetV2) to predict fine-grained soft labels for each 1-second random crop as the training target, replacing the systematic annotation noise caused by directly pasting 10-second segment-level weak labels onto 1-second crops. The conference version's ablation already proved the weight of this link: removing PSL caused AS mAP to collapse from 33.42 to 20.77, and GSCV1 dropped from 97.53 to 96.94. The paper uses the phrasing "partially alleviate," showing awareness of the residual misalignment. Evaluation-side matching processing: inputs longer than 1 second are cut into 1-second chunks, passed through the model segment by segment, and all output scores are averaged—this is also the reason why the method marked with ♦ in Table 2 is evaluated on Audioset in 1-second chunks (the paper notes this affects Audioset performance).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Subsampling Stem — Bypassing Memory Inflation of the First CNN Layer

The first layer (stem) of conventional CNN models maps the 1 × T × F spectrogram input to C × T/2 × F/2, where the number of channels C ≥ 16 (citing MobileNetV2 literature [22]). This means that just after passing through the first layer, the total memory of intermediate features is amplified by at least 4 times (16 channels × half resolution ≈ 4 times the input)—for on-device scenarios where the memory budget is in MB, this is the big chunk eaten up before even entering the transformer. UiT's approach is to merge patchify and dimensionality reduction into one step of "subsampling stem": using convolution to directly map input patches of size P to a low-dimensional space D, with D < P (here P = 256, D = 128, halving it). The essence of this step is changing ViT's original "patchify to D dimensions + subsequent compression" to "compress while patchifying," locking the upper limit of activation memory from the first hop of data entering the network. The peak memory of UiT-XS in Table 1 is only 7.59 MB (1-second input), while the parameters themselves 1.5M convert to about 6 MB in float32—the activation overhead is controlled to be of the same order as the weights, which is the direct return of the stem design.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Bottleneck Attention (BN-A) — Compressing D Inside Attention

The solution for the second quadratic variable D lies inside the attention. The paper's intuition is: the embedding of each patch in the spectrogram contains a large amount of redundant information—the acoustic content of adjacent time patches is highly correlated, and having all D dimensions participate in attention calculations is pure waste. Thus, BN-A reduces the dimension D to a smaller space U (U < D) during the self-attention stage, with all models uniformly taking U = D/4 = 32. In terms of effect, this is not a precision component but a pure engineering component: ablation in Table 4 (Table 4) shows that replacing it with standard self-attention (w/o BN-A), UiT-XS inference increased from 3.4 ms to 4.1 ms, and peak memory increased from 7.59 to 10.28 MB, while GSCV1/AS performance hardly changed (97.76/34.09 vs 97.75/33.76)—speeding up by at least 20% is "free." More interestingly on the small model side: after removing BN-A, GSCV1 for UiT-2XS dropped from 97.31 to 96.94, and for UiT-3XS from 97.18 to 96.71, indicating that for models with tighter capacity, the bottleneck structure actually has a slight regularization effect, forcing the model to compress redundancy and retain discriminative information through low-dimensional attention.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Inference-First Component Selection (ReLU, 3D MLP, Independent Positional Encoding, Family-style MAE Pre-training)

This set of innovations may seem insignificant individually, but together they reflect the paper's stance: in on-device papers, there are no "default configurations," and every component must be re-judged by latency and memory. First, the activation function uses ReLU instead of the transformer's usual GeLU, on the grounds that inference is faster and computational complexity is lower (GeLU involves transcendental function operations, which are costly on CPUs). The quantitative account in Table 4: after switching to GeLU, UiT-XS's AS mAP only increased by 0.03 (34.09 → 34.12), GSCV1 actually dropped by 0.07, but inference slowed from 3.4 ms to 5.7 ms (68% slower), and peak memory increased from 7.59 to 10.28 MB (35% higher)—the paper's conclusion is that it is unaffordable. Second, the MLP hidden layer takes 3D instead of ViT's standard 4D (384 vs 512), following the approach of LETR [11], directly cutting the MLP's share in the D²N term. Third, positional encoding uses time and frequency axis independent (decoupled) learnable embeddings—parameterizing the two axes separately saves parameters, and also prevents the time dimension embedding from coupling with the frequency dimension, which is more compatible with the usage mode of variable-length evaluation by chunk. Fourth, on the training side, a single checkpoint is pre-trained on the full Audioset using a UiT-XS via Masked Autoencoder (AudioMAE method [20]), and all related layers of the entire UiT family are initialized from this single checkpoint—the pre-training cost is amortized across the three tiers of models, and small models (2XS/3XS) borrow the large model's initialization to stabilize accuracy.

### Technical Differences from Existing Methods

**Incremental relative to the conference version UniKW-AT** (the most important comparison system for this paper): The backbone changed from MobileNetV2 to the self-developed UiT transformer, parameters dropped from 2.9M to 1.5M (about half), GSCV1 increased from 97.53 to 97.76 (+0.23), AS mAP increased from 33.42 to 34.09 (+0.67)—smaller and both tasks simultaneously stronger (Table 2). On the deployment side, from zero to one: the conference version had no latency numbers, this paper provides measurements on four chips, UiT-XS is about 1.6–2.4 times faster than MBv2, UiT-2XS about 3.6–4.7 times, UiT-3XS about 5.3–6.7 times (converted from Table 3 numbers, summarized in the abstract as 2–6 times), wake-up latency cut in half from 320 ms to 160 ms, and peak memory (Mpk, Table 1/4) is reported for the first time. The training recipe is also upgraded: Adam upgraded to AdamW with 20 epochs of linear warmup and cosine annealing, up to 800 epochs, adding SpecAugment, random shift, volume gain, and polarity inversion augmentation, γ threshold changed from 0.4 to 0.2, and full Audioset MAE pre-training introduced. It needs to be honestly pointed out: these gains are the result of the superposition of architecture, recipe, and pre-training, and the paper does not isolate their individual contributions (independent ablation of MAE pre-training not reported in the paper).

**Relative to pure KWS transformers (KWT, LETR)**: The latter only solve the single task of KWS, while UiT covers AT simultaneously with similar parameters. On closed-set GSCV1 accuracy, UiT-XS (1.5M, 97.76) is not the Pareto frontier in transformers—LETR-256 achieves 97.85 with 1.1M (Table 2)—UiT's claim is dual-task plus on-device latency, not single-point accuracy brushing. **Relative to large models in the AT community**: AST/AudioMAE (80M, 37.10 mAP) have higher accuracy but require GPUs; UiT-XS achieves 34.09 with 1.5M, exceeding Eff-B2 (13.6M, 34.06) which has 9 times the parameters, making it one of the few AT capability carriers available within mobile latency constraints at the time (Table 2). **Relative to pure KWS small models (TC-ResNet8)**: All tiers of UiT are slower than TC-ResNet8 in speed (3.4–1.2 ms vs 0.4 ms, SD865); the paper's positioning is that TC-ResNet8 represents the speed requirement line of modern KWS systems, and UiT trades a few milliseconds of latency cost for an AT mAP of 8.67 → 30+—buying unified capability, not the speed champion.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Training follows the conference version's dual-dataset configuration: the KWS side uses the common 11-class subset of Google Speech Commands V1 (V1-11), compressing the original 30 classes into 10 target words (Yes/No/Up/Down/Left/Right/On/Off/Stop/Go), with the remaining 20 words uniformly mapped to the AT "Speech" label, each sample 1 second long; the official train/val/test split is 51,088 / 6,798 / 6,835 samples. The AT side uses the balanced subset of Audioset: 60 hours, 21,292 samples, with the longest single sample being 10 seconds. Evaluation is split into two sides: AT uses the official Audioset evaluation subset (18,229 samples, about 50 hours); KWS uses the GSCV1 test set, where target keywords are 2,567 samples and non-targets are 4,268 samples. Segments exceeding the target duration are cut into 1-second chunks, passed through the model separately, and scores are averaged. Compared to the conference version, this paper removed three out-of-domain negative evaluation sets: Musan, VL106, GSCV2, and Xiaomi's internal real product data, narrowing the evaluation matrix to the academic dual-task backbone (see Limitations discussion for details).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Both tasks follow community standard metrics: AT uses mAP, KWS uses accuracy—ensuring direct comparability with literature numbers. KWS accuracy requires thresholded post-processing (γ = 0.2) because multi-label outputs have the ambiguity of "keyword + Speech" being activated simultaneously, which is a special step in the unified framework evaluation process; during training, mAP is used as the main validation metric, selecting the top-4 models on the joint held-out set of GSCV1 validation and AS balanced for evaluation—choosing mAP instead of accuracy for model selection is because it is more sensitive to the overall quality of the 537-dimensional output, reflecting the health of both branches simultaneously. The two new types of deployment metrics added in this paper are absent in the conference version: First, inference latency, measured in float32 precision on the CPUs of four mobile SoCs (Qualcomm flagship Snapdragon 865/888, MediaTek mid-range Helio G90T/Dimensity 700) using the PyTorch Mobile Benchmark Tool, with a process of 10 warm-up iterations followed by 1000 test iterations for averaging, input 1 second—warm-up eliminates first-time compilation/cache jitter, and thousand-average suppresses scheduling noise, which is the standard practice for mobile benchmarks; Second, peak memory Mpk (MB), calculated based on a 1-second input (Table 1). The reason for choosing these two metrics directly corresponds to the three deployment constraints in the introduction (size, speed, latency), upgrading the conference version's "parameter-level inference of on-device feasibility" to measured evidence. The paper does not report power consumption or false alarm/false rejection metrics calculated by the hour.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Table 4 runs three rows for each of the three models on SD865: the complete solution (Proposed), removing bottleneck attention and replacing with standard self-attention (w/o BN-A), and standard self-attention with GeLU (ReLU → GeLU), simultaneously reporting GSCV1, AS mAP, latency (ms), and peak memory (MB). The findings have three layers:

**BN-A is free speedup.** UiT-XS: 97.76/34.09/3.4/7.59 vs w/o BN-A's 97.75/33.76/4.1/10.28—performance hardly changes (GSCV1 diff 0.01, mAP diff 0.33), speed improves by at least 20% (4.1 → 3.4 ms), memory saves 26% (10.28 → 7.59 MB). The same pattern on 2XS and 3XS: 2XS speeds up 23% (2.2 → 1.7 ms), 3XS speeds up 25% (1.6 → 1.2 ms). And as described in the methodology section, removing BN-A from small models actually drops GSCV1 accuracy (2XS drops 0.37, 3XS drops 0.47), indicating that the bottleneck structure has a regularization effect on small models.

**The precision gain of GeLU cannot afford its latency.** UiT-XS switching to GeLU: AS mAP 34.09 → 34.12 (+0.03), GSCV1 97.76 → 97.69 (−0.07), but latency 3.4 → 5.7 ms (68% slower), memory 7.59 → 10.28 MB (+35%). 2XS/3XS follow the same pattern: mAP increases by at most 0.20 (2XS), at the cost of 60–75% slower latency. The paper's conclusion is straightforward: marginal performance improvements significantly slow down inference and increase memory, limiting their practical value—this is quantitative evidence that "activation functions are also deployment decisions."

**Component selection and model scale have no interaction.** The direction of the three ablation rows is completely consistent across the three models, indicating that the benefits of BN-A/ReLU do not depend on specific depth, and can be uniformly adopted within the family, providing ablation backing for the "one recipe, three deployment tiers" productization path. The limitation is that ablation was only done on one chip, SD865 (explicitly stated in the paper); whether the conclusions extrapolate to the other three chips is unverified; components such as MAE pre-training, 50/50 batch ratio, and positional encoding form are not ablated in the paper.

## Main Contributions

1. **First unified KWS+AT transformers for mobile deployment.** The paper claims to fill the research gap in "lightweighting AT models and on-device computation": three deployable models (1.5M/0.8M/574k), with the best UiT-XS achieving 34.09 mAP on Audioset and 97.76% on GSCV1 (Table 2), exceeding both task metrics of the conference version's MBv2 baseline with half the parameters.
2. **A combination of punches to tame transformer quadratic complexity:** 1-second cropping compresses the number of patches from the standard 1212 in Audioset to 24 per second (compressing N), subsampling stem locks activation memory from the first layer by making D < P (compressing the entrance), bottleneck attention U = D/4 compresses D inside the attention, ReLU plus 3D MLP compresses component-level overhead—four methods correspond to different links in the complexity formula and memory path, and are orthogonal and superimposable.
3. **First multi-chip on-device measurement benchmark for UniKW-AT.** Measurements on four commercial SoCs (Snapdragon 865/888, Helio G90T, Dimensity 700) on CPU float32 (Table 3), providing a speedup 2–6 times that of MBv2 and halving latency from 320 ms to 160 ms, turning the conference version's "on-device feasibility" which stayed at the parameter level into reproducible evidence (benchmark protocol: 10 warm-ups + 1000 average).
4. **Quantitative backing for engineering component selection using ablation (Table 4):** BN-A speeds up by at least 20% without loss of performance, and GeLU's +0.03 mAP is not worth the 68% latency penalty—providing a methodological demonstration for subsequent on-device transformer work that "activation functions and attention structures must be judged by latency."
5. **Open source** (github.com/Richermans/UIT_Mobile), including the complete training pipeline initialized with MAE pre-training checkpoints, with reproducibility considered good among similar industrial papers.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**Speed is still an order of magnitude slower than pure KWS models.** UiT-3XS is still 1.1–1.2 ms at the fastest (Table 3), while TC-ResNet8 only takes 0.4–1.1 ms; UiT-XS is 7.1–7.3 ms on mid-range chips, and whether this is acceptable for the power budget of "always on" is not supported by power consumption data in the paper. The unified model trades capability for resident cost, and the boundary of this exchange's validity on high-end and low-end devices is not clearly defined.

**AT performance ceiling is obvious.** 34.09 mAP is 3 points away from AudioMAE's 37.10 (Table 2), and training only used the balanced 60-hour subset; the mismatch between 1-second training context and 10-second evaluation is only "partially alleviated" by PSL (paper's original words), and the model has never learned long-term acoustic structures where events span the entire 10 seconds.

**Non-streaming architecture.** 10-second inputs are handled by offline chunking (1-second chunks) plus score averaging, not causal streaming inference; the 160 ms patch latency floor plus 1-second chunk granularity means this architecture is only barely sufficient for real-time product forms that require "immediate wake-up after speaking" (the paper itself lists low latency as one of the three deployment requirements). Attention itself is also not designed for streaming, and the gap with the WakeUpNet and streaming transformer product line is not discussed.

**Float32 CPU is the only verified form.** There are no experiments on int8 quantization, NPU/DSP delegates, operator fusion, and other real product deployment links; the performance gap between PyTorch Mobile's CPU path and vendor-customized inference engines is unknown, and the extrapolation of measured numbers to product landing is questionable.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

**Evaluation matrix significantly narrowed compared to the conference version.** The three out-of-domain negative sets Musan, VL106, GSCV2 are all deleted, and Xiaomi's internal real product data (Common/Difficult sets) are no longer evaluated—the biggest selling point of the unified framework (noise robustness, rejection of difficult negatives) is not re-verified on the new backbone, and readers can only assume that the architecture replacement does not harm these properties, which is exactly what needs to be verified. Conditional wake-up capability remains at the "logically feasible" level of the conference version, with no quantitative experiments done in this paper.

**Incomplete ablation and single-chip.** Ablation of BN-A and activation functions was only done on SD865 (self-stated in the paper); the contributions of MAE pre-training, 50/50 batch ratio, independent positional encoding, and PSL under the new architecture are not ablated—how much of the +0.23/+0.67 improvement relative to the conference version is due to architecture vs training recipe cannot be split. The γ threshold was changed from 0.4 in the conference version to 0.2, but the tuning process and sensitivity are not reported.

**Comparison settings have imprecise places.** The difference between the two rows of MBv2 baselines in Table 2 (33.42/32.51) is not explained; AT baselines are cited from the PSLA paper, and whether they include ImageNet pre-training is not marked; KWS transformers such as KWT/LETR are not included in latency comparisons, making the on-device competitiveness comparison incomplete; the KWS side is still closed-set accuracy, without the industry-standard false rejection rate at fixed false alarm rate or DET curves.

**Scale and diversity are not explored.** The number of keywords is only verified for K = 10; monolingual (English) academic data; the three tiers of models only change layers, not width, and the limits below D = 128 and L = 4 are not explored. The paper does not report total training time and MAE pre-training costs.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The direction given by the paper itself is to continue to find a better architectural point between KWS size constraints and AT semantic capacity. Based on the full-text analysis, I believe the following are more worth advancing: (1) **Access to full Audioset (about 2 million segments)**—MAE pre-training has already used full data, but supervised training stops at the balanced 60-hour subset; the PSL mechanism was originally designed for large-scale weak supervision, which is the cheapest incremental gain; (2) **Quantization and heterogeneous computing landing**—int8 quantization plus NPU delegate is the standard next step for mobile transformers, and the friendliness of the compressed attention matrix of BN-A to low-bit quantization is worth separate verification; (3) **Streaming transformation**—replacing 1-second chunks with sliding window causal attention, combined with streaming reading within patches, to further lower the 160 ms latency floor, in order to connect with real wake-up products; (4) **Replenish robustness evaluation**—re-run Musan/VL106 and real product difficult negative sets on the new backbone to verify that the core selling point of the unified framework has not been lost with the backbone replacement; (5) **Quantitative verification of conditional wake-up**—construct hierarchical test sets using logical combinations of AT branch outputs (e.g., "do not wake up when human voice music is present"), turning the flexibility selling point into hard numbers; (6) **Output layer stripping and multi-tier deployment**—inheriting the property of the conference version's BCE output nodes that can be physically deleted, cropping the 537-dimensional output according to scenarios, forming a deployment matrix of "width × output" together with this paper's three-tier model family.

Overall, this paper is a key step for UniKW-AT from "task representation innovation" to "engineering landing": it did not invent a new unified framework, but answered the three questions left by the previous version: "can it really run on a mobile phone, how fast does it run, and how much memory does it occupy," and gave a positive answer with a clean set of four-chip measurements and component-level ablations. Its methodological legacy—judging every component by latency, compressing N and D simultaneously, and amortizing pre-training costs through family-style design—is directly reusable for any work that wants to squeeze transformers into always-on on-device scenarios.
