## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Devices such as smart speakers, wireless earbuds, and in-car assistants face an inescapable physical dilemma: they play music or voice prompts through speakers while simultaneously using microphones to continuously listen for wake words. Section 2.1 of the paper provides a time-domain signal model for this scenario: $d[t] = u[t] * w + n[t] + s[t]$, where $u$ is the device's own playback signal, $w$ is the acoustic transfer function from speaker to microphone, $n$ is environmental noise, and $s$ is the user's spoken keyword. The task of the Acoustic Echo Canceller (AEC) is to estimate $w$ and subtract its effect on $u$ from $d$; the Keyword Spotting (KWS) system then identifies $s$ from the residual signal. Figure 1 in the paper depicts the entire chain as a trio: the echo canceller $h_\theta$ is controlled online by an optimizer $g_\phi$, and its output is fed into the keyword classifier $m$. AEC is a standard preprocessing step for almost all smart devices with self-playback, and KWS is the hands-free interaction entry point; their combination represents the most common operational state of smart devices.

The real pain point is not "whether there is an AEC," but "who the AEC is optimized for." Traditionally, adaptive filters (AFs, time-varying filters whose parameters are driven by online optimization rules) belong to the domain of signal processing engineers, with design goals focused on isolated signal-level metrics—such as minimizing residual energy or mean squared error. The toolbox consists of the LMS/RLS family (references [3][4][5], the classic lineage from Widrow to Haykin). However, the entire reason AFs exist in real systems is not "because the residual waveform looks good," but "because the downstream task performs accurately." The first sentence of the paper's abstract points out this misalignment: traditional AF design prioritizes isolated signal-level objectives and ignores downstream task performance, leading to suboptimal overall system performance.

Why do signal-level objectives and downstream performance become decoupled, and why is this decoupling becoming more severe in the deep learning era? Because once the downstream component becomes a deep neural network, its tolerance for residual distortion is highly nonlinear: a classifier might be completely insensitive to certain types of spectral residuals, yet extremely sensitive to others that have low energy but fall precisely within discriminative frequency bands. Section 5.1 provides the most convincing empirical counterexample in the paper: CT-Meta-AEC, trained for classification, achieved only 5.80 dB of echo suppression, whereas the original Meta-AF, trained purely for signal-level objectives, achieved 9.57 dB. Although the former has significantly worse echo suppression metrics, its downstream KWS F1 score is higher by 0.165 (Table 1, synthetic playback). Energy-based signal metrics cannot capture what a DNN classifier truly cares about—this is the cornerstone of the paper's argument and the direct evidence for its claim that "signal-level metrics are not effective descriptors of downstream performance."

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

Section 1 of the paper categorizes prior work into four routes, each with structural flaws:

- **Manual Task-Unification Methods**. Likelihood-maximization beamforming by Seltzer et al. [10], phase-based dual-microphone speech enhancement [11], and tightly coupled array processing with model compensation [12] were among the first to prove that "feeding downstream task knowledge to the frontend" can indeed improve performance. However, this approach requires complex task/model-dependent derivations for specific tasks and models, and must feed back downstream model feedback to the frontend in real-time during testing. The engineering consequence is that every time a downstream task or model version changes, one must re-derive the criterion and re-wire the feedback path, making it unscalable.
- **Decoupled Pipeline Approach**. Treating AF and the downstream model as two sequentially concatenated modules in a large DNN pipeline ([13][14][2][15], e.g., Beamnet, joint neural beamforming and ASR). While highly modular and allowing for replaceable components, the training scheme is complex and incurs high engineering overhead (evidenced by reference [16] in the paper)—tuning and coordinating convergence for cascaded trainable modules is costly.
- **End-to-End Approach**. Device-oriented speech detection [17], optimizing neural AEC with ASR [18], task splitting [19], and the work most similar to this paper's task: Cornell et al.'s "Implicit Acoustic Echo Cancellation for KWS" [20]. These methods bake preprocessing directly into the classification network, simplifying design and training at the cost of sacrificing modularity—AEC is no longer a replaceable component that engineers can understand and control. Cornell [20] is particularly noteworthy: it combines KWS with implicit AEC, nearly identical to this paper's task setting. The difference lies in how it couples echo cancellation inside the classifier, whereas this paper insists on retaining an explicit AF with online-evolving parameters.
- **Meta-Learning Approach (Original Meta-AF [1])**. Using learning-to-learn to directly learn the AF update rule from data, eliminating the pain of manual step-size and regularization tuning, which is an improvement. However, its meta-loss remains a signal-level self-supervised log-mse (Equation 5), still ignoring any downstream tasks. Additionally, many signal-level supervised schemes require oracle ground truths like clean speech or impulse responses, which are unavailable in real playback data.

In summary: Until this paper, "task-aware frontends" either relied on manual derivation plus test-time feedback (not general), end-to-end unification (not modular), or ignored tasks entirely (suboptimal). The empty spaces in these three corners are where this paper aims to position itself.

### Key Challenges to Address

How to inject downstream classification task knowledge into the AF update rule while adhering to four engineering bottom lines: no task-dependent manual design derivations, no downstream model feedback required at test time, no reliance on oracle signal-level supervision like clean speech or impulse responses (thus, in principle, directly trainable on real mixed data), and no increase in inference-time complexity relative to existing methods. On top of this, the solution must simultaneously support two realistic deployment scenarios: one where only an off-the-shelf frozen classifier (KWS that cannot be modified or retrained) is available, and another where sufficient data and compute allow for joint end-to-end training. Finally, the meta-learning scheme itself requires BPTT (Backpropagation Through Time) along the $L$-step optimization process, with gradients passing through the entire filter evolution trajectory, posing a naturally high risk of training instability; the scheme must converge stably in practice—Section 5.3 explicitly reports no training stability issues, and this "casual" statement is actually an important part of the scheme's engineering feasibility.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Motivations

The system consists of three separable modules (Fig. 1, Fig. 2):

1.  **Echo Canceller $h_\theta$**: A block-frequency domain filter implemented using overlap-save (the multi-delay block frequency domain adaptive filtering framework by Soo and Pang [25]), with window length $K=1024$, hop size $R=512$, and number of blocks $B=4$, with block steps of $K/2=512$ samples (Section 4.1). The filter coefficients $\theta[\tau] = \hat{w}[\tau] \in \mathbb{C}^{K \times B}$, meaning there is one complex coefficient per frequency point per block, directly mimicking the speaker-to-microphone transfer function $w$; the output is the echo-reduced signal $e[\tau] = h_{\theta[\tau]}(d[\tau], u[\tau])$. Based on $B \times K/2 = 4 \times 512 = 2048$ samples (approximately 128 ms at 16 kHz), the equivalent impulse response covers the length of typical direct sound plus early reflections for small devices. The reason for choosing frequency-domain blocking over time-domain FIR is practical: long impulse responses are implemented via FFT convolution in the frequency domain, which has significantly lower computational cost than point-by-point convolution in the time domain, which is also the standard practice for various practical AECs.
2.  **Optimizer $g_\phi$**: A complex-valued DNN acting as the "learned update rule" driving the evolution of $\theta$. It adopts the higher-order frequency-dependent architecture proposed by the same authors at IWAENC 2022 [23], with hyperparameters: group size 5, group hop 2, hidden 48, totaling approximately 32K complex parameters (Section 4.1). It operates per-frequency with independent internal states per frequency. The motivation for introducing cross-frequency groups is that real acoustic transfer functions are highly correlated between adjacent frequency bins; purely per-frequency independent updates (the approach of classic LMS) waste this structural information. The group mechanism allows the update for frequency $k$ to see gradients and errors from neighboring frequencies, effectively embedding a "spectral smoothing" prior into the optimizer.
3.  **Keyword Classifier $m$**: Based on the model by Cornell et al. [20], taking 40-dimensional log-mel filterbank features as input (STFT with 512-point window, 256-point hop). The main body consists of 3 residual blocks, each structured as: 1×1 Conv → LayerNorm → ReLU → Dilated Conv (kernel width 5) → LayerNorm → ReLU → 1×1 Conv. The last residual block averages over time, followed by a fully connected layer and softmax to output the class distribution (Section 4.2, Fig. 2). All KWS models have approximately 300K parameters and about 20 MFLOPs per second—this is the normal magnitude for edge-side wake-word models. Trained with binary cross-entropy.

The core of the design motivation can be summarized in one sentence: **Maintain modularity while feeding task knowledge in via gradients.** The caption for Fig. 2 specifically emphasizes that "only AEC parameters change at test time"—among the three modules, only $\theta$ evolves online over time, while the weights of $g_\phi$ and $m$ are all frozen at test time. This means AEC remains the replaceable component engineers recognize (changing the optimizer network is equivalent to changing the adaptive algorithm, while the filter structure and signal flow remain unchanged), and the classifier remains the classifier; classification loss only intervenes as a gradient source during training. This simultaneously avoids the "derivation hell" of the manual unification camp and the "module dissolution" of the end-to-end camp.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

**Layer 1: Additive Update Framework for AF (Equation 1)**. $\theta[\tau + 1] = \theta[\tau] + \Delta[\tau]$. Any function that produces an update amount $\Delta[\tau]$ can serve as an adaptive algorithm, opening the door to "replacing LMS/RLS with neural networks" and allowing BPTT to pass directly through the update process.

**Layer 2: Meta-AF Meta-Learning Objective (Equation 2)**. $\hat{\phi} = \arg\min_\phi \mathbb{E}_D[\mathcal{L}_M(g_\phi, \mathcal{L}(h_\theta, \cdots))]$. Note that the optimized variable is the optimizer parameter $\phi$, not the filter coefficients—this is a bi-level structure: the inner level is the evolution of filter coefficients over time driven by $g_\phi$, and the outer level evaluates "how good this driving rule is" over the dataset distribution $D$. Learning is completed by unfolding a finite number of steps ($L$ optimization steps) via BPTT and using the meta-optimizer Adam.

**Layer 3: Per-Frequency Optimizer Interface (Equations 3, 4)**. $(\Delta_k[\tau], \psi_k[\tau+1]) = g_\phi(\xi_k[\tau], \psi_k[\tau])$, $\theta_k[\tau+1] = \theta_k[\tau] + \Delta_k[\tau]$, where the subscript $k$ indexes the $K$ frequencies. The same $g_\phi$ shares parameters across all frequencies, with each frequency maintaining independent internal states $\psi_k$—parameter sharing allows the model to serve all 513 frequency bins with only 32K complex parameters. The complexity is "one small network forward pass per block per frequency," which is naturally parallelizable. The input $\xi_k[\tau] = [\nabla_k[\tau], u_k[\tau], d_k[\tau], e_k[\tau], y_k[\tau]]$ is a quintuple: current filter gradient, far-end reference signal, near-end mixture, error signal, and filter output. This input design is sophisticated: it feeds all available statistics derived manually in classic adaptive filtering theory (gradients, errors, reference signals) directly into the network, allowing the network to learn smarter combinations than fixed step sizes or Kalman gains—equivalent to "learning on the shoulders of LMS and RLS."

**Layer 4: Self-Supervised Log-MSE Meta-Loss (Equation 5)**. $\mathcal{L}_M(\bar{e}) = \ln \mathbb{E}[\|\bar{e}[\tau]\|^2]$, where $\bar{e}$ is the concatenation of $L$ frames of output. Intuitively, it encourages the optimizer to minimize AF output energy as much as possible, without requiring oracle echo ($u * w$) or oracle speech ($s$)—because during training, echo energy is usually much larger than keyword energy (when SER is as low as −25 dB, echo energy is about 316 times that of the keyword), so minimizing output energy largely means suppressing echo. Taking the logarithm pulls the loss from an energy scale back to a trainable range, making it smoother for samples of different loudness. This design brings a key freedom: **training is possible without ground truth, and real mixed data is theoretically usable**. However, its flaw is equally obvious—energy minimization does not distinguish between suppressing echo and suppressing speech, which is precisely the root cause why pure signal-level training suffers in downstream tasks.

**Layer 5: The Paper's Joint Loss $\mathcal{L}_{CM}$ (Equation 6)**. $\mathcal{L}_{CM}(\bar{e}, c, \hat{c}) = \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_C(c, \hat{c}) + (1-\lambda) \cdot \mathcal{L}_M(\bar{e})$, where $\hat{c} = m(\cdot)$ is the classifier prediction, and $\lambda \in [0,1]$ is the weight. The paper sets $\lambda = 0.5$ (Section 4.1), while the original Meta-AF is equivalent to $\lambda = 0$. The paper's description is: this loss **implicitly couples** the representations learned by $g_\phi$ and $m$ without explicitly sharing parameters, borrowing the idea of task-splitting [19][20]. Mechanistically, retaining the $(1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}_M$ term is a necessary anchor: if there were only classification loss, the meta-optimizer might learn "non-physical outputs tailored for this classifier"—for example, shaping the residual signal to the classifier's most comfortable form regardless of whether echo suppression itself is physically reasonable; the self-supervised term constrains the solution to the physically reasonable manifold of "suppressing echo," with gradients from the two losses balancing each other, ultimately yielding an update rule that "bypasses the classifier's pain points while suppressing echo."

**Layer 6: Two Training Modes (Equations 7, 8)**. Frozen classifier mode (Section 3.2): $\hat{\phi} = \arg\min_\phi \mathbb{E}_D[\mathcal{L}_{CM}(m, g_\phi, \mathcal{L}(h_\theta, \cdots))]$, where the classifier $m$ is frozen and only the optimizer is trained; the classifier appears only as a component of the loss function with zero modifications to itself. Joint training mode (Section 3.3): $\hat{\phi}, \hat{\varphi} = \arg\min_{\phi,\varphi} \mathbb{E}_D[\mathcal{L}_{CM}(m_\varphi, g_\phi, \cdots)]$, where both the optimizer and classifier are adjusted by classification feedback—the optimizer learns to produce outputs more favorable for classification, while the classifier simultaneously learns to adapt to the optimizer's outputs, achieving bidirectional co-adaptation. Training details (Section 4.1): Adam, batch size 16, learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, $\beta_1 = 0.99$, truncated length $L$ randomized, with gradient clipping; learning rate halved if validation set shows no improvement for 10 epochs, training stops if no improvement for 30 epochs. In frozen classifier experiments, $\phi$ is trained from scratch; in joint training, the best pre-trained $\phi$ is used for initialization. The random truncation of $L$ deserves a separate mention: it controls both the computational cost of BPTT and generalization to long sequences—training playback is only 3 seconds, but testing runs for 4 seconds and 12 seconds. Random truncation forces the optimizer to perform stably on trajectories of any length, and the strong results for 12-second testing (see below) corroborate this.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Meta-Loss $\mathcal{L}_{CM}$ with Joint Classification Feedback and Self-Supervision

This is the core innovation of the paper and the source of the name CT-Meta-AF (classification-trained Meta-AF). Its essence is implementing "downstream task awareness" as a **gradient path during training** rather than a signal path at test time: the gradient of the classification loss $\mathcal{L}_C$ with respect to the optimizer parameters $\phi$ is backpropagated through the classifier and the entire filter evolution trajectory back to $g_\phi$, thereby directly telling the optimizer "whether each update amount $\Delta$ you produce ultimately leads to correct or incorrect classification." In contrast to the manual unification of the Seltzer era [10]—which requires humans to derive dedicated criteria for specific tasks and models and continuously feed back downstream feedback at test time—here, "unification" is completed automatically by automatic differentiation, being task-agnostic, model-agnostic, and requiring zero feedback at test time. The direct realization of this innovation in experiments is Table 1: with the same architecture and same training pipeline, simply changing the loss from $\lambda=0$ to $\lambda=0.5$, the F1 score on synthetic playback rose from .335 (.340) to .500 (.508), and on real playback from .226 (.236) to .317 (.326).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Frozen Classifier Mode—Customization for Off-the-Shelf KWS without Modification

The mode corresponding to Equation 7 targets the most common real-world constraint: manufacturers receive KWS as third-party IP or solidified firmware, which cannot be modified internally or retrained, but they have playback data from their own devices. In this mode, CT-Meta-AF trains only the 32K complex-parameter optimizer, leaving the classifier intact. The paper specifically points out that this "automatically accomplishes the model unification proposed by Seltzer et al. [10], and in a model- and task-agnostic way"—meaning customization occurs on the optimizer side, and the classifier doesn't even need to know that the frontend has been customized. The practical value of this direction is further amplified in the specialized experiments in Table 2 (see ablation section): an optimizer customized for a specific keyword set, even if trained on only one-third of the data, can outperform a general optimizer trained on the full dataset for the corresponding task.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Joint Training Mode—Co-evolution of Optimizer and Classifier

Equation 8 corresponds to the mode with sufficient data and compute. There are three key engineering decisions. First, initialization strategy: $\phi$ is initialized with pre-trained weights, and the classifier is first pre-trained on data without playback and then fine-tuned on AEC outputs (Section 5.3), starting from a good point to avoid the two modules dragging each other into bad solutions. Second, fairness control: when jointly training with Diag. KF or Meta-AEC, the pre-trained $\phi$ is not updated, only KWS is trained (Section 4.1), ensuring that the comparison isolates only the variable of "optimizer customization brought by classification training." Third, the paper leaves an explicit extension hook in Section 3.3: allowing the classifier to use information other than the output of $h_\theta$ (e.g., directly observing the mixed signal $d$) can further improve performance—this aspect is not explored in the paper's experiments, left for future work. In terms of results (Table 3), JCT-Meta-AEC in joint training is .008 F1 higher than Meta-AEC on real playback (p-value .01, average of three trials), and the gap widens to 0.057 on 12-second long sequences (p-value .0005), with no stability issues throughout the training process.

### Key Technical Innovation 4 (Experimental Methodology Level): Clean Single-Variable Controls and "Charitable Baselines"

A design detail that is easily overlooked but worth mentioning separately: CT-Meta-AF and the Meta-AF baseline use the exact same higher-order architecture [23] and training scheme, with the only difference being $\lambda$ in the loss (0.5 vs. 0)—thus, the entire Table 1 and Table 3 constitute a strict single-variable ablation, cleanly isolating the effect of the single factor "classification training." Meanwhile, the traditional baseline Diag. KF (block Kalman AEC [24]) is not run with default parameters, but is the strongest version **after grid-search tuning based on KWS accuracy** (Section 4.1)—after giving the baseline a green light for task-aware tuning, it still loses to CT-Meta-AF (Table 1 synthetic .196 vs. .500), making the conclusion far more persuasive than scoring points with a weakened baseline. The two anchors, No-Echo (no playback environment, upper bound) and No-AEC (playback present but not cancelled, lower bound), also clearly define the task's difficulty and the room for improvement.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

-   **Vs. Manual Task-Unification Methods [10][11][12]**: Both pursue task awareness, but this paper uses automatic differentiation to replace manual derivation and training-time gradients to replace test-time feedback, achieving zero dependency on tasks and models; the cost is the need for labeled classification data for training (manual methods can be label-free using likelihood criteria).
-   **Vs. Decoupled Pipelines [13][14][2][15]**: Both maintain modularity, but this paper does not require coordinating complex training schemes for two modules—the classifier can be frozen throughout; the difference lies in the fact that this paper's AF parameters are online-adaptive, rather than fixed-weight neural preprocessing at inference time.
-   **Vs. End-to-End Approaches [17][18][19][20]**: Cornell [20] implicitly couples AEC into the KWS network, which is simple to design and train but makes the frontend inseparable; this paper does the opposite—explicitly retaining the AF structure (only $\theta$ evolves at test time, Fig. 2), with task knowledge implicitly coupling the representations of the two modules via $\mathcal{L}_{CM}$ **without sharing any parameters**. One can say this paper stands between the two camps: structurally belonging to the modular camp, while enjoying the simplicity of the end-to-end camp in training.
-   **Vs. Original Meta-AF [1]**: Architecture, BPTT training, and self-supervised log-mse are all inherited, with the only change being the addition of downstream classification loss to the meta-loss. The inheritance is intentional—this allows the "value of classification training" to be precisely measured, and makes CT-Meta-AF a direct drop-in replacement upgrade for Meta-AF (the paper emphasizes no additional tuning is required).
-   **Vs. Methods Requiring Oracle Supervision**: Neither component of $\mathcal{L}_{CM}$ requires clean speech or impulse responses, and in principle, it can be trained directly on real mixed data—this is a deployment advantage relative to all "clean target" supervised methods (although this paper's experiments have not yet realized this, training still uses synthetic data, see Limitations).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Keywords are from Google Speech Commands V2 [21], sampled at 16 kHz; playback data is from the Microsoft AEC Challenge [22], where real playback is used only for evaluation, and all training uses synthetic playback (Section 4.3). The construction protocol for training mixtures: playback is cropped to 3 seconds, randomly mixed with keywords, zero-padded if necessary, subjected to random time shifts, and the Signal-to-Echo Ratio (SER, energy ratio of keyword speech to echo) is uniformly randomly sampled between −25 dB and 0 dB, with each keyword used once. Test playback is cropped to two lengths: 4 seconds or 12 seconds. Evaluation uses three subsets: the full set of 35 classes, and two smaller subsets of 10 classes (approximately one-third scale) and 2 classes (approximately one-tenth scale); fixed folds are used, and folds are never mixed across boundaries. An SER as low as −25 dB means echo energy can be up to about 316 times that of the keyword, deliberately maximizing task difficulty—the No-AEC baseline's F1 dropping to .087 (.098) in Table 1 is a direct manifestation of this difficulty. The specific total number of samples, total duration, and which keywords were selected for the 10-class and 2-class subsets are not reported in the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The primary metric is macro F1, with micro F1 provided in parentheses. The rationale stated in the paper is straightforward: KWS datasets suffer from class imbalance, and macro averaging by class ensures that rare classes receive their due weight, while micro averaging reflects overall sample-level performance; reporting both allows one to see both "overall accuracy" and "inter-class fairness." Accuracy is not used because, under class imbalance, it would be artificially inflated by the majority class. Streaming detection metrics like false alarm rate/hit rate (which are standard metrics for product-level KWS) are not used; this paper's protocol is clip-level multi-class classification—this is a noticeable gap between evaluation design and real-world deployment. Additionally, Section 5.1 supplements the comparison with echo suppression amounts in decibels (5.80 vs. 9.57 dB) as a signal-level reference, to support the argument that "signal-level metrics are decoupled from downstream performance."

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Pre-trained Frozen KWS Scenario (Table 1, SER ∈ [−25, 0] dB, format: macro F1 (micro F1))**. Synthetic playback: No-Echo upper bound .928 (.935), No-AEC lower bound .087 (.098), Diag. KF .196 (.208), Meta-AEC .335 (.340), CT-Meta-AEC .500 (.508). Real playback: No-Echo .931 (.936), No-AEC .102 (.117), Diag. KF .165 (.180), Meta-AEC .226 (.236), CT-Meta-AEC .317 (.326). Three-layer reading: First, the collapse from upper bound to lower bound (synthetic .928 to .087) proves the task construction is sufficiently difficult, establishing the value of AEC; Second, the improvement of CT over Meta is 0.165 F1 on synthetic playback and 0.091 F1 on real playback, and CT is the best overall on real playback—real data is a pure out-of-domain evaluation (training is entirely synthetic), and maintaining a large advantage in-domain indicates that the learned update rules have real generalization; Third, the traditional SOTA Diag. KF, bolstered by grid search, is significantly outperformed by CT in both playback types (.196 vs. .500, .165 vs. .317). **Secondary Evaluation on 12-Second Long Playback** (main text of Section 5.1): CT is 0.279 F1 higher than Meta on synthetic playback and 0.119 F1 higher on real playback—the gap is further widened compared to the 4-second results (0.165/0.091); the complete table is not reported by the paper. **Signal-Level Counterexample**: CT achieves only 5.80 dB echo suppression, while the original Meta-AF achieves 9.57 dB, yet the former is comprehensively better downstream. The authors' interpretation is: this exposes the limitations of signal-level metrics as descriptors of downstream performance, especially when the downstream component is nonlinear processing (DNN classifier); conversely, CT's optimizer learns to "arrange residuals to be harmless to the classifier," rather than "minimizing residual energy to the lowest level."

**Joint Training KWS Scenario (Table 3, asterisks mark average of three independent trials)**. Synthetic playback: No-Echo .922 (.928), No-AEC .609 (.619), Diag. KF .778 (.781), Meta-AEC* .870 (.876), JCT-Meta-AEC* .871 (.877). Real playback: No-Echo .931 (.936), No-AEC .583 (.593), Diag. KF .690 (.698), Meta-AEC* .746 (.754), JCT-Meta-AEC* .754 (.762). Two observations. First, absolute performance is significantly higher than in Table 1, not because AEC became stronger, but because KWS became smarter: the classifier underwent pre-training without playback and fine-tuning on AEC outputs (Section 5.3), learning to tolerate residual echo—even No-AEC rose from .087 to .609. Second, the gain of JCT over Meta-AEC is .008 F1 on real playback (p-value .01, statistically significant) and .001 on synthetic playback (not significant); but on 12-second long playback tests, the gap widens to 0.057 (p-value .0005). The pattern revealed by these numbers is consistent with Table 1: **the advantage of classification training amplifies with playback length**—the longer the sequence, the more adaptive steps the learned update rule has to accumulate its targeted optimization for the classifier. Since both models were trained on 3-second playback, this length-amplified advantage comes from the generalization of the update rule itself, not from coverage of the training distribution.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

**Ablation of $\lambda$ is naturally embedded in all main tables**. Meta-AEC is simply CT-Meta-AF with $\lambda=0$, with identical architecture and training; thus, Table 1 and Table 3 constitute an ablation of $\lambda$ from 0 to 0.5: gains of 0.165 (synthetic) / 0.091 (real) under pre-trained frozen KWS, and gains of 0.001 (synthetic, not significant) / 0.008 (real, p=.01) under joint training. Notably, the gain is much larger in the frozen KWS scenario than in the joint training scenario—a reasonable explanation is that in joint training, the classifier itself is also learning to adapt to residuals, so the marginal value of optimizer customization is partially diverted by the classifier's adaptability; in the frozen scenario, the optimizer is the only channel for adaptation, and the value of classification feedback is fully realized. As for sensitivity scanning of $\lambda$ itself (whether 0.5 is optimal, how it varies with task scale), the paper does not report this.

**Specialization and Sensitivity Ablation (Table 2, synthetic playback, different KWS trained to match the dataset of each column are swapped in at test time, SER ∈ [−25, 0] dB)**. Rows are AEC models, columns are 35/10/2-class test sets: Meta-AEC gets .335 (.340) / .476 (.465) / .751 (.754); CT-Meta-AEC-35 gets .500 (.508) / .513 (.483) / .786 (.788); CT-Meta-AEC-10 gets .344 (.348) / .533 (.529) / .781 (.781); CT-Meta-AEC-2 gets .274 (.281) / .413 (.400) / .802 (.802). Three findings. First, the diagonal (where training and testing keyword settings match) is the best in every column—the optimizer indeed learned specialization for specific classification tasks/keyword sets, rather than general enhancement. Second, **Data Efficiency**: CT-Meta-AEC-10, trained with only about one-third of the data, achieves .533 on the 10-class test, surpassing CT-Meta-AEC-35 trained with triple the data (.513) and Meta-AEC trained with full data (.476)—the paper concludes that "classification training is a data-efficient way to improve downstream performance," which is a direct benefit for scenarios like custom wake words that naturally lack data. Third, **Transfer Has Boundaries**: Non-diagonal CTs usually still outperform Meta-AEC (e.g., CT-10 on 35 classes .344 vs. .335), but this is not universally true—CT-Meta-AEC-2 achieves only .413 on 10 classes, significantly lower than Meta-AEC's .476. The optimizer trained on 2 classes is overly specialized to the discriminative needs of those two keywords, and suffers when transferring to more complex tasks; the paper honestly admits this exception with the phrasing "usually still outperform."

**Training Mode Ablation (Table 1 vs. Table 3)**. The same AEC method shows a completely consistent ranking under frozen/joint KWS modes (CT series best, Meta second, KF third), indicating that the benefits of classification training do not depend on a specific deployment mode—this is experimental support for the "general framework" claim.

**Sequence Length Generalization (4 seconds vs. 12 seconds)**. As mentioned above, the gain amplifies with length (0.165 to 0.279, 0.091 to 0.119, joint scenario 0.001 to 0.057), and training playback is only 3 seconds, while 12 seconds is out-of-domain in length; this item can be viewed as an ablation verification of the effectiveness of the random truncation training strategy. The number of trials and variance for each model in Table 1 and Table 2 are not reported by the paper (only Table 3 declares three averages and p-values).

## Main Contributions

The paper states three contributions at the end of Section 1: First, a general framework that seamlessly integrates downstream task knowledge into AF update rules, applicable to various AFs and tasks; Second, task-specific insights for AEC and KWS, producing models that surpass existing methods; Third, comprehensive empirical coverage across different classifier models, multiple training schemes, and real and synthetic data, with all code and weights open-sourced (page see paper footnote 1).

Stripping away the rhetoric, there are four things this paper truly establishes. First, it demonstrates the correct way to open "task-aware frontends": replacing test-time feedback paths with training-time gradient paths, and manual derivation with automatic differentiation, making modularity and end-to-end no longer a binary choice. Second, it provides quantitative evidence of the failure of signal-level metrics (5.80 dB vs. 9.57 dB yet surpassing by 0.165 F1), which serves as a methodological reminder for the entire speech frontend research community—what you optimize is more important than how much you optimize. Third, the frozen classifier mode proves that effective customization for off-the-shelf KWS can be achieved by training only a 32K complex-parameter optimizer, and specialization requires only one-third of the data (Table 2), offering extremely high engineering cost-performance. Fourth, the strict single-variable experimental design (same architecture, same pipeline, only loss differs; baselines given the strongest treatment of task-aware tuning) makes the conclusions difficult to question as "unfair comparisons."

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

-   **Sacrifice of Signal-Level Performance**. CT's echo suppression drops from 9.57 dB to 5.80 dB (Section 5.1)—the optimizer is directionally trained to be "harmless to this very specific classifier." If the same frontend needs to serve other consumers (human hearing, call quality, another ASR engine), specialization may backfire. In reality, AEC is often shared infrastructure for multiple parties, and the "good" defined by a single classifier's feedback is not necessarily globally good.
-   **Linear Echo Path Assumption**. The signal model $d = u * w + n + s$ is linear convolution, and the filter is a frequency-domain linear filter; nonlinear distortions common in large-volume small-speaker scenarios (harmonic distortion, mechanical distortion) are not within the modeling scope. Practical AECs often cascade nonlinear post-processing to suppress nonlinear residuals after linear residuals; this framework does not cover this layer.
-   **$\lambda$ Fixed at 0.5**. Task scales range from 2 classes to 35 classes, with data volume differing by ten times; the optimal weight is likely not the same 0.5 for all; the paper does not report any $\lambda$ sensitivity, which is both a lack of hyperparameter transparency and suggests that results may not be fully tuned.
-   **Absolute Performance is Still Far from Practical**. In the frozen KWS scenario, the best result on real playback is .317 (.326), compared to the No-Echo upper bound of .931 (.936) (Table 1); in the joint training scenario, the best is .754 (.762) vs. .931 (.936) (Table 3). While an SER as low as −25 dB creates a harsh task construction, the large gap indicates that this setting is far from solved.
-   **Training Cost and Inference Overhead of Meta-Learning are Opaque**. BPTT passes through the optimization trajectory of randomly truncated $L$ steps, requiring gradient clipping and carefully set learning rate schedules; wall-clock training time is not reported by the paper. At inference time, the optimizer network requires a forward pass for each block, and its FLOPs are not reported (only 32K complex parameters are given)—although it is zero increment relative to Meta-AF (same architecture, classification loss only intervenes during training), there are no numbers for the absolute overhead relative to No-AFC.
-   **No Task Feedback at Test Time Also Means No Task Protection**. Classification feedback exists only during training; at test time, when encountering acoustic conditions out of the training distribution (new rooms, new devices, playback with heavier nonlinearity), one can only rely on the self-supervised term and the learned update rules to generalize on their own; the framework lacks an online error-correction mechanism.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

-   **Generalization Claim is Not Empirically Supported**. The framework claims to adapt to "various AFs and tasks," but experiments only cover the single combination of AEC plus KWS; other AF tasks like beamforming, dereverberation, and noise reduction are not verified.
-   **One Wing of the Control Group is Missing**. Section 1 divides DNN methods into decoupled pipelines and end-to-end approaches and positions itself between them, but experiments only compare against two signal-level methods: Kalman filtering and the original Meta-AF; none of the two DNN camps (including the task-closest Cornell [20] implicit AEC-KWS) have any numbers entering the field. The lack of direct confrontation with [20] is particularly regrettable—their task settings are nearly identical, and missing this cell removes the most critical horizontal evidence for the claim that "retaining modularity is still better."
-   **The Selling Point of "Trainable on Real Mixed Data" Lacks Experiments**. The self-supervised design explicitly states no oracle is needed and direct compatibility with real mixed data training (Section 3.2), but all training uses synthetic playback, and real playback is used only for evaluation (Section 4.3). The framework's most differentiated value is precisely what was not tested.
-   **Evaluation Protocol is Distant from Product-Level KWS**. Clip-level multi-class classification (single keyword appearance, zero-padding, random time shifts) rather than streaming detection; there are no hard metrics for wake-word products like false alarms per hour, hit rate, or response latency; nor are there real-time measurements on edge devices.
-   **Statistical Completeness is Uneven**. Table 3 has averages of three trials and p-values, but the number of trials and variance for Table 1 and Table 2 are not reported by the paper, making single-run possibilities non-excludable; the 12-second evaluation only provides differences (0.279/0.119/0.057), and the complete table is not reported.
-   **Subset Composition is Opaque**. Which keywords are included in the 10-class and 2-class subsets, and how they were selected, are not reported by the paper—the conclusions of the specialization experiments (Table 2) may be sensitive to keyword selection.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

-   **Expand Task Coverage**: Move the same $\mathcal{L}_{CM}$ recipe to other AF tasks like beamforming, dereverberation, and noise reduction, and to other downstream classification tasks (sound event detection, speaker recognition), to fulfill the "general framework" claim; the authors also self-expect a plug-and-play design in the conclusion.
-   **Training on Real Mixed Data**: The self-supervised term was designed for this; directly training the optimizer on unlabeled real playback (or weakly labeled real data) is a natural extension of the framework's logic and best distinguishes it from oracle-dependent methods.
-   **Adaptive and Scheduled $\lambda$**: Replace 0.5 with a schedule that adjusts according to the training process or task scale, or perform systematic sensitivity scanning; further, multiple downstream classifiers could be placed in $\mathcal{L}_{CM}$ simultaneously to train a shared frontend serving multiple consumers, alleviating the "specialization backfire" problem.
-   **Fulfill the Hook in Section 3.3**: Allow the classifier to use information other than the output of $h_\theta$ (e.g., directly observing the mixed signal); the paper explicitly states this can further boost performance but did not experiment with it.
-   **Nonlinear Echo Modeling**: Introduce nonlinear pre-distortion or neural residual modules within the Meta-AF framework to handle speaker distortion components that linear filters cannot reach.
-   **Streaming Evaluation and Edge Deployment**: Switch to a streaming wake protocol (false alarms/false negatives, latency, actual device power consumption and compute), to verify the position of this method under real product constraints; the magnitude of 32K complex parameters for the optimizer looks friendly for edge devices, but needs support from FLOPs and actual measurement data.
