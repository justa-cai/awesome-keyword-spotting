- **Authors/Affiliations**: Yong-Hyeok Lee, Namhyun Cho (NCSOFT Corporation Speech AI Lab, South Korea)
- **Date**: August 2023 (arXiv:2308.16511v1, submitted August 31, 2023)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.16511 (Code available: https://github.com/ncsoft/PhonMatchNet)
- **Keywords**: zero-shot keyword spotting, user-defined keyword spotting, phoneme-level detection loss, self-attention, cross-modal matching, open-vocabulary, pre-trained speech embedder

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) is the task of determining whether a specific keyword appears in an audio segment within speech processing. It serves as the core voice interface for products such as smart speakers, AI assistants, and personalized digital humans. The traditional training paradigm for KWS relies on a closed vocabulary: large amounts of labeled audio are collected for a fixed set of wake-up words to train a classifier or detector. This paradigm works well in scenarios where manufacturers preset wake-up words, but commercial trends are moving away from it. The paper points out at the beginning that with the popularization of smart speakers, AI assistants, and personalized digital humans, consumers wish to replace manufacturer-prescribed words with their own keywords, and the demand for personalized services continues to grow.

User-Defined Keyword Spotting (UDKWS) brings a structural shock to the data paradigm. The paper categorizes the difficulties into three types of scenarios: target keywords are rare (almost non-existent in daily corpora), specialized (proper nouns such as product names and personal names), and user-defined (arbitrary words that manufacturers cannot foresee). The commonality among these three types of scenarios is that it is impossible to collect sufficient labeled data for every possible keyword in advance. The traditional KWS mode of "one vocabulary, one training" fails completely here because the annotation cost grows linearly with the number of keywords, while the space of user-definable keywords is open.

Zero-shot KWS is a direction proposed to address this dilemma: the model directly detects the target keyword without having seen any training samples of it. This requires the model to learn not the "acoustic pattern of a specific word," but the transferable correspondence of "whether speech matches a text description." Once this correspondence is learned, adding a new keyword only requires changing the text input; no retraining is needed, nor does the user need to record additional audio.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

**First shortcoming: Registration burden of the Query-by-Example (QbyE) route.** Most UDKWS models adopt the QbyE method, comparing input speech with pre-registered speech templates in a latent space (the paper cites a series of works including LSTM comparison, CTC-based Donut, multi-head attention with soft triplet loss, and QbyE-MLPMixer). The paper's evaluation of this route is that it is limited in both performance and implementation, for a direct reason: the other party in the comparison is "pre-registered speech," which means that every time a user changes a keyword, they must first read several registration audio clips to the device. The registration process brings three costs: usage friction (the setup process becomes longer), registration sample quality bottlenecks (the channel, speaker state, and noise environment during registration become sources of bias for subsequent comparisons), and the storage and matching overhead for the device to save audio templates for each keyword. Essentially, QbyE binds the "definition of the keyword" to audio examples rather than to more natural text descriptions.

**Second shortcoming: Sentence-level granularity bottleneck of Cross-Modal Correspondence Detectors (CMCD).** The recent work CMCD (Shin et al., Interspeech 2022), which the paper targets, adopts the idea of comparing speech with text, eliminating the need for speech registration when adding new keywords, which is a clear progress. However, the paper points out two specific defects. First, "the similarity between speech and text is calculated at the utterance level"—the representation of the entire speech segment is aggregated into a whole to be compared with the text representation, causing it to fail to correctly distinguish pairs of words with similar pronunciations. The mechanism of this defect is worth expanding on: for word pairs like "friend" and "trend" that differ by only one or two phonemes, the difference in sentence-level representations is diluted by the whole-word information—most phonemes in a sentence are shared, and the differences are concentrated in individual positions; the aggregation operation thins the strong signal across the entire vector, resulting in insufficient signal-to-noise ratio. Second, CMCD's audio encoder "consists entirely of fully trainable modules," and therefore cannot properly handle uncommon pronunciations not present in the training dataset. Being fully trainable means the encoder's representational capacity is determined entirely by the training distribution; when encountering pronunciation patterns of proper nouns that are rare or missing in the training set, there is no fallback prior.

**Third shortcoming: Incompatibility of temporal localization supervision and CTC loss.** The paper starts from evidence in related fields: in various detection tasks such as speech-text, vision-text, speech-vision, and pure vision, the temporal localization information of the target significantly helps achieve high performance. Intuitively, when KWS judges "is this sentence 'hello'", if the model knows approximately which frame each phoneme appears in, it is much more stable than blurring the entire sentence into a vector and then judging. However, labeling at each timestamp is very difficult for sequence data, with high costs and prone to errors. Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss is the standard solution for alignment-free training, and the paper acknowledges this, but immediately points out its fundamental incompatibility with UDKWS: the premise of CTC is that speech and text labels must match (this is the task definition of speech recognition), while UDKWS training must include negative samples where speech and keyword text do not match—CTC does not define a reasonable supervision signal for "mismatched pairs." So the problem becomes: how to introduce temporal granularity localization supervision into UDKWS training without requiring precise time-alignment information.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, this paper aims to solve four mutually constraining challenges simultaneously: First, significantly improve the discrimination ability of keyword pairs with highly similar pronunciations without registering speech and under zero-shot conditions (the core shortcoming of CMCD); Second, design a fine-grained (phoneme-level) supervision signal that does not rely on alignment annotations, bypassing CTC's assumption that labels must match; Third, enable the audio encoder to possess prior knowledge of general pronunciations (for robustness in general conversation) while adapting to special pronunciations not present in the training set (proper nouns), without pushing the parameter count to levels unacceptable for KWS; Fourth, fit all three of the above into a single end-to-end model under the constraint of keeping the parameter count consistent with the baseline (655K vs. 653K, Table 1).

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

PhonMatchNet consists of three sub-modules: a two-stream speech encoder with a pre-trained embedder, a pattern extractor based on self-attention, and a pattern discriminator. The overall data flow is as follows: speech and keyword text are encoded into time-series embeddings, concatenated along the time dimension, and passed through self-attention for cross-modal fusion. Then, both sentence-level matching probability and phoneme-level matching probability sequences are read out from the fused representation, and each path's probability is calculated against the labels using binary cross-entropy loss.

**The design motivation for the two-stream speech encoder** is a direct response to CMCD's defect that "fully trainable encoders cannot handle rare pronunciations." It consists of two parallel branches. The first is a frozen pre-trained speech embedder (Lin et al., ICASSP 2020, a work using limited and synthetic speech to train keyword detectors). This embedder works with a 775 ms window, outputting a 96-dimensional feature vector every 80 ms. The paper explains the selection logic in the related work section: recent research on non-semantic speech representations (wav2vec 2.0, TRILL, FRILL, LillS, etc.) has allowed a single embedder to be reused across tasks. After weighing KWS performance and model size, the authors selected and froze this embedder. The second branch is a fully trainable feature extractor, consisting of two one-dimensional convolutions (kernel size 3) plus batch normalization and ReLU, with the first convolution having a stride of 2 and the rest having a stride of 1. The input starting points of the two branches are different: the trainable branch directly consumes 40-dimensional Mel-filter bank coefficients extracted with a 10 ms frame shift and 25 ms window length; the output of the pre-trained embedder is then sampled in feature and time dimensions via a one-dimensional transposed convolution (kernel 5, stride 4) plus a fully connected layer. Finally, the feature vectors of the two branches are added frame by frame to obtain 128-dimensional features at a rate of one frame per 20 ms, denoted as the audio embedding. The frozen branch provides "pronunciation priors on the general conversation distribution" (the embedder was trained on general conversations on YouTube), while the trainable branch is responsible for "discriminative fine-tuning within the training set distribution and adaptation to special pronunciations." Adding the two paths rather than concatenating them allows the information from both paths to be fused with equal weight in each frame, so that the advantage of either path can directly enter subsequent modules.

**The text encoder** is similar to CMCD: first, a pre-trained Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) model converts the keyword text into a phoneme sequence (the paper uses Kyubyong's open-source g2p implementation), takes the last hidden state of the encoder as the G2P embedding, and then passes it through a fully connected layer and ReLU activation to obtain a text embedding with the same dimension as the audio embedding. Dimension alignment (both are 128) and sequence form (both are frame sequences on the time axis) are prerequisites for subsequent concatenation and fusion—after encoding, the text is not a single vector, but a sequence of one frame per phoneme, which is the representational basis for the validity of phoneme-level supervision.

**The pattern extractor uses self-attention rather than cross-attention**, and the paper gives two layers of reasons. In terms of parameter efficiency, citing Chefer et al.'s interpretability research on bi-modal and encoder-decoder Transformers: in multimodal fusion, self-attention methods do not require additional modules, whereas cross-attention mechanisms require constructing projections for queries, keys, and values for each modality separately, resulting in more modules and parameters—the KWS task naturally requires maintaining fewer model parameters, which is especially important in the context of edge-side deployment. In terms of information flow, self-attention calculates attention on the concatenated sequence, and its output contains information in all directions: speech-to-speech, text-to-speech, and text-to-text; whereas the output of cross-attention is only one modality querying another. The paper further explains in the ablation analysis: this "attention output enhanced by speech information" helps distinguish the pronunciation similarity of rare cases in the training dataset—proper nouns appear rarely in the training set, and unidirectional alignment from text to speech is not reliable enough; the self-attention context of the speech itself can provide additional evidence.

**The pattern discriminator** reads out two paths. The sentence-level path processes the fused embedding along the time dimension using a 128-dimensional single-layer GRU, takes the last hidden state, and sends it to a fully connected layer with sigmoid to output the matching probability of the entire sentence. The phoneme-level path does not perform sequence aggregation; instead, it directly cuts out the position of the corresponding text segment from the fused embedding (the frame index falls in the interval after the audio length and before the total sequence length), passes it frame by frame through a fully connected layer with sigmoid, and outputs a matching probability sequence with a length equal to the number of phonemes. One performs global judgment, and the other performs per-phoneme judgment, corresponding exactly to the two-level loss.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The pattern extractor uses standard scaled dot-product attention (Equation 1): the attention output is calculated by three sets of matrices for query, key, and value. The square root of the key's dimension is used to scale the dot product, preventing the inner product from entering the softmax saturation zone as the dimension increases. The single-modality embeddings of the two modalities are first concatenated along the time dimension to obtain the concatenated embedding (Equation 2): the audio embedding and text embedding are concatenated head-to-tail, maintaining a dimension of 128, with a total length equal to the sum of the lengths of the two sequences. Then, the concatenated embedding serves simultaneously as query, key, and value for self-attention, obtaining the fused embedding (Equation 3), with the same length as the concatenated embedding. A lower triangular matrix is used as the attention mask during attention calculation, using only causal information within the modality—i.e., each position can only attend to positions before itself. This is consistent with the causality of online processing of speech signals and also prevents information leakage from future phonemes of the text segment into early audio frame representations.

The two readouts of the discriminator are written as Equations 4 and 5: the sentence-level probability is the scalar resulting from mapping the GRU fused embedding output through weights, bias, and the sigmoid function; the phoneme-level probability is the vector resulting from mapping the fused embeddings of each frame of the text segment through an independent set of weights, bias, and sigmoid, with a length equal to the length of the phoneme sequence.

The training criterion (Equation 6) is a simple sum of two binary cross-entropy losses: the sentence-level detection loss plus the phoneme-level detection loss. The sentence-level loss is the same as in general KWS methods, calculating BCE using the sample-level ground truth (whether this speech matches this keyword) and the sentence-level probability, enabling the model to learn speech-keyword similarity judgment at the sentence level.

The phoneme-level loss is the mathematical core of the paper. The phoneme-level ground truth is constructed according to Equation 7: for each position in the phoneme sequence, if the phoneme at that position in the speech label is the same as the phoneme at that position in the keyword label, it is 1; otherwise, it is 0. Note that this construction only requires the phoneme transcription of the speech side and the text side respectively, and completely does not require time-alignment annotations between the two—this is a direct response to the pain point that "timestamp labels are difficult to obtain." Using the example in Figure 1 of the paper: the speech is "Hallow," transcribed by G2P into the phoneme sequence (HH, AE1, L, OW0); the keyword text is "Hello," transcribed into (HH, EH1, L, OW0). After position-by-position comparison, the labels for the first, third, and fourth phoneme positions are 1 (correct), and the label for the second position (AE1 vs. EH1) is 0 (mismatch). In Figure 1, the mismatched phoneme, label, and corresponding BCE loss are marked with bold red arrows. The model then calculates BCE using the phoneme-level ground truth and the predicted probability sequence.

**Why phoneme-level supervision can distinguish similar pronunciations** can be understood from the granularity of the supervision signal. The sentence-level label is one bit for the whole word: the sentence-level label for "Hallow" vs. "Hello" is 0, and the sentence-level label for "Hallow" vs. "monkey" is also 0; the two types of "mismatch" are indistinguishable in the loss. The phoneme-level label, however, splits "Hallow" vs. "Hello" into four positions, three matches and one mismatch—a structured label of "mostly correct,个别 errors." It explicitly tells the model: your task is to compare phoneme by phoneme, and the error is located at the second vowel. This is equivalent to transforming the difficult problem of "distinguishing similar pronunciations" from "whether the distance between two whole-sentence vectors is far enough" to "whether the per-phoneme comparison is correct," which is a form that the model is easier to learn and easier to correct with gradient signals. At the same time, it naturally accommodates negative samples: when speech and text do not match, most phoneme positions can still be 1 (e.g., the first phoneme is the same), and only the differing positions are 0; the loss is defined, and the gradient has a direction—this is something CTC cannot do.

**Why choose sigmoid per-frame binary classification instead of sequence alignment losses**: The output requirement of UDKWS is "matching probability" rather than "alignment path." Per-frame sigmoid output can be viewed globally (all 1s mean the whole word matches) or locally (which phonemes are wrong), and inference does not require decoding search, keeping it lightweight.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Phoneme-Level Detection Loss

This is the direct source of "Phoneme-Guided" in the paper's title and the core contribution distinguishing it from all previous works. Its innovations can be summarized as three "no needs": no need for speech-pronunciation alignment information (labels are constructed using position-by-position comparison of phoneme sequences, Equation 7); no need for labels to necessarily match (mismatched pairs still produce effective supervision with mostly 1s and a few 0s, bypassing the applicability premise of CTC); no need for additional alignment decoding modules (phoneme-level probabilities are read out frame by frame directly from the text segment of the fused embedding, Equation 5). The design motivation comes from a clear causal chain: sentence-level comparison dilutes local differences (CMCD's defect) → need for finer-grained supervision → timestamp labels are unavailable and CTC is incompatible → use "pronunciation identity" as a label to replace "precise time information" (original paper quote: propose using the identity of speech and text pronunciations as labels, rather than precise time information). The experimental section (Figure 2) verifies that this design indeed brings discriminative power in the low normalized edit distance (highly similar pronunciation) interval that sentence-level methods lack.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Self-Attention Pattern Extractor

Changing cross-modal fusion from "one modality querying another" to "concatenating and then uniformly performing self-attention" is a small change but with clear benefits. There are three technical key points: First, parameter efficiency, self-attention fusion does not require preparing additional modules for each modality, conforming to the KWS constraint of few parameters; Second, information completeness, the fused embedding contains attention information in three directions: speech-to-speech, text-to-speech, and text-to-text; the text segment representation enhanced by speech context is more robust to rare words; Third, causality, the lower triangular attention mask ensures that only causal information within the modality is used, preserving structural possibilities for streaming processing. Ablation experiments (Row 4 of Table 1) show that after switching to cross-modal attention, the EER on the proper noun dataset Q deteriorated from 6.05 to 18.24 (compared to two fusion methods with the phoneme-level loss removed, both being isolated variables), which is the most severe degradation in a single set among the three ablations, indicating that this design has the greatest value for rare pronunciation scenarios.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Two-Stream Speech Encoder (Frozen Pre-trained Embedder Plus Fully Trainable Branch)

This design responds to the dilemma of "pre-trained embedders and special pronunciations." Using only a fully trainable encoder (CMCD's approach) lacks general pronunciation priors; using only a pre-trained embedder is constrained by its training distribution (general conversations on YouTube), and it handles out-of-distribution pronunciations such as proper nouns poorly. The two-stream addition is a compromise: the frozen branch guarantees general scenarios, and the trainable branch learns compensatory representations for tasks and special pronunciations during training. Ablation (Row 5 of Table 1) shows that after removing the trainable branch (using only the pre-trained embedder, without convolutional layers), the EER on all test sets deteriorated comprehensively (G from 6.77 to 14.34, Q from 4.75 to 22.90, LPE from 2.80 to 5.13, LPH from 18.82 to 25.77), which is the worst among all ablation configurations, proving that the trainable branch is indispensable; conversely, the paper also points out that although the configuration using only the pre-trained embedder has limited improvement on Q, it has considerable improvement on other datasets compared to the fully trainable CMCD baseline, indicating that the general prior of the frozen branch is also indispensable.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the QbyE route: PhonMatchNet defines keywords using text, and the parties being compared are speech embeddings and text phoneme embeddings. Adding new keywords does not require registering audio, eliminating registration friction and template storage. Compared to CMCD, there are three substantial differences: in supervision granularity, CMCD only calculates similarity at the sentence level, while PhonMatchNet adds phoneme-level loss to form two-level supervision; in encoder composition, CMCD's audio encoder is fully trainable, while PhonMatchNet introduces a frozen pre-trained embedder to form a two-stream structure; in fusion mechanism, PhonMatchNet uses self-attention to fuse on the concatenated sequence, whereas cross-modal attention-style fusion was proven to be comprehensively inferior to self-attention in ablation (Table 1). Compared to CTC-style alignment methods: CTC requires speech and labels to match, while PhonMatchNet uses "pronunciation identity" labels to replace time-alignment labels, bringing mismatched samples into the effective range of fine-grained supervision. Compared to full-shot models: the latter binds one training to each vocabulary, while PhonMatchNet serves an open vocabulary with one training, at the cost of still having a gap in accuracy (see Table 3).

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

The training phase uses the training set of LibriPhrase, mixed with babble noise from the MS-SNSD dataset to enhance detection robustness; the paper states that detailed training conditions are similar to the original CMCD paper, but the specific scale of the training set (number of hours or sample pairs) is not reported in the paper and requires tracing back to the original LibriPhrase paper. The evaluation phase uses four test sets: Google Speech Commands V1 (denoted as G), Qualcomm Keyword Speech dataset (denoted as Q), LibriPhrase-easy (LPE), and LibriPhrase-hard (LPH). Among them, LPE and LPE are obtained by reclassifying the LibriPhrase test set into easy and hard versions based on "the distinguishability of anchor sample and negative sample pairs," covering three levels of difficulty: familiar words (e.g., "yes" and "no"), proper nouns (e.g., "snapdragon" and "lumina"), and indistinguishable pronunciations (e.g., "friend" and "frind"). The sample scale of each of the four test sets is not reported in the paper.

G and Q do not provide negative sample pairs, so the authors construct them themselves: for each dataset, all keywords in the candidate list except the positive pair are treated as negative pairs, i.e., one anchor keyword compared against multiple negative keywords in an open comparison. Table 2 gives an example: for the G dataset, the anchor word is "go," and the negative words are "yes," "no," "up," "down," "left," "right," "on," "off," "stop" (nine words); for the Q dataset, the anchor word is "hi galaxy," and the negative words are "hey android," "hey snapdragon," "hi lumina." Note that this construction is stricter than "one-to-one pairing": the model must distinguish the anchor word from all other candidate words simultaneously, and any confusion will be counted as an error.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The main metrics are Equal Error Rate (EER) and Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), calculated at the sample level. The rationale for selection: UDKWS evaluation is a sample-level binary classification (whether this speech matches this keyword). EER is the error rate when the false alarm rate equals the miss rate; a single number summarizes the detector's balance point on two types of errors, independent of specific operating point selection; AUC integrates discriminative ability across all thresholds and is insensitive to thresholds. Using both together looks at both the balance point and overall separation, which is the standard combination for detection tasks. When comparing with traditional full-shot models (Table 3), Accuracy is used instead, because PhonMatchNet only outputs matching probability, which cannot be directly aligned with classification models that output category distributions. Thus, the authors fixed the threshold at 0.8, judged a hit if the matching probability exceeded 0.8, and calculated accuracy to make the outputs of the two types of models comparable.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Comparison with baseline CMCD (Table 1)**: The CMCD (653K parameters) reproduced by the authors had EERs of 31.94, 26.09, 10.48, 29.34 on the four test sets, and AUCs of 73.86, 82.42, 95.63, 77.60. PhonMatchNet (655K parameters, basically consistent with the baseline) had corresponding EERs of 6.77, 4.75, 2.80, 18.82, and AUCs of 98.11, 98.90, 99.29, 88.52. Averaged across the four datasets, EER improved by 67% relative, and AUC improved by 80% relative (consistent with reports in the abstract and main text). Looking at the improvement magnitude per set: G's EER dropped from 31.94 to 6.77 (approx. 79% relative decrease), Q from 26.09 to 4.75 (approx. 82%), LPE from 10.48 to 2.80 (approx. 73%), LPH from 29.34 to 18.82 (approx. 36%)—it can be seen that the improvement for the hardest distinguishable pronunciation set, LPH, is relatively minimal, which is consistent with the shortcomings acknowledged in the paper's conclusions. It should be noted that the reproduced CMCD performance on LPE and LPH was comparable to the original paper, but lower than the original paper on G and Q. The authors attribute this to different negative sample pair construction methods (one anchor against all other keywords, rather than the pairing method in the original paper), and believe that their construction method, which calculates the relationship of one anchor with all other keywords, can measure the performance of these models with higher precision—i.e., the evaluation is stricter, but the absolute numbers of the baseline are not directly comparable to the original paper.

**Comparison with full-shot models (Table 3)**: On the Google Speech Commands V1-12 test set, with a threshold of 0.8, the accuracies of various full-shot models are: DS-CNN 95.4, Att-RNN 95.6, TC-ResNet 96.6, MHAtt-RNN 97.2, MatchBoxNet 97.5, KWT-3 97.5, BC-ResNet-8 98.0; PhonMatchNet achieved 96.8 in a zero-shot manner. The reasonable way to interpret this result: it exceeds four classic models (DS-CNN, Att-RNN, TC-ResNet, MHAtt-RNN), is only 0.7 percentage points behind MatchBoxNet and KWT-3 (97.5), and 1.2 percentage points behind the strongest BC-ResNet-8; and this number was achieved when the model's training data did not contain the Google Speech Commands dataset at all—all full-shot opponents were specifically trained on this vocabulary. The paper also emphasizes that this is a noteworthy result. Of course, it must be clearly seen: this is a trade-off where the convenience dividend of zero-shot vs. full-shot is exchanged for a reduction in the upper limit of accuracy, and accuracy is not on the same scale as EER/AUC; the two types of comparisons cannot be mixed.

**Performance Analysis Stratified by Pronunciation Similarity (Figure 2)**: The authors define the normalized Levenshtein distance between speech-text label pairs as a measure of pronunciation similarity (the smaller the distance, the more similar the pronunciation). On the LibriPhrase test set, buckets are formed according to similarity, and the mean squared error between model predictions and labels is calculated. The results show: PhonMatchNet outperforms the baseline in all distance intervals; more critically, the difference is morphological—the baseline model's error remains constant in the interval where the normalized Levenshtein distance is below 0.45 (meaning that as pronunciations become more similar, it sees no improvement, hitting the ceiling of sentence-level representations), while PhonMatchNet shows a linear improvement as distance decreases, i.e., the more similar the pronunciation, the more accurate the model's judgment. The authors attribute the performance improvement on similar pronunciations to the effectiveness of the phoneme-level detection loss, which corroborates the ablation conclusions.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Ablations are conducted in Table 1, where the authors adjusted the parameter counts of each ablation model to be the same as the complete model (655K), excluding interference from capacity differences. The three ablations respectively remove the phoneme-level detection loss (training with only sentence-level loss), replace the self-attention pattern extractor with cross-modal attention, and remove the fully trainable speech encoder (audio encoder keeps only the pre-trained embedder, removing the convolutional layer).

**Contribution of phoneme-level loss**: When using only sentence-level loss, EER deteriorated from the complete model's 6.77, 4.75, 2.80, 18.82 to 7.52, 6.05, 3.39, 19.65 (G, Q, LPE, LPH respectively), and AUC decreased slightly in sync (e.g., G from 98.11 to 97.45, Q from 98.90 to 98.45). The absolute increments seem small, but the paper points out with Figure 2 that its value is concentrated on the blade: on the LPH dataset and in the low normalized Levenshtein distance (highly similar pronunciation) interval, the discriminative power improvement brought by the phoneme-level loss is most obvious—exactly the blind spot where sentence-level methods fail. This indicates that the phoneme-level loss is not a panacea for comprehensive gain, but a targeted reinforcement for the specific shortcoming of "distinguishing similar pronunciations."

**Contribution of self-attention fusion**: After replacing the pattern extractor with cross-modal attention, EER deteriorated to G 11.32, Q 18.24, LPE 3.47, LPH 20.51, and AUC deteriorated to 95.73, 90.14, 99.26, 87.49. The deterioration on the Q dataset was the most severe (relative to the self-attention configuration with no phoneme-level loss, EER went from 6.05 to 18.24, approx. three times; AUC dropped from 98.45 to 90.14). The paper's explanation is: Q's keywords consist of proper nouns uncommon in typical training data. The output produced by self-attention on concatenated bi-modal data contains information in all modality directions: speech-to-speech, text-to-speech, and text-to-text. The attention output enhanced by speech information helps distinguish the pronunciation similarity of rare cases in the training set; cross-modal attention has only a unidirectional cross-modal information flow, and when the text side itself is unreliable for rare words, there is no speech self-context to fall back on. It is worth noting that the advantage of self-attention on G and LPE is relatively small (G 7.52 vs. 11.32, LPE 3.39 vs. 3.47), indicating that the benefits of the fusion method are strongly correlated with data distribution—on common words, cross-modal attention is already sufficient.

**Contribution of the trainable branch of the two-stream encoder**: Removing the fully trainable speech encoder (keeping only the pre-trained embedder) is the worst configuration among the three ablations: EER was G 14.34, Q 22.90, LPE 5.13, LPH 25.77, and AUC was 93.91, 84.19, 98.71, 82.53. The paper explains: the embedder used was trained on general conversations on YouTube, which is the boundary of its capability—out-of-distribution pronunciations (proper nouns) are not covered, so fully trainable modules must be added to compensate; after adding them, all test sets including Q improved (compare Row 4 and Row 5 of Table 1). Another observation in the opposite direction is also valuable: comparing Row 1 and the last row of Table 1 (CMCD vs. configuration using only embedder), using only the pre-trained embedder already had considerable improvement on datasets other than Q compared to the fully trainable CMCD, indicating that the general prior of the pre-trained embedder itself is worth the parameters; the two-stream approach is "prior plus adaptation," taking the strengths of both rather than any single route.

## Main Contributions

- **Proposed a zero-shot UDKWS model using both sentence-level and phoneme-level information**: Previous cross-modal methods only estimated at the sentence level. PhonMatchNet is the first work to explicitly introduce phoneme-level information into the comparison framework for this task, with an average relative improvement of 67% in EER and 80% in AUC across four datasets (Table 1).
- **Proposed phoneme-level detection loss**: Constructed per-phoneme labels using "the identity of speech and text pronunciations" (Equation 7). It requires no alignment annotations, no label matching, and no decoding modules, providing a fine-grained supervision solution for open-vocabulary negative sample scenarios where CTC is incompatible.
- **Two-stream speech encoder**: A frozen pre-trained speech embedder and a fully trainable convolutional branch are added together, simultaneously obtaining general conversation priors and adaptation capabilities for special pronunciations such as proper nouns. Ablation shows that both branches are indispensable (Rows 4 and 5 of Table 1).
- **Self-attention-style cross-modal fusion**: Replaced cross-modal attention with unified self-attention after concatenation, without adding modules and preserving three-direction information flow. The benefit is greatest in proper noun scenarios (Q dataset EER 6.05 vs. 18.24, Table 1).
- **Feasibility verification of zero-shot vs. full-shot**: Achieved 96.8% accuracy with a 0.8 threshold on Google Speech Commands V1-12, which was unseen during training, exceeding multiple classic full-shot models specifically trained (Table 3), and the code and implementation are open source.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

**Highly similar pronunciations remain a hard nut to crack.** The EER on the LPH dataset is 18.82 (Table 1). Although this is a significant improvement over the baseline's 29.34, the relative improvement magnitude (approx. 36%) is far lower than that of other datasets (approx. 73% to 82%). The paper's conclusion explicitly acknowledges that the relative performance improvement in this challenging scenario is lower than in other test sets, and lists improving performance on highly similar pronunciations as a future research direction. That is, phoneme-level supervision alleviates the sentence-level dilution problem but does not completely solve it—when the differing phonemes themselves have highly similar pronunciations (e.g., only slight differences in stress or vowels), the discriminative basis for per-phoneme comparison itself becomes blurred.

**Implicit dependence on G2P transcription quality.** The entire phoneme-level supervision system is built on the premise that "both speech and text can be transcribed into phoneme sequences." The text side relies on the correct transcription of the pre-trained G2P model (Kyubyong/g2p). Transcription errors produced by G2P for unconventional spellings, foreign words, or spoken variations will directly pollute the position-by-position labels—a misalignment of one phoneme will cause misalignment of all subsequent labels. The paper does not discuss this error propagation path, nor does it report the transcription accuracy of G2P on each test set.

**Domain boundaries of the pre-trained embedder.** The frozen embedder was trained on general conversations on YouTube, and the boundary of its representational capacity is one of the boundaries of the model; although the trainable branch can compensate, the compensation capability is limited by the branch's capacity (two layers of one-dimensional convolutions), and the paper does not separately report performance under extremely harsh channel conditions (far-field, strong noise, multi-speaker overlap). The robustness conclusion only extends to babble noise augmentation.

**Missing deployment-side metrics.** The paper only reports the parameter count (655K), and does not report inference latency, floating-point operations per second, memory usage, or streaming behavior. The lower triangular causal mask of self-attention structurally preserves the possibility for streaming, but attention itself requires the entire sequence to enter the calculation. The paper does not discuss actual per-frame streaming inference schemes (such as windowed incremental attention or distillation into a recursive structure); the GRU sentence-level path needs to hear the end of the sequence to output, and the paper does not report the response latency characteristics of wake-up.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

**Transparency of training data scale and augmentation details.** The paper only states that it used the LibriPhrase training set plus MS-SNSD babble noise, and that detailed conditions are similar to the original CMCD paper. The training set scale, noise SNR configuration, and augmentation ratios are all unreported, requiring cross-referencing with previous works for reproduction.

**Model selection directly uses the test set.** The paper's implementation details state that after training for one hundred epochs, "the best model was selected based on test set performance"—using the test set for model selection means the reported numbers carry a slight optimistic bias towards the test set. Strict evaluation should use an independent validation set to select the model and then report once on the test set. This is the most criticized point in the experimental design of the entire paper.

**Fairness of baseline comparison is affected by differences in negative sample construction.** The authors constructed "one anchor against all other candidate words" negative pairs for G and Q themselves, which is different from the construction in the original CMCD paper, causing the reproduced baseline to be significantly lower than the original paper's report on G and Q (Table 1 is not directly aligned with the original paper). The authors argue that their method is stricter and more accurate, and this argument has its merits (in an open-vocabulary scenario, it is inherently one word against multiple words), but the comparison between the baseline and the proposed method under the modified evaluation protocol mixes the influence of the evaluation protocol change into the 67% and 80% average relative improvements, and cannot be fully attributed to the method itself.

**Single dimension of comparison with full-shot.** Table 3 only has one accuracy number under a 0.8 threshold, with no same-metric comparison of EER/AUC, nor any threshold sensitivity analysis (how the value of 0.8 was selected is not explained). The degree to which the gap between zero-shot and full-shot depends on operating point selection is unknown.

**Single evaluation language and unreported scale.** All experiments were conducted on English data (LibriPhrase is derived from LibriSpeech, G and Q are English command words). It has not been verified whether the phoneme-level method is equally effective for languages with different phoneme systems (tonal languages, syllabic languages). The sample scale of each test set is not reported in the paper, and statistical significance tests (such as confidence intervals) are not provided.

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

The direction explicitly pointed out by the paper itself is to improve performance in scenarios with highly similar pronunciations (original quote in the conclusion: "Improving performance on highly similar pronunciations is our future research direction." Following the technical thread of this paper, several candidate paths can be naturally extrapolated (the paper does not elaborate, these are inferences by the author based on its framework): First, change the phoneme-level labels from position-by-position hard comparison to soft labels weighted by phoneme similarity (utilizing the acoustic distance between phonemes), allowing differences between similar vowels like "AE1 vs. EH1" to enter the loss as continuous signals rather than hard zeros; Second, jointly fine-tune or distill G2P, allowing text-side transcription and audio encoder representations to calibrate each other, alleviating transcription error propagation; Third, explicitly use phoneme-level probabilities for interpretable rejection during inference (outputting specifically which phoneme does not match), serving error-correction guidance for user-defined keywords (prompting the user "I heard X instead of Y"); Fourth, complete streaming and quantization on the deployment side, further compressing the 655K parameter model to enter edge-side resident listening; Fifth, expand multi-language evaluation to verify the universality of the phoneme-guided paradigm across phoneme systems.
