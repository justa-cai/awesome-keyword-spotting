- **Date**: September 2023 (arXiv:2309.16036v1; v2 updated in February 2024)

- **Keywords**: voice trigger detection, multichannel acoustic modeling, transform-average-concatenate, speech separation front-end, channel selection, far-field keyword spotting, on-device modeling

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Voice Triggering (VT) is the primary entry point for voice assistants on devices such as smart speakers and smartphones, where users activate the device by speaking a wake word. This task has three inherent attributes that dictate its engineering form. First, it must be always-on: the device does not know when a user will speak, so the front-end detection module must continuously monitor microphone input, which places extremely tight constraints on power consumption and computational budget. Second, it must work in the far-field: users are typically several meters away from the device, and the signal undergoes attenuation due to room reverberation, while being overlaid with various noises and interfering speakers such as TV, appliances, and music playback. Third, it exhibits high asymmetry in the costs of two types of errors: false rejection (FR, where the user says the wake word but the device does not respond) directly harms user experience, while false acceptance (FA, where the device wakes up without a user speaking) harms trust. In product terms, an extremely low FA rate is usually required (the paper's experiments fix the operating point at 1 false alarm per 100 hours, i.e., 0.01 FA/hr), and the False Rejection Rate (FRR) is further minimized at this stringent operating point.

To combat far-field noise, the standard practice in industrial systems (as mentioned in the paper's introduction) is to attach a front-end system before the acoustic model to perform speech enhancement and/or speech separation. The separation module is particularly critical in multi-speaker scenarios—typical acoustic models cannot directly process speech mixtures, while separation can split the target speaker and interference (e.g., voices from the TV) into different channels. The problem lies in the fact that the output of an enhancement-plus-separation front-end consists of **multiple** signals (one enhanced signal plus multiple separated signals), whereas the back-end VT acoustic model only accepts **single-channel** input. Therefore, a channel selection step is required to pick one channel from the multiple outputs to feed into the VT model.

The pain point of this paper lies precisely in this interface gap of "multiple outputs to single input." Channel selection is essentially a lossy hard decision: the unselected channels are discarded entirely. The paper presents two specific failure modes (Introduction and Section 3). First, the front-end may incorrectly suppress part of the target speech during enhancement/separation and introduce distortion in the selected channel—the suppressed part of the target speech may be preserved intact in other discarded channels. In other words, the correct answer is thrown away, leaving only a defective copy. Second, when there is no interfering speech or background noise in the environment, the enhancement/separation module is not only useless but harmful: it suppresses the target speech and introduces distortion, negatively affecting VT. These two failure modes point to the same conclusion: the single-channel interface is an information bottleneck, and the VT model should see all outputs from the front-end.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

Sections 2 and 3 of the paper categorize existing approaches into four types and point out their deficiencies one by one:

- **End-to-end multichannel acoustic modeling (Far-field ASR route, Sainath et al. [4][5][6], similar approaches on KWS [7])**: These methods feed raw multichannel waveforms directly into a CNN, allowing the model to learn spatial and spectral characteristics simultaneously. While these methods are end-to-end optimized for the target task, the joint modeling of spatial and spectral features significantly increases model complexity and computational overhead, making them unsuitable for VT tasks that must run on devices with low latency requirements. More importantly, these methods have no mechanism to distinguish between the target speaker and interfering speakers—a VT task is precisely one that "recognizes only the owner, not others."
- **Cross-channel modeling based on attention ([8][9])**: These methods use cross-channel attention to learn characteristics between channels, facing similar computational overhead issues and also lacking a target speaker identification mechanism.
- **Lightweight multichannel KWS (Feature concatenation [10], attention-weighted summation [11][12])**: These operations are simple and computationally light, but linear weighted summation may be insufficient to model non-linear characteristics between channels. Preliminary experiments in the paper provide direct evidence (beginning of Section 5): simple multichannel feature concatenation [10] **did not exceed the single-channel baseline** in their task—linear fusion is too weak. This is important because it indicates that the paper's gains do not come from the action of "multichannel" itself, but from non-linear inter-channel modeling.
- **Baseline two-stage system (Apple's Siri on HomePod route [2])**: A first-stage small model runs continuously for detection, and a second-stage large model verifies, using the first-stage score for channel selection. The deficiencies of this system are the two failure modes mentioned above: all unselected channels are discarded (noise and interference information contained therein could have helped the second-stage model make more accurate judgments), and enhancement/separation suppresses and distorts target speech in clean environments, acting negatively on VT.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

Translating the above pain points into technical challenges, the paper aims to simultaneously satisfy four mutually constraining conditions: (1) Allow the second-stage VT acoustic model to directly consume all multichannel outputs from the front-end, thereby losing no information; (2) Inter-channel fusion must be **non-linear** (linear concatenation/weighted summation has been proven ineffective by preliminary experiments), yet it must be **lightweight**—it cannot follow the heavy route of end-to-end multichannel ASR spatial-spectral joint modeling, because VT must run on devices; (3) When multiple speakers are present, the model must know which speaker to "listen" to—purely permutation-invariant channel processing cannot provide this clue; (4) The architecture must be insensitive to the number of channels and microphone array morphology, allowing direct reuse of existing front-ends and existing single-channel acoustic model backbones. Items (2) and (3) are the core contradictions: common practices for non-linear inter-channel modeling (cross-channel attention) are heavy, while lightweight practices (permutation-invariant TAC) naturally erase channel identity information. The paper's answer is to **inject the result of channel selection** into the TAC block, trading asymmetry for target speaker clues while preserving lightness.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system follows Apple's deployed two-stage VT framework (Section 3, Figure 2). The front-end system includes a speech enhancement module and a speech separation module: the enhancement module outputs a single-channel enhanced speech, and the separation module outputs $N-1$ separated signals, totaling $N$ signals (in experiments, $N=4$: 1 enhanced + 3 separated). The first-stage VT model is a very small model that continuously monitors streaming audio for each channel, scoring each channel independently; once the VT score for a segment exceeds a threshold, that audio segment is sent to the larger second-stage model for verification to reduce false alarms. The motivation for this hierarchy is runtime cost on devices: the small model runs all the time, and the large model runs only on candidate segments.

The baseline (Figure 2(a)) performs channel selection at the second-stage entry: it selects the channel with the highest first-stage score and feeds it alone to the second-stage model. The paper's modification (Figure 2(b)) precisely changes only the second stage: the first stage continues to run per channel and perform channel selection, but the second-stage acoustic model now receives **features from all $N$ channels**, **plus the selected channel** (denoted as selected channel) picked by the channel selection. This design has two motivations. First, it preserves the baseline's advantage in multi-speaker scenarios—channel selection itself is a coarse-grained target speaker locator, and feeding the selected channel as an explicit input gives the model a clue about "who to focus on." Second, it allows the second-stage model to learn cross-channel compensation itself: which channel's distortion should be compensated by which channel is left to the data to learn, rather than cutting off options with hard rules in advance.

The choice of the acoustic backbone also reflects the philosophy of "reuse": the second-stage model uses a Transformer encoder ([19][20]), with input being 40-dimensional log-mel filterbank features for each channel. Multichannel fusion does not redesign the backbone; instead, it **prepends a TAC block and an average pooling layer** before the baseline model (Section 5.2). This means that spectral-temporal modeling is fully inherited from the mature single-channel Transformer, and multichannel knowledge is injected only through a lightweight module—incremental modification, keeping engineering risks and computational increments controllable.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

The TAC (Transform-Average-Concatenate) block was originally proposed for multichannel modeling in speech enhancement/separation ([3]). Let the front-end output $N$ channel signals, and the feature time series for the $i$-th channel be $Z_i \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times F}$, where $z_{i,t}$ is the $F$-dimensional feature vector at time frame $t$. The TAC block consists of three steps:

Step 1, per-channel transformation (Equation 1):
$$h_{i,t} = P(z_{i,t})$$
where $P(\cdot)$ is a linear layer followed by PReLU activation. Each channel independently passes through the same transformation, projecting channel features into a common latent space.

Step 2, cross-channel averaging (Equation 2):
$$h_t^{avg} = Q\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} h_{i,t}\Big)$$
The latent vectors of all channels at the same time instant are arithmetically averaged, then passed through a second linear layer with PReLU. This average vector is a global channel context—it encodes "what all channels look like combined at this moment."

Step 3, concatenation transformation (Equation 3):
$$\hat{h}_{i,t} = R([h_{i,t} ; h_t^{avg}])$$
Each channel's own latent vector is concatenated with the average vector, then passed through a third linear layer with PReLU. Finally, a residual connection is added (Equation 4):
$$\hat{z}_{i,t} = z_{i,t} + \hat{h}_{i,t}$$

From the perspective of information flow, the output of each channel is determined by "self + global average": if the target speech in a channel is partially suppressed by the front-end, but that part exists in other channels, it will be reflected in the average vector. The concatenation in Step 3 gives the model the opportunity to perform cross-channel compensation. Mathematically, more importantly, is the **permutation invariance**: since P, Q, and R are shared across all channels and the averaging operation is insensitive to channel order, the entire TAC block is naturally invariant to the permutation of input channels. This brings mic-array agnostic modeling capability—any number of enhancement/separation signals can be fed in directly, without needing to train separately for each array morphology. This is the direct reason the paper chose TAC over heavy cross-channel attention (beginning of Section 4): TAC uses only channel-level transformations, pooling, and concatenation, which is simple and lightweight.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Modified TAC Block with Injected Selected Channel

This is the paper's core knife (Section 4.2, Figure 2). Permutation invariance is a double-edged sword: it makes TAC robust to array morphology, but it also means TAC **treats every channel equally**—it has no clue about which channel is the target speaker. When the separation module splits target voice and TV voice into different channels, vanilla TAC cannot distinguish whom to trust, confusing both training and inference. This prediction was precisely verified by experiments: under noisy (TV voice interference) conditions, vanilla TAC deteriorated from the baseline's 6.75% to 10.7% (Table 1), and the paper explicitly attributed failure cases to "vanilla TAC having no clue to judge the target speaker when there are multiple speakers in the separated signals."

The improvement is to use the result of traditional channel selection as an additional input. Let $z_{sc,t}$ be the feature vector of the selected channel (selected channel). The processing flow of the improved TAC block is: apply the same transformation as Equation 1 to $z_{sc,t}$ (Equation 5):
$$h_{sc,t} = P(z_{sc,t})$$
The averaging operation is still performed on $h_{i,t}$ (i=1,...,N) according to Equation 2. Then concatenate the three (Equation 6):
$$\hat{h}_{i,t} = R([h_{i,t} ; h_t^{avg} ; h_{sc,t}])$$
For the selected channel itself, pass through a linear layer with PReLU to reduce the dimension back to the input dimension, then perform residual connection (Equation 7):
$$\hat{z}_{sc,t} = z_{sc,t} + S(h_{sc,t})$$
The other channels $\hat{z}_{i,t}$ are obtained according to Equation 3 (including residual Equation 4).

The ingenuity of the design lies in the introduction of "asymmetry": normal channels and the selected channel follow **different processing paths**—the fusion vector for normal channels is composed of "self + average," while the information of the selected channel enters the transformation of all channels as a third concatenation component, and the selected channel itself has a dedicated dimension-reduction residual branch. The entire block is no longer permutation-invariant (the position of the selected channel is fixed and treated differently). This "intentional destruction of permutation invariance" is precisely to inject the prior of "who is the target." Channel selection was originally an information bottleneck criticized in the baseline system; the paper demotes it from a "hard gate of choosing one of two" to a "soft hint feature," preserving its localization capability in multi-speaker scenarios without causing information discard.

There is also a handling of a practical issue during training (Section 5.2): the training data is not a keyword-specific dataset, so true channel selection cannot be performed on it (the first-stage VT model needs to be trained for wake words to score), so during training, the **output channel of the enhancement module is used as the pseudo selected channel**. During inference, the real channel selection result is used. This is a pragmatic engineering compromise, and it also sows the seeds of distribution inconsistency between training and inference (see Limitations).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Post-TAC Average Pooling Compression to Avoid Computational Explosion of Per-Channel Self-Attention

Section 4.3 addresses a computational trap that is easily overlooked. The original literature of TAC [14] combines the TAC block with self-attention layers: performing self-attention on **each output channel** of the TAC. This is acceptable in offline speech separation, but disastrous for VT—self-attention has quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length, multiplied by the number of channels $N$, effectively multiplying the most expensive part of the computation by $N$, while VT must run with low latency on devices. The paper's solution is simple and crude: before feeding the multichannel output of TAC to the self-attention layer (responsible for temporal and spectral modeling), add an **average pooling layer** to compress the $N$ channels into one. This way, the computational load of the Transformer backbone is exactly the same as the single-channel baseline, and all multichannel overhead is concentrated in the pre-pended TAC block. This design decouples "multichannel modeling" and "sequence modeling" into two serial modules, each using its optimal solution, which is typical layered thinking for on-device architecture.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared with various existing routes line by line, the differences fall on four dimensions: "input form × fusion operation × target speaker mechanism × computational cost." Compared with end-to-end multichannel ASR/KWS [5][6][7]: those methods learn spatial+spectral joint representations from raw multichannel waveforms, while this paper **reuses the existing front-end**, with input being the enhanced/separated signals output by the front-end (the paper argues these signals are "potentially more informative" than raw multichannel microphone signals, and saves the cost of redesigning the front-end), so spatial information no longer needs to be relearned by the acoustic model. Compared with cross-channel attention [8][9]: the paper replaces attention with non-linear but lightweight operations such as channel-level transformation + pooling + concatenation, with much smaller parameter and computational increments. Compared with lightweight multichannel KWS [10][11][12]: the linear concatenation/weighted summation of those methods is insufficient to model inter-channel characteristics (in preliminary experiments, concatenation did not even beat the single-channel baseline), while TAC provides true non-linear inter-channel modeling. Compared with baseline channel selection [2]: information is no longer hard-discarded, and channel selection is demoted to a hint input. Additionally, this paper is the only one among these methods to explicitly handle the distinction mechanism of "target speaker vs. interfering speaker"—achieved through asymmetric injection of the selected channel, while [5]-[12] all lack this mechanism.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

All experiments use Apple's internal data (Section 5.1), on the grounds that public datasets cannot well represent actual usage scenarios (e.g., echo when the device itself plays music, interfering speakers). The training data consists of approximately **2.3 million** manually transcribed single-channel speech utterances. Multichannel reverberant signals are simulated by convolving measured room impulse responses (RIR) with single-channel speech. RIRs were recorded using a **six-channel microphone array** in various rooms and at various microphone positions. Additionally, approximately **20%** of the corpus is convolved with simulated **four-channel** RIRs, and multichannel non-speech noise or multichannel played audio is added for enhancement. The three types of corpus are merged into a simulated multichannel training set, and all data is passed through the front-end system, resulting in 1 enhanced signal and 3 separated signals per utterance.

The evaluation set is also self-built. Positive samples come from **100** participants saying wake words to a six-microphone smart speaker under controlled conditions, totaling **1300** positive samples, covering four acoustic conditions: quiet (no playback, no noise), external noise (TV or appliances), device playing music at medium volume, and device playing music at high volume. Negative samples are **2000 hours** of podcasts, audiobooks, etc., containing no wake words, also recorded using smart speakers. Evaluation data is also passed through the front-end to obtain enhanced/separated signals. It is worth noting that there is a **microphone array mismatch** between training and testing data (some training data uses different arrays than the test array), which is compensated for by the front-end always outputting fixed four channels—this itself indirectly validates the practical value of TAC's array-agnostic design.

Model configuration (Section 5.2): The first-stage model is a 5×64 fully connected DNN, outputting frame-level posteriors for 20 classes (18 wake word phoneme classes + silence + other speech), with an HMM decoder producing streaming VT scores and alignments. The second-stage baseline is a 6-block Transformer encoder (each block has 4-head self-attention, 256 latent units, 1024 feed-forward units), outputting logits for 54 phoneme labels plus 1 blank, trained with CTC loss, with VT scores taken as the decoding scores for wake words; input is 40-dimensional log-mel features plus ±3 frames of context. The proposed model prepends a (modified) TAC block and an average pooling layer before the baseline. For the modified TAC, P and Q use 3×256 latent units, and R and S use 280 units. Parameter counts: baseline 4.8M, standard TAC version 6.1M, modified TAC version 6.5M. Training uses Adam, initial learning rate 0.0005, decayed by 1/4 after the 10th epoch, for a total of 28 epochs, on 32 GPUs with batch size 128 per card.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The metric is **False Rejection Rate (FRR)**, measured at a fixed operating point of "1 false acceptance per 100 hours (1 FA/100 hrs, i.e., 0.01 FA/hr)" (Table 1). The rationale for this combination comes directly from VT product logic: there is a sliding operating curve between FA and FR, and reporting FRR alone without an operating point is meaningless; moreover, the tolerance for false alarms in products is measured in "how many hours per occurrence," and 0.01 FA/hr is a stringent requirement for smart speaker class. Comparing FRR at this operating point is equivalent to comparing "who misses fewer wake words under the premise of not disturbing the user." The threshold is uniformly tuned for each model to the entire dataset's 0.01 FA/hr to ensure fairness. Additionally, results are listed by four acoustic conditions, which exposes the applicability boundaries of the mechanism better than looking at the overall result alone—indeed, the ranking of the three systems is completely different under different conditions.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline and SOTA Methods

Table 1 gives the FRR (%) for four conditions plus overall (operating point 1 FA/100 hrs):

| System | Quiet | Noise | Medium Volume Playback | High Volume Playback | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline (Channel Selection) | 4.48 | 6.75 | 5.23 | 15.32 | 7.74 |
| Proposed (vanilla TAC) | 2.25 | 10.7 | 3.78 | 12.46 | 7.20 |
| Proposed (+Selected Channel) | 3.12 | 7.12 | 4.55 | 14.62 | 7.16 |

Three headline numbers (Section 5.3): The proposed model with selected channel achieves relative FRR reductions of **30%**, **13%**, and **4.6%** in quiet, medium volume, and high volume playback conditions, respectively, with an overall relative reduction of **7.5%**. Interpreting these numbers condition by condition, each has a clear mechanistic explanation:

**Quiet condition is the scenario with the greatest improvement** (4.48% → 2.25%, vanilla TAC relative reduction 49.8%; with selected channel 3.12%, relative reduction 30.3%). The paper's explanation (Section 5.3): in quiet conditions, enhancement/separation is not needed and actually introduces distortion to the target speech in the selected channel; the proposed model compensates for these distortions by looking at all four channels (plus the selected one). This precisely verifies Failure Mode 2 in the introduction—front-end is a negative asset in clean environments, and multichannel input gives the second-stage model the ability to "bypass" front-end damage. It is worth noting that vanilla TAC performs better than the version with selected channel in quiet conditions (2.25% vs 3.12%), indicating that symmetric fusion is purer when there is no interference, and the additional input from the selected channel causes slight interference at this time.

**Music playback conditions show moderate improvement** (Medium volume 5.23% → 3.78%/4.55%, High volume 15.32% → 12.46%/14.62%). The paper's explanation: multichannel models can more effectively learn **echo residuals** from multichannel signals—each channel undergoes different front-end processing, and music echoes leave different residual forms in different channels. Multichannel input gives the model richer echo clues to distinguish "word-like sounds in music" from "true wake words." High volume is the most difficult of all conditions (all systems have FRR above 12%), but the relative improvement is the smallest (4.6%), indicating that high-volume echo has approached the capability boundary of this mechanism.

**Noise condition is the only scenario where it failed, and also the most informative scenario.** Vanilla TAC deteriorated from the baseline's 6.75% to 10.7% (relative deterioration 58.5%), while the version with selected channel pulled back to 7.12%, basically tying with the baseline (relative difference 5.5%). The paper's analysis of failure cases: failures concentrated on TV voice interference; when there are multiple speakers in the separated signals, vanilla TAC has no clue to judge which is the target speaker, and permutation invariance becomes a fatal flaw here. The injection of the selected channel is precisely targeted at this problem, saving the noisy condition from "significant regression" to "tying with the baseline," while maintaining leadership in the other three conditions—this is the entire meaning of the asymmetric design in Equations 5-7. But we must also honestly see: the model with selected channel **still did not exceed the baseline** under noisy conditions, 7.12% vs 6.75%. In multi-speaker scenarios, the channel selection baseline remains the strongest; the improvement mechanism only eliminated the shortcoming of the multichannel route in this condition, without converting it into an advantage.

**Interpretation of the overall numbers should be restrained**: 7.74% → 7.16%, a 7.5% relative reduction looks small, but it is the synthetic result of "two wins, one tie, one loss" across four conditions, and the operating point is fixed at 1 FA/100 hrs. The true value lies in the condition distribution: multichannel methods systematically won in quiet and playback scenarios (three conditions in total), while the selected channel mechanism made up for the shortcoming in human voice interference.

**Parameter count control** (Section 5.2): Baseline 4.8M, standard TAC 6.1M, modified TAC 6.5M, the proposed model has about 35% more parameters. The paper explicitly states that in preliminary experiments, **simply increasing the size of the baseline single-channel model did not improve VT performance**—this rules out the explanation that "gains come from a larger model," attributing the improvement to multichannel information itself. Coupled with the preliminary experiment where feature concatenation [10] lost to the baseline, these two controls converge the conclusion to: non-linear inter-channel modeling (TAC) is a necessary condition for multichannel gains.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

The paper does not have an independently named ablation section, but Table 1 is essentially a complete ablation of three systems × four conditions. The core findings can be summarized into four points:

1.  **Multichannel information itself is effective**: With the same Transformer backbone, changing the input from single-channel (selected channel) to four-channel TAC fusion nearly halved the FRR in quiet conditions (4.48% → 2.25%), and also reduced it overall (7.74% → 7.20%). Discarding channels is indeed information loss.
2.  **Permutation invariance is a negative asset in human voice interference**: Vanilla TAC regressed by 58.5% in noisy conditions (6.75% → 10.7%). This is the most important ablation finding in the paper—it independently proves the argument that "multichannel modeling must have a target speaker mechanism," and explains why the paper went to the trouble of modifying TAC.
3.  **The gain from injecting the selected channel is "loss prevention" rather than "gain"**: After adding the selected channel, noisy improved from 10.7% to 7.12% (relative improvement 33.5%), but quiet slightly regressed from 2.25% to 3.12%, and medium volume playback regressed from 3.78% to 4.55%. That is, the selected channel mechanism has a slight cost in non-interference conditions and a significant remedy in human voice interference conditions, with the overall (7.20% → 7.16%) basically持平 and slightly better. This is a trade-off design, not a pure gain design.
4.  **Several design dimensions were not ablated**: Only one TAC block was tried for the TAC block; the trade-off between average pooling and per-channel attention has only computational arguments but no empirical comparison (the paper did not report actual numbers for the per-channel self-attention version); the impact of using pseudo selected channels vs. real selected channels during training was not evaluated separately; only four channels were tested for the front-end. These are open spaces left by the paper.

## Main Contributions

1.  **Proposed the first complete scheme to directly feed front-end multichannel enhancement/separation outputs into the VT acoustic model** (modifying the second-stage model within the two-stage VT framework), using the TAC block to complete inter-channel fusion within the acoustic model, replacing the traditional interface of "channel selection + discard," and verifying its effectiveness on Apple-scale internal data (overall FRR relative reduction 7.5%, highest relative reduction 30% in quiet conditions, Table 1 and Section 5.3).
2.  **Identified and quantitatively characterized the structural deficiency of permutation-invariant multichannel modeling in VT tasks**: VT requires target speaker priors, while permutation-invariant operations naturally do not provide them. The 58.5% relative deterioration of vanilla TAC in human voice interference conditions (Table 1) is direct experimental evidence for this argument.
3.  **Proposed the Modified TAC Block (Equations 5-7)**: Injects the result of traditional channel selection as an asymmetric input into TAC, treating the selected channel differently with a dedicated transformation path, restoring target speaker localization capability while preserving TAC's lightness, and pulling the noisy condition from significant regression to tying with the baseline.
4.  **Provided a multichannel architecture paradigm adapted for on-device use**: The three-stage decoupling of TAC (channel fusion) + Average Pooling (channel compression) + Transformer (time-frequency modeling) concentrates multichannel incremental cost in a pre-pended block, keeping the backbone computational load identical to the single-channel baseline, and avoiding the computational explosion of quadratic complexity × number of channels from per-channel self-attention (Section 4.3).
5.  **Demonstration in engineering methodology**: Proved that multi-scenario gains can be obtained by prepending only a 1.7M parameter fusion module (4.8M → 6.5M) without retraining the front-end, changing the first-stage model, or significantly modifying the second-stage backbone, while also gaining system properties of array-agnosticism and flexible channel numbers.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

-  **Multi-speaker scenarios still do not surpass the baseline**: The model with selected channel is 7.12% under noisy conditions, while the baseline is 6.75% (Table 1), indicating that the current "concatenation-style injection of selected channel" mechanism can only prevent loss, not surpass. The deep reason is that the selected channel is only concatenated as one feature stream, and the model's utilization of it is still soft; whereas the baseline's channel selection is a hard decision, and in human voice interference, the target channel is often truly picked out by the first-stage score.
-  **Distribution inconsistency of selected channels between training and inference**: During training, the output of the enhancement module is used as the pseudo selected channel (Section 5.2), while during inference, the first-stage channel selection result is used. The distributions of the two are different (enhanced signal vs. enhanced or separated signal picked by score). This gap is not evaluated in the paper and may be part of the reason why it failed to surpass the baseline under noisy conditions.
-  **Average pooling is irreversible compression**: To save computational cost, the N channels are compressed into one after TAC (Section 4.3), and fine-grained information between channels is compressed before entering the Transformer, which subsequent layers cannot access again. This is a trade-off between computational cost and performance, and theoretically, there are better learnable compression methods.
-  **Inter-channel modeling depth is only one layer**: All multichannel interactions are concentrated in the pre-pended single TAC block, whereas in speech separation literature, TAC is usually used repeatedly at multiple levels. The cross-channel compensation patterns learned by a single-layer TAC may be insufficient to handle complex multi-speaker superposition.
-  **Actual on-device overhead is not quantified**: The paper reports parameter counts (4.8M/6.1M/6.5M), but key on-device indicators such as runtime latency, FLOPS, memory usage, and power consumption are not reported; although the second-stage model runs only on candidate segments, the true cost of doubling feature extraction (four log-mel streams) and fusion computation brought by the TAC block cannot be assessed.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

-  **Based entirely on internal data, unable to reproduce and compare externally**: The training set of 2.3 million utterances, six-microphone smart speakers, and real wake words are Apple private. The composition of the 2.3 million utterances (language, number of speakers, duration) is not reported in detail in the paper. The 1300 positive samples are also on the small side for analysis subdivided by four conditions (about 300+ samples per condition), and differences between conditions may be affected by sample size.
-  **Only one combination of channel number and array morphology**: The front-end fixedly outputs four channels (1 enhanced + 3 separated), and the TAC's "arbitrary channel number" design capability is not experimentally verified; the mismatch between training/test arrays is compensated by the front-end's fixed four channels, but the impact of this mismatch itself is not quantified.
-  **Lack of key ablations**: Empirical comparison between per-channel self-attention vs. average pooling, the impact of the number of TAC blocks, the impact of the pseudo selected channel strategy, and the interaction between ±3 frames of context and multichannel input are not reported by the paper; the two important assertions that "simple concatenation loses to baseline" and "increasing model size is useless" are only briefly mentioned in preliminary experiments without providing numbers (not reported by the paper).
-  **Single wake word, single language, single device form**: The transferability of conclusions to other wake word lengths, multiple languages, and near-field devices like earphones is unknown; there is also no head-to-head comparison with cross-channel attention methods like [8][9], only citing their computational defects as a reason for not comparing.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

-  **Stronger target speaker mechanism**: Upgrade the selected channel from "concatenated feature" to gating or bias modulation (e.g., using it to generate attention biases for each channel), or introduce speaker vectors for personalized VT, allowing the multichannel model to truly surpass the channel selection baseline in human voice interference scenarios rather than just tying.
-  **Differentiable/soft channel selection joint training**: End-to-end joint optimization of channel selection and the second-stage model to eliminate the training-inference inconsistency of pseudo selected channels; even allowing the first-stage model and TAC fusion to share representations, reducing the overall resident cost.
-  **Multi-layer TAC and learnable channel compression**: Interleave TAC blocks between Transformer layers, using learnable weighted summation or low-rank projection to replace hard average pooling, approaching the expressive power of per-channel self-attention within computational constraints.
-  **Extension to first-stage models and more channel numbers**: Verify whether prepending TAC to the first-stage small model can also bring multichannel gains (currently the first-stage still scores each channel independently), and verify the array-agnostic characteristics in 6/8 channel front-ends and distributed microphones (multi-device collaborative listening).
-  **Public benchmarks and completion of on-device metrics**: Reproduce on public multichannel KWS data, and complete latency/FLOPS/power data, making this "front-end multichannel + TAC fusion" paradigm evaluable and iterable by the community.
