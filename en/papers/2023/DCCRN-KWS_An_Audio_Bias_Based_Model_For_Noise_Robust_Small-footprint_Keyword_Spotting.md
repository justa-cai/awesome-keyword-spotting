# DCCRN-KWS: an audio bias based model for noise robust small-footprint keyword spotting

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Shubo Lv, Xiong Wang (co-first authors), Sining Sun, Long Ma, Lei Xie (corresponding author) (Audio Speech and Language Processing Research Group ASLP@NPU, Northwestern Polytechnical University; Tencent Technologies Co., Ltd., Beijing)
- **Date**: May 2023 (arXiv:2305.12331, v3 updated on June 13, 2023)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12331
- **Keywords**: keyword spotting, speech enhancement, audio context bias, DCCRN, multi-task learning, noise robustness, low-resource edge deployment

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The task of Keyword Spotting (KWS) is to determine whether a preset keyword is present in the input speech signal. As the most typical KWS application, Wake-up Word (WuW) detection is the first step in user-voice interaction with smart devices: the device must be on standby listening all day and night, waiting for a "ding-dong" or "Hello Xiao X" to be called out before lighting up the entire subsequent interaction chain. The starting point of this paper is built on a very specific physical scenario: there is usually an interaction distance between the user and the device. The speech signal attenuates as it propagates through the air, and is superimposed with environmental noise and room reverberation. By the time it reaches the microphone, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is often already very low. The experimental design of the paper directly reflects the severity of this pain point—the internal test set was recorded under far-field noise conditions, with SNR ranges for positive and negative samples as low as -6 to -2 dB (Section 3.1); when testing on the public HIMIA dataset with added noise, it was specifically compressed to three low-SNR levels of -5, 0, and 5 dB (Section 3.1). That is, this is not a robustness test at the level of "adding pink noise in the lab," but a survival problem under real living rooms, real distances, and real background noise.

Another constraint is edge-side deployment. Wake-up word detection must run locally on the device (privacy and power consumption do not allow cloud processing), and the computing power on consumer-grade smart speakers, car infotainment systems, and wearable devices is very limited. The paper explicitly chose the RK3326 Cortex-A35 with a clock frequency of 1.5 GHz as a low-resource platform for inference testing (Section 3.4, Table 1), which shows that the authors took "whether it can be truly deployed" as a hard constraint from the beginning, rather than just brushing up metrics on server GPUs. This constraint directly rules out an intuitive solution: concatenating a complete deep speech enhancement model and a KWS model in series, as the computational cost would be unacceptable—the actual results of the joint-train comparison system in the paper show a CPU usage of 94% and a Real-Time Factor (RTF) of 2.85 (Table 1), meaning it cannot even achieve real-time performance.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper systematically reviews existing routes for noise-robust KWS in the introduction and points out their deficiencies one by one:

- **Traditional signal processing enhancement**: When multi-channel signals can be collected, microphone arrays can be used for beamforming or multi-channel enhancement; for single-channel, traditional methods apply spectral suppression gain (or filters) to noisy signals under the framework of statistical signal processing theory. These methods have weak suppression capabilities for non-stationary noise (sudden noise, keyboard sounds, music) because their statistical assumptions (such as short-term stationarity of noise) often do not hold in real environments.
- **Cascaded usage of neural network speech enhancement**: Deep learning models speech enhancement as a supervised learning problem, trained on simulated clean-noisy speech pairs, and has become mainstream in denoising capabilities (especially for non-stationary noise); networks like SDD-Net and DCCRN that explicitly model complex spectra have performed outstandingly in recent denoising competitions (INTERSEECH 2020/2021 DNS Challenge). Advances in speech enhancement have also driven downstream speech recognition applications: the front-end enhancement module can be independently optimized first and then connected to the acoustic model, or jointly optimized with the acoustic model. Work integrating DCUnet with multi-channel acoustic models through multi-task learning (Reference [12]) has already proven that the value of front-end and back-end integration can be directly transferred to KWS.
- **Limitations of existing KWS robustness solutions**: For the KWS task itself, there is actually a prior that is ignored by most solutions—keywords are fixed and limited in number, and linguistic and acoustic information is constrained and known. Existing work has made attempts in this direction: TDSE (Reference [14]) uses neural networks for text-dependent speech enhancement, specifically recovering clean speech signals for specific keywords; open-vocabulary KWS (Reference [15]) uses attention for text-audio cross-modal matching and applies denoising loss to the acoustic embedding network to improve noise robustness. However, these two solutions have two common gaps: First, the bias information goes through the "text" channel. Audio-text cross-modal matching is more indirect than audio-audio similarity calculation, and does not fully utilize the acoustic details carried in real keyword audio samples; Second, they do not answer the question of "how the enhancement module and KWS model share computation"—if the enhancement network is only for KWS but has to run the full process of "enhancing to time-domain waveform, re-extracting features, and then entering KWS," the computational redundancy is unacceptable on the edge side.

In addition, directly concatenating the enhancement network and KWS network into a cascaded structure and training end-to-end using only KWS loss (i.e., without enhancement supervision signals), the paper cites Reference [12] pointing out that this direct combination is inferior to a multi-task learning framework with two explicit optimization objectives, and the overall complexity is higher.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

In summary, this paper aims to simultaneously satisfy four mutually restrictive requirements: First, significantly reduce the miss rate (at the same false alarm rate) under far-field noise conditions as low as -6 dB; Second, fully utilize the task prior of "keywords are known," but inject it in the form of audio rather than text, so that the bias information is directly comparable and can assist the enhancement task; Third, share the computation of the enhancement network and KWS network so that no fraction is wasted during inference—the enhancement decoder only exists during training and is completely discarded during inference; Fourth, control the RTF to be within 1 and leave enough CPU margin on a low-resource platform at the RK3326 level. The intersection of these four requirements is the design space of DCCRN-KWS.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall skeleton of DCCRN-KWS is a multi-task learning (MTL) framework (Fig. 2): the main task is keyword detection, and the auxiliary task is speech enhancement. The front-end selects DCCRN (Deep Complex Convolution Recurrent Network, Reference [6])—it starts from CRN, introduces complex convolution and LSTM, adopts an encoder-decoder Unet structure, and has superior performance in noise suppression. The motivation for choosing DCCRN over other enhancement networks lies in its complex-domain modeling capability: the amplitude and phase of speech are modeled simultaneously, which is consistent with the backend KWS directly connecting to the complex features output by the encoder (rather than the enhanced time-domain waveform).

There are three key architectural decisions, each with a clear "why":

**Decision 1: Share the encoder, rather than cascade.** The most intuitive approach is to directly cascade DCCRN and the KWS model, allowing the DCCRN front-end to learn a hidden representation beneficial to KWS on its own without enhancement signal supervision, relying entirely on the optimization signal from the KWS backend. The paper rejects this solution for two reasons: first, the overall complexity increases; second, Reference [12] has proven that this direct combination is inferior to MTL with two explicit optimization objectives. The deeper reason is: if there is only KWS loss, the representation learning of the encoder has no constraint that "speech content must be preserved," and the network may completely learn shortcut features that are effective for KWS on the training set but have poor generalization to noise changes; while the SI-SNR enhancement loss explicitly requires the decoder to reconstruct clean speech from the encoder output, effectively forcing the encoder to preserve the essential structure of speech content, which provides a stable anchor for keyword discrimination under noise conditions.

**Decision 2: Discard the enhancement decoder during inference.** The entire architecture is jointly optimized during training, and the speech enhancement part is simply discarded during inference—only the shared encoder continues to work. This is the essential difference between this solution and the joint-train cascaded system: in the cascaded system, the enhancement decoder must run completely during inference and output enhanced speech, re-extract features, resulting in actual CPU usage of 94% and RTF 2.85 (Table 1); whereas DCCRN-KWS only runs the encoder plus the KWS module, with CPU 35% and RTF 0.67 (Table 1), costing less than one-quarter of the cascaded solution.

**Decision 3: Use DTC blocks for the KWS backend.** The backend KWS module consists of stacked Dilated Temporal Convolution (DTC) blocks (Reference [16], i.e., the NPU team's system in the 2020 Personalized Voice Trigger Challenge): first, a dilated depthwise separable 1D convolution is used to obtain temporal context, then two pointwise convolutions integrate hidden features from different channels, and finally a fully connected layer plus softmax outputs the posterior probability of keywords (Fig. 1). The motivation for choosing DTC over RNN is streaming and efficiency: all convolutions are made causal, with no recursive dependency for frame-by-frame inference, and the depthwise separable structure compresses the computational cost within the small footprint budget.

On top of this skeleton, the paper adds three plug-and-play modules: Audio Context Bias, Feature Merge, and Complex Context Linear, which are elaborated below.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Multi-task Loss (Equation 3)**: The speech enhancement task uses the typical time-domain SI-SNR loss (Reference [20], proposed by Conv-TasNet), and the KWS task uses Binary Cross Entropy (BCE):

$$L_{BCE} = -y_i^* \ln(y_i) - (1-y_i^*)\ln(1-y_i)$$
$$L = L_{SI\text{-}SNR} + L_{BCE}$$

where $y_i = H(x_i;\theta) \in \{0,1\}$ is the posterior predicted by the KWS model $H$ for the $i$-th frame, and $y_i^*$ is the ground truth label. The two losses are added directly without weighting coefficients. Why use SI-SNR instead of frequency-domain Mask loss: SI-SNR directly constrains time-domain reconstruction quality, and its scale invariance makes it more stable for training samples with different energy levels, and it is a standard choice verified by extensive experience in the enhancement field; importantly, its role here is not to "output enhanced speech," but to provide a gradient signal for content fidelity to the shared encoder.

**Frame-level Energy Visualization (Equation 1)**: To analyze what each encoder layer is actually doing, the paper defines frame-level energy:

$$m_t = \frac{\sum_c \sum_f \sqrt{\Re(E)^2_{c,f,t} + \Im(E)^2_{c,f,t}}}{M}$$

where $E_{c,f,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times F \times T}$ is the output of a certain encoder layer, $C, F, T$ are the number of channels, frequency bins, and time frames respectively, $\Re(E)$ and $\Im(E)$ are the real and imaginary parts, and $M$ is a normalization factor. The physical meaning of this quantity is to calculate the L2 norm of the complex features by channel and frequency, and then normalize by frame, obtaining the "energy intensity curve" for each frame. By visualizing the energy of each layer with a keyword sample and a non-keyword sample respectively (Fig. 3), the most important empirical observation of this paper is obtained: in keyword samples, the keyword segment presents significantly higher energy relative to the rest, and this phenomenon becomes more significant in higher encoder layers; non-keyword samples do not show this phenomenon—the energy distribution patterns at each layer are similar. It is concluded that the DCCRN encoder spontaneously learns to "enhance the energy of the keyword part" under multi-task training, which can be utilized by the KWS module. This observation is the direct basis for the Feature Merge module—since each layer has discriminative information and it is more significant in higher layers, there is no need to use only the last layer.

**Label Construction Strategy**: During training, for keyword audio segments, only the last 10 frames near the end are taken as positive samples (label 1); the remaining frames in the keyword segment are treated as "ambiguous frames" and discarded directly, not participating in training. All frames of negative sample speech are treated as negative samples (label 0). The rationality of this strategy lies in: the moment the keyword is finished is the golden moment for discrimination, while the intermediate frames within the keyword have ambiguous labels for frame-level binary classification (half-said is neither a complete keyword nor a non-keyword), forcing labels would introduce noisy supervision. This is a typical approach for handling "frames within keywords" in frame-level KWS training.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Audio Context Bias

This is the core innovation in the paper's title, borrowing ideas from the context bias strategy in ASR (Reference [17], Deep Context): in ASR, embeddings of the context vocabulary are injected into the decoding process to bias the model's attention to specific words; this paper transfers this idea to KWS, but makes a key change—**do not use text embeddings, use audio embeddings**.

Specific process: First, select and construct a keyword audio list (list) from the keyword corpus. The list can be fixed (selected once and no longer changed) or variable (randomly sampled from the corpus each time). The keyword audio samples in the list are sent to an embedding extractor to extract bias embeddings. The extractor selects ECAPA-TDNN (Reference [18]), because it excels at aggregating information from short audio segments and has reached SOTA in speaker verification—keyword registration audio is exactly a short segment scenario. Finally, the average of all bias embedding vectors on the list is calculated and concatenated with the output of the last layer of the DCCRN encoder as the input to the KWS module (Fig. 2). The embedding dimension is set to 192, ECAPA-TDNN is implemented using the SpeechBrain toolkit (Reference [19]), and is jointly trained with the entire DCCRN-KWS structure.

Why use audio instead of text? The paper gives two reasons: First, measuring audio-audio similarity is more direct than audio-text similarity (cross-modal matching requires learning an alignment space, while same-modal similarity is naturally comparable); text bias is more flexible for custom keywords, but that is not the purpose of this paper. Second, audio bias can better utilize real audio samples, further helping the speech enhancement task, thereby improving the robustness of keyword discrimination under noise conditions—the registration audio itself carries details of the keyword in real acoustics (timbre, duration, formant structure), and this information forms a mutual benefit in the collaborative training of the enhancement branch. The paper also did a sanity check in the ablation study using learnable bias embeddings (initializing a set of parameters directly to update with model training), verifying that the design of "extracting from real audio" is not an optional decoration.

The runtime overhead analysis is the most beautiful part of this module: when the keyword audio list is fixed, the bias embedding is a fixed constant during inference, and the embedding extractor does not work at all during runtime—so the additional complexity of the audio bias module during the inference period can be ignored. Actual tests also confirm this: after adding AudioBias, CPU usage increased from 35% to 36%, and RTF from 0.67 to 0.69 (Table 1), with almost zero cost. This is a typical design of "heavy prior during training, zero cost during inference."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Feature Merge

The motivation comes directly from the energy observation in Equation 1: since the outputs of each encoder layer carry keyword discriminative information (and higher layers are more significant), taking only the output of the last layer is a waste of information. The Feature Merge module aggregates the outputs of each layer: initialize a set of feature merge ratios $w_i$, with the last layer ratio initialized to 1 and the rest to 0; since the dimensions of the preceding encoder layers are twice that of the last layer, downsample the outputs of these layers first, then perform weighted averaging to obtain the merged output $E'$:

$$E' = \sum_i w_i * \text{downsample}(E_i) / \sum_i |w_i|$$

where $w_i$ are learnable weights during training and fixed during inference. There are two noteworthy "whys" in the design details: First, initializing with "last layer is 1, others are 0" means that the initial state of the module is equivalent to not merging (using only the last layer), and training starts from a neighboring region of converged behavior, avoiding random initialization from destroying the pre-trained information flow; Second, the denominator $\sum_i |w_i|$ performs normalization, ensuring that the magnitude of the merged output does not drift with the absolute size of the weights, stabilizing training. Visualization verification (Fig. 3 (d) and (h)) shows: after merging, the significance of the keyword segment is further enhanced, while non-keyword samples remain almost unchanged—i.e., feature merge is a directional amplification of discriminative information, rather than indiscriminate amplification of energy. The actual overhead is also minimal: CPU 36%, RTF 0.70 (Table 1).

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Complex Context Linear

This module solves the "context vs. complexity" dilemma when fusing bias embeddings with encoder outputs. The conventional fusion strategy is to directly concatenate the bias embedding and encoder output through a fully connected layer, but this does not utilize context; and context information is essential for KWS—keyword discrimination obviously relies on evidence accumulation over continuous frames. Another strategy is to concatenate the history of several frames of encoder features and the bias embedding together as the input to the fully connected layer, but the paper points out that this simple concatenation will lead to an explosion in computational complexity: the encoder output is complex and high-dimensional, stacking context frame by frame causes the input dimension of the fully connected layer to expand exponentially.

The paper's solution (Fig. 4): First, separate the encoder output by real and imaginary parts, concatenate them with the bias embedding respectively, and then use the combination of the current frame (t) and the context features of the previous two frames (t-1, t-2) as the input to the fully connected layer. Since the real path and imaginary path independently pass through linear layers and then combine (similar to the idea of grouped convolution), the complexity is almost halved compared to simple context concatenation. The dimension comparison intuitively shows the compression effect (Section 3.2): without audio bias, the fully connected layer from the encoder output to the KWS module is 1024×128; without complex context linear (i.e., ordinary concatenation fusion) it is 1216×128 (1024-dimensional encoder features concatenated with 192-dimensional bias embeddings); with complex context linear it is 608×64×3—the real and imaginary paths each have 608 dimensions (1024 split in half then concatenated with bias information), 64-dimensional output, and 3 frames of context. The number of context frames is taken as 3 (the previous two frames plus the current frame). Actual overhead: CPU 38%, RTF 0.72 (Table 1), still lightweight.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to independently optimized enhancement front-end plus KWS cascade (Reference [10] route), DCCRN-KWS shares the encoder and discards the decoder during inference, avoiding the generation of enhanced waveforms and secondary feature extraction; compared to joint-train cascade (Table 1 comparison system: DCCRN two losses jointly trained, enhanced speech sent to DTC-KWS), RTF drops from 2.85 to 0.67, CPU usage drops from 94% to 35%, while the performance route remains unchanged. Compared to DCUnet plus multi-channel acoustic model MTL (Reference [12]), this paper advances this idea to the single-channel KWS task, and adds the audio bias, a KWS-specific prior injection mechanism, targeting the task characteristic of "fixed keywords." Compared to text-dependent TDSE (Reference [14], recovering clean speech for specific keywords) and open-vocabulary cross-modal matching (Reference [15]), this paper uses same-modal audio embeddings for bias, and the bias information serves both the enhancement and discrimination branches simultaneously. Compared to pure KWS models (without front-end), the miss rate is significantly lower at the same false alarm rate (Fig. 5(a)), at the cost of RTF increasing from 0.27 to 0.67 (Table 1)—exchanging about 2.5 times the computation for usability under noise conditions.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

**Internal Dataset** (used for large-scale ablation): Detects the Mandarin keyword "ding-dang" (ding1-dang2-ding1-dang2). Positive sample training data is 158 hours of keyword-specific speech; negative sample training data is 139 hours of speech collected from YouTube. The test set was recorded under far-field noise conditions, containing 239 positive samples and 12,480 negative samples, with an SNR range of -6 to -2 dB (Section 3.1). Training data is generated on the fly, with added noise SNR range 0 to 15 dB, reverberation simulated using the image method, RT60 from 0.05 seconds to 0.95 seconds; internal task noise comes from the internal noise library plus freesound, totaling 174 hours; 1 to 4 types of noise are randomly superimposed on each speech; speakers and noise do not overlap between training and testing.

**HIMIA Public Dataset** (Reference [21]): A text-dependent keyword library recorded with high-fidelity microphones, the keyword is Mandarin "ni hao mi ya" (ni2-hao3-mi1-ya4). The training set contains 10 hours of keyword samples from 254 speakers; the test set comes from 3,520 keyword samples from another 44 speakers. Negative samples are selected from the librivox corpus, 90 hours for training; during testing, another 3,600 negative samples are randomly selected from librivox, totaling 7,120 audio clips for testing. Since speaker labels are known, speaker-dependent keyword bias audio lists were additionally studied. Negative samples (training and testing) are randomly cropped to short segments of 1 to 3 seconds to avoid the model overfitting to specific durations—because positive sample durations are also between 1 and 3 seconds. During testing, HIMIA samples are added with noise at three SNR levels of -5, 0, and 5 dB (Section 3.1). HIMIA training noise is 56 hours of freesound.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **ROC Curve (Internal Set, Fig 5)**: Plots the miss rate (FR) corresponding to each false alarm (FA). The lower the miss rate at the same false alarm rate, the better. Rationale: Product constraints for wake-up systems are usually expressed as "allowing a few false wake-ups per day," i.e., examining missed wake-ups under a fixed false alarm budget; ROC directly corresponds to this engineering language; and FAR/FRR change continuously with the decision threshold, so single-point numbers would mask the impact of operating point selection.
- **Wake-up Accuracy (HIMIA, Fig 6)**: Statistics are collected under the setting of exposing continuous noisy speech for 10 hours with at most 1 false alarm trigger. This is the industry-standard wake-up rate evaluation protocol (10 hours, 1 false wake-up), fixing the false alarm constraint as an evaluation condition, making the wake-up rate comparable horizontally and vertically.
- **RTF and CPU Usage (Table 1)**: Measured on the RK3326 Cortex-A35 low-resource platform with float32 precision and ONNX runtime engine. Rationale: One of the selling points of this paper is deployability; server-side FLOPs/parameter numbers cannot represent the real performance of such ARM platforms, so direct testing on the target hardware is required.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

On the internal dataset (Fig 5(d) total comparison): The pure KWS model (without DCCRN) performs the worst under low-SNR far-field conditions; performance improves significantly after adding the DCCRN multi-task framework (Fig 5(a)), indicating that the explicit speech enhancement module in the MTL framework can indeed assist the KWS module in improving performance. On the HIMIA dataset (Fig 6): Regardless of low-SNR, high-SNR, or clean scenarios, each proposed module shows effectiveness. Specific miss rates and wake-up rates are presented in graphs in the paper, and specific numbers are not reported in the text (paper does not report).

The inference efficiency comparison (Table 1, RK3326 platform) is the hardest quantitative evidence in the entire paper:

| Model | CPU Usage (%) | RTF |
|---|---|---|
| Pure KWS | 16 | 0.27 |
| DCCRN-KWS | 35 | 0.67 |
| + AudioBias | 36 | 0.69 |
| + FM | 36 | 0.70 |
| + CCL | 38 | 0.72 |
| DCCRN-KWS-joint-train | 94 | 2.85 |

Three conclusions: First, the complete DCCRN-KWS (including all modules) has an RTF of 0.72, which is feasible for real-time on Cortex-A35, and the three plug-and-play modules only increase the RTF by 0.05 and CPU by 3 percentage points; Second, the joint-train cascaded system has an RTF of 2.85 and CPU 94%, which is completely unacceptable, confirming the necessity of the architectural route of sharing the encoder and discarding the decoder during inference; Third, compared to the pure KWS of 0.27, the cost of noise robustness is 2.7 times the RTF, which is a typical accuracy-power trade-off point in products.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

Ablation on the internal dataset (Fig 5) verifies the four components item by item:

- **DCCRN Module (Fig 5(a))**: Performance improves significantly after adding DCCRN, proving that explicit speech enhancement supervision in the MTL framework is of substantial help to KWS.
- **Audio Bias (Fig 5(b))**: After adding bias, the miss rate is lower while the false alarm rate is kept at the same level as the no-bias system, verifying the effectiveness of keyword audio embeddings. Two detailed findings: Fixed keyword audio list (Fix) performs better than variable list during training. The authors speculate that the fixed list makes it easier for the model to train and optimize—the intuition behind this explanation is that variable bias embeddings are equivalent to continuously changing the input distribution for the network, and the network needs to additionally learn the ability to be "invariant to bias changes," dispersing the optimization budget; Embeddings extracted from real audio by ECAPA-TDNN are better than learnable embeddings (sanity check). The authors explain from two aspects: real keyword audio embeddings contain more structural information of the keyword, and embeddings extracted from real samples have a more direct similarity calculation with the internal representations of the DCCRN-KWS model (same acoustic space, no need to learn alignment).
- **Feature Merge (Fig 5(c))**: Performance further improves after adding, consistent with the visualization in Fig 3—feature merge amplifies the discriminativeness of the keyword part, helping the KWS module distinguish keywords from other content.
- **Complex Context Linear (Fig 5(c))**: The system achieves the best performance after adoption, indicating that the combination of context information and bias embeddings can better discriminate keywords.

There is an additional finding on HIMIA: speaker-dependent audio lists are better than speaker-independent lists—because the embeddings extracted from speaker-dependent lists contain both keyword information and speaker information, effectively giving away speaker prior for free (Fig 6). Meanwhile, the improvement of each method on HIMIA is smaller than on the internal dataset. The authors attribute this to the registration audio in HIMIA being relatively shorter, and empirically, longer bias keyword audio exports more robust keyword embeddings—this also points out a sensitive factor of this method: bias quality depends on the sufficiency of registration audio.

## Main Contributions

1. **Proposed the DCCRN-KWS front-end and back-end integration framework**: Integrates the speech enhancement front-end and KWS back-end through multi-task learning—DCCRN is used for speech enhancement, and its encoder output is simultaneously coupled to the KWS model; the enhancement decoder is only used during training and discarded during inference. While maintaining the benefits of enhancement supervision, the inference overhead is compressed to less than one-quarter of the cascaded solution (RTF 0.67 vs 2.85, Table 1).
2. **First to introduce the context bias idea to KWS in audio form**: Borrowing the ASR context bias strategy, ECAPA-TDNN is used to extract embeddings from the keyword audio list and inject them into the KWS module, and during inference the embedding is a fixed constant and the extractor does not work, so the additional runtime complexity can be ignored (RTF increment 0.02, Table 1).
3. **Feature Merge Module based on inter-layer energy observation of the encoder**: Using the frame-level energy analysis in Equation 1, it was found that the encoder spontaneously focuses on keyword energy (Fig 3), and based on this, a learnable ratio hierarchical weighted aggregation (Equation 2) was designed, initially equivalent to using only the last layer, and training directionally enhances discriminativeness.
4. **Complex Context Linear Module**: Splits the real and imaginary parts, concatenates each with the bias embedding, and feeds three frames of context into the fully connected layer (Fig 4), obtaining context modeling capability at the cost of approximately grouped convolution, compressing the fusion dimension from 1216×128 to 608×64×3 (Section 3.2), avoiding complexity explosion.
5. **Closed-loop actual testing on low-resource platforms**: System-level actual testing of RTF and CPU is given on the RK3326 Cortex-A35 (Table 1), and the RTF of 0.72 for the complete configuration proves the edge-side deployability of the solution, not just theoretical complexity on the GPU.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Cost of Changing Words and Loss of Flexibility**: The paper explicitly admits that text bias is more flexible for custom keywords but "is not the purpose of this paper." Audio bias requires preparing a real audio list for each keyword. Changing the wake-up word means re-collecting/selecting audio and retraining (the embedding extractor and the overall model are jointly trained), and it cannot be done like text bias where only the vocabulary is changed without changing the model. This is a structural shortcoming for product forms that require user-customized wake-up words.
- **Dependence on Registration Audio Duration and Quality**: The reason for the smaller improvement on HIMIA compared to the internal set is attributed to shorter registration audio (Section 3.3.2), indicating that the robustness of the bias embedding is strongly correlated with the sufficiency of registration audio; the paper does not give a lower limit or recommended value for the duration of registration audio (paper does not report), which is an unquantified sensitive hyperparameter in engineering implementation.
- **Applicability Premise of Speaker-Dependent Gain**: Speaker-dependent lists are better than speaker-independent lists (Fig 6), but this requires knowing speaker labels and having user registration audio; how to maintain the bias list in real products with multi-user shared devices and speaker switching scenarios is not discussed by the paper (paper does not report).
- **Language and Keyword Coverage**: All experiments only cover two Mandarin keywords (internal set "ding-dang", HIMIA "ni hao mi ya"), and scenarios such as English, multi-syllable length changes, and multi-keyword parallel bias have not been verified (paper does not report).
- **Performance Ceiling under Causal Constraints**: The model is purely causal and does not consider future frames (Section 3.2), which is a necessary cost for streaming, but also means there is a natural upper limit compared to solutions allowing a small amount of lookahead; the paper does not compare with non-causal variants to quantify this cost (paper does not report).

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Core Performance Numbers Not Reported in Text**: The main results of the internal set and HIMIA are only presented as ROC curves (Fig 5) and bar charts (Fig 6). The main text does not give any specific miss rate or wake-up rate numbers (paper does not report), making it difficult for readers to directly compare with table numbers from other papers, and the cost of reproduction and comparison is high.
- **Lack of Parameter Count and Model Size Reporting**: The paper gives actual testing of RTF and CPU, but does not report the parameter count and model storage size for each configuration (paper does not report), which is a key supplementary indicator for a paper with a "small footprint" positioning—RTF only reflects computation, not storage pressure.
- **Multi-task Loss Weights Not Explored**: $L = L_{SI\text{-}SNR} + L_{BCE}$ is added with equal weights directly (Equation 3), and no sensitivity analysis was performed on the weight coefficients (paper does not report); in MTL literature, the loss ratio usually significantly affects the direction of shared representations.
- **Combination Method of Ablation**: Ablation is performed by adding modules one by one (Fig 5), but no fine-grained analysis of reverse dismantling or interactions between modules (e.g., whether there is overlap in benefits between AudioBias and FM) is reported (paper does not report).
- **Limited Baseline Coverage**: There is no horizontal comparison with other contemporaneous noise-robust KWS methods (such as TDSE-type or data augmentation-type solutions) under the same protocol. The comparison objects are mainly its own ablation variants plus the joint-train system (paper does not report external SOTA baseline tables).

### Possible Future Improvement Directions

- **Hybrid Bias**: Combining text and audio dual-channel bias, where the text channel provides flexibility in changing words and the audio channel provides acoustic details, may be a path to achieve both custom wake-up words and robustness; the text embedding space of open-vocabulary cross-modal matching (Reference [15]) can be directly grafted.
- **Systematic Study of Registration Audio Duration and Bias Robustness**: The paper itself points out that "longer bias keywords export more robust embeddings" is empirical. Turning it into a data-supported design guide (shortest registration duration, number of speakers, sampling diversity) is a follow-up work with engineering value.
- **Productization of Personalization and Speaker Bias**: The gain of speaker-dependent lists hints at the possibility of merging KWS bias with the speaker verification registration process—once registration serves both tasks.
- **Further Study of Training-Test SNR Strategy**: Training adds noise online 0-15 dB while internal testing is -6 to -2 dB. The model still benefits significantly from SNRs lower than the training distribution (Fig 5). Expanding training strategies for lower SNRs (such as curriculum-style noise addition) may further push the limits.
- **Quantization and Lower Bit Deployment**: The current actual test is float32 plus ONNX runtime (Section 3.4), and RTF 0.72 still has room for reduction; combined with quantization (such as the binarization route of BiFSMN in the future), the complete configuration can be compressed to the level of pure KWS overhead.
- **Multi-keyword and Incremental Learning**: Real devices often need to listen to multiple wake-up phrases simultaneously. The extension mechanism of the audio bias list (list increment, embedding concatenation method) is a natural extension direction.
