## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

First, let us establish the necessary domain context for readers of this repository: most papers in this repository focus on Keyword Spotting (KWS) as speech wake-word detection (locating keywords within an audio stream). However, the "keyword spotting" in this paper refers to a task with the same name in the field of document images—specifically, Query-by-String (QbS), where, given a string query, one must find all rectangular regions on a page containing that word and rank them by similarity. Both domains share a core idea: they do not pursue full recognition but instead "locate keywords" within continuous media, using soft-selection to tolerate local recognition errors. For readers in the speech domain, the value of this paper lies in methodological transfer: the CTC loss (used here for image column sequences rather than audio frame sequences), integral image acceleration (analogous to efficient frame accumulation in speech), and counting-based relaxed representations are design choices rarely seen in speech KWS literature.

Returning to the problem space of the paper itself. The digitization of historical documents is a genuine need for libraries and archives. This paper is funded under the Bessarion project (T6YBΠ-00214) within the "OPEN INNOVATION IN CULTURE" framework, jointly funded by the EU and Greece, with the target scenario being the retrieval of manuscript collections. Complete Handwritten Text Recognition (HTR) requires outputting full-page character decoding, which is expensive and fragile for historical manuscripts with extreme variations in writing styles. Keyword retrieval serves as an alternative, requiring only a judgment of "does this region resemble the query word." Even if character decoding would otherwise fail, retrieval still has the chance to "recover" the word. This explains why KWS is treated in document analysis as a practical alternative to HTR rather than a sub-problem.

The paper further distinguishes the document scenario from natural scene (in-the-wild) text detection. This distinction directly determines the design of the method's inductive bias: First, the same character appears many times in documents, whereas in the wild, character appearance differences mainly come from perspective distortion and font diversity; document perspective distortion is negligible. Second, document information is highly structured (strong priors exist for the spatial organization of lines, words, and characters), allowing these priors to be encoded into the model structure—the 1×5 horizontal convolution kernels and the monotonic increase of "counting with distance" in binary search in this paper are explicit uses of such structural priors. Wild methods (such as Mask TextSpotter, Text Spotting Transformers) use general object detection frameworks to handle these priors, at the cost of requiring models that are large and highly general.

There are two core classification axes in the field: by query type, QbE (Word Image Query) and QbS (String Query); by whether the document is assumed to be pre-segmented into word or line images, segmentation-based and segmentation-free. This paper addresses segmentation-free QbS—assuming no prior knowledge of word locations and retrieving directly on the full-page image. The practical necessity of segmentation-free approaches lies in the fact that correctly segmenting a document page into words and lines is itself a non-trivial task, especially for manuscripts with complex table structures and extensive marginalia. Segmentation-based methods push the hardest sub-problem to the front end; segmentation errors propagate comprehensively to downstream retrieval.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper's review of segmentation-free directions reveals four categories of shortcomings:

- **Inherent dependency of segmentation-based representation methods**. A series of attribute representation methods, represented by PHOC (Pyramid of Histograms of Characters) embeddings, as well as alternative embeddings and recognition-based embedding systems by Wilkinson et al., are all built on the assumption that "cut word or line images have already been obtained." Word image inputs simplify the task but leave the segmentation difficulty to the front-end pipeline, which is unreliable for manuscripts with complex layouts.
- **Efficiency bottlenecks of sliding-window segmentation-free methods**. Sliding windows are the most direct approach for segmentation-free methods. BoF-HMM, character bigram indexing, and word-attribute-based sliding window frameworks are efficient variants. However, the essence of the sliding window framework is an exhaustive search over all possible window positions and scales. The paper explicitly quantifies this cost in the methodology section: the complexity of a naive sliding box is $O(N_r^3)$, where $N_r$ is the number of pixels in the downsampled feature map, which is infeasible for full-page documents. Ghosh et al. also used integral images for acceleration but remained within the exhaustive window framework.
- **Complexity costs of two-stage methods (candidate region generation + segmentation-based re-ranking) and end-to-end object detection methods**. Generating word region candidates and then re-ranking using segmentation-based methods like PHOC is another route. Candidate generation can be made end-to-end, following the latest literature in object detection, represented by Ctrl-F-Net (ICCV 2017) and ResNet50+FPN (ACCV 2020). The paper criticizes this route on three points: complex prediction heads and large models (ResNet50-level backbones); a predefined limit on the maximum number of detections, which conflicts with the document retrieval requirement of "any location, any quantity"; most critically, training requires document-level annotations—either manually annotating word boxes at the page level or generating synthetic data to cover the huge variation in word positions, both of which are costly.
- **Mismatch between evaluation metrics and task semantics**. Object detection uses mAP with IoU thresholds for evaluation, but IoU penalizes detections that "enclose the target word but are too large" too heavily. Figure 6 (left) of the paper provides a striking example: an arguably correct detection box (green) has an IoU of only 0.33 with the ground truth box (blue). This is particularly detrimental to the proposed method because its design philosophy is "constrain the box to contain the target word, not to constrain the tightness of the box"—loose boxes are a feature, not a defect, as they enable sub-word and multi-word retrieval. Using tightness metrics to evaluate inclusion-based designs is a methodological mismatch.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

Converging the above pain points into one sentence: How to achieve segmentation-free, high-efficiency (millisecond-level per query) keyword retrieval on full-page handwritten documents with retrieval accuracy exceeding complex end-to-end detection models, using only word-level supervision (no document-level annotations, no synthetic data) and a lightweight pure convolutional network of approximately 6M parameters. This overall goal breaks down into four sub-challenges that must be addressed one by one: First, transform the spatial search problem of "finding words" into a form that can be computed efficiently; Second, reduce the naive $O(N_r^3)$ search complexity to an engineering-usable magnitude; Third, resolve the inherent ambiguity of counting-based representations—character counting histograms are insensitive to character order, so "and" and "dan" have identical counting descriptions (Figure 5 left); Fourth, correct over-estimated boxes that enclose neighboring words (Figure 5 right) and align evaluation metrics with the detection semantics of "inclusion equals correctness."

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The core idea of the entire method is clearly stated in one sentence by the paper: "efficiently estimate the bounding box by counting character occurrences." The architecture consists of three components (Figure 1):

- **CNN Backbone**: A lightweight ResNet-style backbone. The first layer is a 7×7 convolution with stride 2 and 32 output channels, followed by cascaded 3×3 residual blocks (2 blocks of 64 channels, 4 blocks of 128 channels, 4 blocks of 256 channels), all convolutions followed by ReLU and Batch Normalization; 2×1 max pooling is used for downsampling between cascades, resulting in an overall downsampling factor of 1/8. The authors explicitly state that they did not deeply explore architecture choices, as efficiency, rather than accuracy limits, is the theme of the paper.
- **CNN Decoder**: Two convolutional layers output the character probability map $F$. The first layer is a 3×3 convolution with 128 channels, and the second layer is a 1×5 convolution with output channels equal to the character vocabulary size $C$. This flat 1×5 kernel is a direct encoding of the horizontal writing prior—looking at a longer temporal context along the writing direction, while only local aggregation is needed in the vertical direction. The entire decoder uses only convolutions, no recurrent structures, for very engineering reasons: recurrent alternatives (such as LSTM) must define sequence order, while the 2D structure of raw document pages cannot efficiently define this order; pure convolutions allow the same network to accept both word images and full-page images.
- **CNN Scaler**: Two 3×3 convolutions (128 channels, then 1 channel) output the scale map $S$, with a sigmoid at the end to constrain values to [0,1]; $s=1$ indicates that the pixel corresponds to a whole character.

Training is performed only on word-level images, with two auxiliary tricks: First, taking a larger area around the word as input allows the network to learn "noise such as adjacent characters may exist outside the box"; Second, applying a constant negative penalty to pixels outside the bounding box before column-wise max pooling, so they are naturally ignored in the max operation and do not participate in recognition. The total loss is $L_{CTC} + 10 \cdot L_{count}$, with weights set empirically; the authors explicitly state they did not explore these weights; the weight for the counting regression loss is larger because it is the main task of this method. Training configuration: single 1080Ti GPU, PyTorch, 80 epochs, Adam optimizer with cosine annealing schedule, initial learning rate 1e-3.

The inference pipeline (Figure 3) consists of four serial stages: Box proposal (binary search on y-axis, binary search on x-axis) → Candidate pruning → First-stage scoring (pyramid counting similarity plus threshold) → Second-stage scoring (CTC scoring plus threshold), with Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) applied after each of the two scoring stages.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Formalization of Counting (Eq. 1)**. Let $F$ be a 3D tensor of size $H_r \times W_r \times C$, the character-level probability map predicted by the network (softmax along the character dimension for each pixel), where $H_r \times W_r$ is the size of the original $H \times W$ input after downsampling; $S$ is a character-agnostic scale matrix of size $H_r \times W_r$. The scaled feature map is defined as the element-wise product $F_s[i,j,k] = F[i,j,k] \cdot S[i,j]$. Given a bounding box containing a word (start point $(s_i, s_j)$, end point $(e_i, e_j)$), the character occurrences are obtained by summation: $y_c = \sum \sum F_s(i,j)$, where $y_c \in \mathbb{R}^C$ (Eq. 1). The entire method is built on this decoupling of "probability $\times$ scale $\rightarrow$ summation."

Why must it be decoupled? The paper's argument is: the most direct counting regression approach—a DNN using mean squared error loss, taking word images as input, and directly regressing the character histogram—cannot be applied to page-level images. By decomposing counting into two sub-problems: "character probability of each feature point" and "scale of each point relative to the character it belongs to," page-level counting becomes the summation of arbitrary rectangular regions, and rectangular summation is exactly an operation that can be accelerated to $O(1)$ using integral images. The representation design directly serves the algorithmic efficiency later, which is the consistent design logic throughout the paper.

A notable detail about the scale map $S$: it has no direct supervision signal and is "implicitly learned." The authors' explanation is that the elegant combination of CTC loss and counting regression loss allows $S$ to automatically learn scale semantics. Mechanistically, this can be understood as follows: CTC loss anchors $F$ to have real recognition semantics (the character probabilities of each column must be able to spell out the target word), while the counting loss requires that the sum of the product of $F$ and $S$ within the box precisely equals the target histogram—since $F$ is pinned to recognition semantics, for the summation to converge to the correct count, $S$ is forced to express "what proportion of its belonging character this point represents." Using a strong supervision signal to anchor one branch and letting another branch be shaped automatically by structural constraints is one of the cleverest training signal designs in this paper.

**Complexity Chain**. The naive approach checks all possible bounding boxes for each pixel of the feature map and sums them, with complexity $O(N_r^3)$, where $N_r = H_r \times W_r$; integral images reduce the summation of rectangles of any size to an $O(1)$ constant operation (a traditional computer vision technique, with SURF features being a famous application); binary search reduces window search to $O(N_r \log N_r)$; candidate point pruning further reduces the constant factor. This complexity chain is the main evidence that "simple methods can also be efficient."

**IoW Metric (Figure 6 right)**. The new metric is defined as $IoW = \text{CorrectIntersection} / (\text{WordArea} + \text{ErroneousIntersections})$, where the numerator is the intersection between the detection box and the ground truth word region, and the denominator is the ground truth word area plus the sum of all erroneous intersections between the detection box and other (neighboring word) regions. The design semantics are very clear: as long as the box does not cut into other words, the size of the box is not penalized; once neighboring words are enclosed, the amount enclosed is penalized.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Probability-Scale Decoupled Character Counting Representation

Transforming character counting from "word-level regression" to "sum of per-pixel probability multiplied by per-pixel scale" is the foundational innovation of the entire paper. It brings threefold benefits: First, the representation naturally supports page-level inference; the network is trained on word images but can directly perform forward propagation on full-page images (Figure 2 shows page-level extracted $F$, $S$, $F_s$, and activation maps for characters 'e' and 'y'); Second, the summation form perfectly interfaces with integral images, and efficiency comes from representation rather than engineering optimization; Third, counting is insensitive to the spatial arrangement of characters, which is both a weakness (ambiguity) and a feature—boxes do not need to be tight, making sub-word and multi-word retrieval possible (Figure 9: multi-word hit for query "he came", hit for "fast" on the suffix of "breakfast", and simultaneous hits on printed reference text of different scales at the top of the page).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Segmentation-Free Scanning with Integral Image and Binary Search, plus Two-Level Pruning

The validity of binary search comes from a simple property of the feature map: starting from the start point and moving further away, the counting result is monotonically non-decreasing. Thus, the 2D search for "finding the end point" is split into two monotonic sub-tasks, each of which can be solved efficiently using binary search. The specific implementation operates only on the integral image of the scale $S$, in two steps: Step 1, find a rectangular region with a count of 1—equivalent to detecting a single character, whose side length is a reasonable estimate of the word height at that position; this step is query-independent and can be pre-computed once for each image (Figure 4a). Step 2, given the search area height, find the width that can accommodate the number of query characters (Figure 4b).

Why insist on the two-step "fix height first, then fix width" rather than direct 2D search? The paper provides a diagram of cross-line ambiguity (Figure 4c): when querying "and", combining the "a" at the end of the previous line and the "nd" at the beginning of the next line into one box yields the same correct counting histogram—this "correct counting across lines" is a spatial ambiguity of pure counting methods. By first anchoring the line height with "one character," the width search is restricted to a single line, structurally excluding solutions that拼 words across lines. This is a clean case of using problem structural priors to resolve representation ambiguity.

Two-level candidate pruning further compresses the search space. Level 1: The start point must have a non-trivial probability for the first character of the query at that point or its neighborhood (simulating the neighborhood using max pooling with a kernel of 3, i.e., morphological dilation); points below the threshold are discarded; the threshold is set quite low (0.1 in the text, 0.05 by default after ablation), with the reason being robustness to partial character misclassification. Level 2 "centering check": use the integral image to calculate the count within a reduced-height sub-window at the center of the initial detection box; if the ratio of the sub-window count to the total box count is below the threshold ratio, it indicates that the information in the box is not "centered characters" but possibly a拼 of character fragments across lines, and the point is discarded. Ablation data (Table 1) shows that pruning affects speed by orders of magnitude: relaxing the probability threshold from 0.05 to 0.001 increases the box retrieval time per (image, query) pair from 0.433 to 1.243 (column r=0.5), indicating that the vast majority of candidate points are indeed cut off by the first character probability rule alone.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Two-Stage Scoring and Two-Way CTC Re-scoring with Box Correction

The detection algorithm is completely character-agnostic except for the first character of the query; the scoring stage must align candidate boxes with the real query. The first stage scoring is Pyramid Counting (PCount): the query string is evenly divided into several segments, character counting is calculated for each segment, repeated $l$ layers, resulting in a descriptor of dimension $l(l+1)C/2$; characters across segments are distributed to their belonging multiple segments using Gaussian weighting. For the predicted pyramid counting on the detection box side, sub-region sums of horizontal segments within the box are also calculated efficiently using integral images on $F_s$, and matching uses cosine similarity. The paper also implements a PHOC version as a control—thresholding the counting histogram into a binary attribute where each bin does not exceed 1—this control directly tests whether "continuous counting is superior to binary attributes."

The second stage scoring uses CTC re-scoring, which has higher computational cost, so the descriptor from the first stage is used for pre-filtering with a low IoU threshold (0.2) NMS, applying only to the top $K=30$ results per page. In implementation, vertical max pooling with a kernel of 3 is pre-calculated on the character probability map, and the probability sequence is extracted from the center y-value of the detection box, and then the CTC score is calculated on the detection box, similar in idea to forced alignment.

Here appears the most honest and instructive part of the paper: this intuitive scheme was initially under-performing, often worse than multi-layer pyramid counting. Error analysis revealed the main cause was box over-estimation—the box extended to neighboring words, and the neighboring words happened to contain characters also present in the query (Figure 5 right). The solution lies in a deep understanding of the CTC algorithm structure: CTC forward pass produces a score matrix $D \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}$, where $D[t,c]$ corresponds to the score assuming character $c$ exists at step $t$; the standard approach takes the score of the last character of the query (along with blank, omitted here for brevity) on the last step, while this paper changes to searching for the optimal score of the last character of the query across all steps—the meaning is that the entire query sequence must be recognized, and only redundant predictions are ignored. Coupled with the intentional over-estimation of the x-axis endpoint, this modification causes the neighboring word part to naturally fall outside the optimal alignment path; reversing both the probability sequence and the query characters and doing it again symmetrically corrects the start point, which is the "two-way" variant. Thus, re-scoring not only provides better scores but also incidentally corrects the bounding box itself. The authors also point out that if needed, re-scoring can be handed over to an independent large model for further performance gains, but this paper deliberately reuses the already trained network completely to avoid introducing the computational overhead of additional deep components—the principle of efficiency first runs through to the end.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: IoW Overlap Metric

As given by the formula in the previous section. The reason for replacing IoU is elaborated in the problem statement: words in documents are disjoint entities, and a reasonable definition of a good detection is "enclosing the target word and not intersecting any neighboring words," rather than "the box tightly fits the ground truth." IoU puts the detection box area in the denominator, systematically penalizing the loose box design of this method; IoW only penalizes the truly harmful part (the erroneous intersection area). The paper also reports a compromise control metric x-IoU: assuming the detection and ground truth have the same y-coordinates, calculating overlap only on the x-axis, and requiring the initial IoU to exceed 0.1—a metric closer to line-level retrieval semantics. Comparing the three metrics on the same performance curve (Figure 7) is experimental evidence for the assertion that "metric semantic mismatch is the main cause of performance degradation."

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Vs. PHOC-based segmentation methods**: Segmentation-free vs. dependency on pre-segmentation; continuous counting vs. binary attributes. PHOC is essentially a thresholded degenerate version of PCount in this framework; the control in Table 2 shows that at a single layer, PCount 67.99 vs. PHOC 64.60, continuous information is indeed useful; but at multiple layers, PHOC surpasses (3 layers 73.45 vs. 72.65), and binary attributes are more stable under coarser spatial structures.
- **Vs. Ctrl-F-Net-like end-to-end detection methods**: Training requires only word-level annotations, no document-level box annotations or synthetic data; no predefined limit on the maximum number of detections; detection box sizes are unconstrained, naturally supporting sub-word and multi-word retrieval. Training only learns recognition, not detection, which directly explains robustness in small data settings (significant lead on GW5-15).
- **Vs. Sliding window methods**: Integral image plus binary search plus two-level pruning vs. window enumeration, reducing complexity from $O(N_r^3)$ to $O(N_r \log N_r)$ and further reducing constants.
- **Model Size**: The entire network has approximately 6M parameters (stated in both ablation and conclusion), far smaller than backbones like ResNet50 plus FPN; the paper does not report comparisons of parameter counts or computational costs with baseline methods in the same table.
- **Paradigm Difference in Box Philosophy**: Existing methods take "tight fit to ground truth" as the goal, while this paper takes "enclosing the target word and not touching neighboring words" as the goal, andaccompanies this with a modified evaluation metric, IoW. This is a self-consistent design starting from task semantics, rather than a simple engineering simplification.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

Two widely used English datasets. **IAM**: Consists of handwritten text from 657 writers, split into training, validation, and test sets in a writer-independent manner; its ground truth contains word-level annotations with errors, and these regions are masked in the page-level images. **GW (George Washington)**: George Washington manuscript collection, totaling 20 pages and 4860 words; the train-test split follows the protocol of existing work, adopting two settings of 4-fold cross-validation—15 pages training, 5 pages testing (GW15-5, data sufficient) and the more challenging 5 pages training, 15 pages testing (GW5-15, data scarce), reported values are all 4-fold averages. The model is trained only on available pages, with no pre-training.

Preprocessing follows the standard paradigm for both datasets: ignore punctuation, merge case; all words containing no non-alphanumeric characters are treated as queries; only on IAM, queries in the official stopword list are removed by convention. Implementation details: training was completed on a single NVidia 1080Ti using PyTorch; the retrieval algorithm acting on CNN feature maps was implemented using CPU-based Numba to ensure runtime efficiency; the authors stated they did not explore GPU implementation because operations like binary search cannot be directly ported using efficient PyTorch functions, but GPU-ification is expected to significantly improve time, listed as a potential extension.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The main metric is mAP (mean Average Precision), the standard metric for retrieval problems. Since the task is segmentation-free retrieval, it must be paired with an overlap metric between detection boxes and ground truth boxes to determine hits. The paper concurrently examines three: standard IoU; x-IoU (assuming detection and ground truth have the same y-coordinates, focusing on x-axis overlap, requiring initial IoU > 0.1, a line-level metric); and the proposed IoW. Reported thresholds follow literature conventions, taking 25% and 50%. Rationale for selection: the 25% low threshold is a conventional comparable setting for segmentation-free work; the 50% high threshold exposes differences in box quality; and the distortion of IoU at high thresholds (detailed below) is exactly the metric issue this paper aims to argue, so x-IoU and IoW are needed as diagnostic perspectives.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Comparison objects are segmentation-free SOTA (Table 3a): BoF-HMMs (ICDAR 2015), BG Index (ICDAR 2015, only IAM 50% has data 48.6), Word Hypothesis Method (ICDAR 2017, only GW15-5), Ctrl-F-Net DCToW and PHOC variants (ICCV 2017), ResNet50+FPN (ACCV 2020, only GW15-5).

**GW15-5 (Data Sufficient)**: This paper (IoU) 25%/50% is 91.6/66.4, trailing behind ResNet50+FPN's 96.5/94.1 and Ctrl-F-Net DCToW's 95.2/91.0. **GW5-15 (Data Scarce)**: This paper 85.9/66.3, significantly surpassing Ctrl-F-Net DCToW's 76.8/73.8 (leading by 9.1 points at 25% threshold) and BoF-HMMs' 58.1/54.6. **IAM**: This paper 85.8/59.2, exceeding Ctrl-F-Net DCToW's 82.5/80.3 (leading by 3.3 points) and PHOC variant's 80.8/78.8 at 25% threshold; significantly trailing at 50% threshold.

The paper offers three observations, each with clear attribution: First, at 25% IoU, this paper substantially exceeds all comparison methods (including complex end-to-end architectures) on the two difficult settings of GW5-15 and IAM, and is robust with small training amounts, because this paper does not learn detection and recognition tasks simultaneously, relying only on recognition—parameters are spent on the discriminative capabilities truly needed for retrieval. Second, performance drops at the 50% threshold, but switching to x-IoU and IoW reveals the method's effectiveness at larger overlap thresholds (Table 3b); the paper admits these numbers are not directly comparable to baselines relying on IoU. Specific numbers in Table 3b: This paper's x-IoU on GW15-5, GW5-15, IAM is 93.2/92.9, 86.8/86.7, 86.9/86.8 (25%/50%), and IoW is 92.7/87.6, 86.8/83.0, 86.9/86.3—almost no decay at the 50% threshold, forming an extreme contrast with 66.4/66.3/59.2 under IoU, strongly proving that high-threshold decay mainly comes from the metric's penalty on loose boxes rather than insufficient retrieval capability. Third, the reason for not beating SOTA on GW15-5 was located through error analysis: proposal boxes also expanded on the y-axis, leading to lower overlap scores—this paper's "coarse localization" characteristic becomes a shortcoming in settings with sufficient data and high-quality opponent boxes.

Visual results (Figure 8, Figure 9) supplement qualitative evidence: The top six boxes in GW retrieval are not tight and can be extended; a typical false detection occurs when querying "them"—"the" followed by a word starting with m is拼 into one hit, which is empirical evidence of residual counting ambiguity. Figure 9 demonstrates capabilities that most methods lack: multi-word query "he came" and sub-word query "fast" (hitting the suffix of breakfast, and simultaneously hitting handwritten body text and printed reference text of different scales at the top of the page).

### Findings from Ablation Studies

All ablations were performed on the IAM validation set using a network of approximately 6M parameters, with default IoU threshold.

**Pruning Threshold Sensitivity (Table 1)**: Grid search was performed on probability threshold $p_{thres}$ and centering ratio threshold $r_{thres}$, using only cosine similarity of character counting histograms for scoring (thus MAP absolute values are lower). The most striking structure in the table is the cliff-like drop of $r_{thres}$: $r=0.5$ is a clear sweet spot (best in each row is in this column, e.g., 67.99 when $p=0.05$), MAP drops from 67.99 to 52.02 when $r$ rises to 0.66, and further drops to 31.17 at $r=0.75$—the centering check is too loose, allowing a large number of pseudo-boxes拼 across lines to survive. Regarding $p_{thres}$, performance is almost identical for 0.01 and 0.05 (67.97 vs. 67.99), and 0.05 was finally selected because it brings substantially faster retrieval (time in the same column 0.433 vs. 0.628); while the lower 0.001 has acceptable performance (66.15), time deteriorates to 1.243. The bracketed times in Table 1 do not specify units, but the note in Table 2 declares them as milliseconds, and the values match the same configuration (0.433 vs. 0.44), allowing the inference that they are also milliseconds per (image, query) pair.

**Impact of Scoring Methods (Table 2)**: Rows are layers 1 to 5, columns are three configurations: PHOC/PCount cross without CTC, One-way CTC, Two-way CTC. Four core findings. First, adding CTC re-scoring brings the largest improvement: 1-layer PCount rises from 67.99 without CTC to 85.58 one-way and 88.98 two-way—the reinforcement of recognition-level scoring to counting-level representation is nearly 21 points, directly quantifying the cost of "counting ambiguity" and the value of CTC. Second, two-way is consistently better than one-way by about 3.4 points (88.98 vs. 85.58), indicating start point correction is independently effective. Third, after using CTC, the number of layers in the first step no longer brings benefits (1-layer two-way 88.98 is the best in the entire table, 5 layers drops to 87.61); the paper attributes this to box expansion being a common error, and segmenting expanded boxes does not correspond to true intra-word segmentation—the benefits of the pyramid are eaten up by box errors. Fourth, pure counting scoring (no CTC column) indeed rises first and then falls with layers, PHOC reaches the best no-CTC score of 73.45 at 3 layers, surpassing PCount's 72.65 at this point. In terms of time, from 0.44 for 1-layer no-CTC to 0.81 for 1-layer two-way CTC, almost doubling but still in the millisecond range, performance priority leads to selecting 1-layer PCount plus two-way CTC as the default configuration for the entire paper.

**Overlap Metric Comparison (Figure 7)**: The optimal system (88.98, 25% IoU) drops sharply as the IoU threshold increases, leaving only 54.68 at 50% (validation set; test set Table 3 is 59.2, settings differ); x-IoU and IoW maintain almost the same level until the 60% threshold. IoW performs robustly throughout, proving that the main source of performance drop at large thresholds is the overly strict definition of "what constitutes a good detection." Another quantitative number from the limitations section: if sub-word hits are counted as correct, MAP (25% IoU) rises from 88.98 to 89.62—the ambiguity of hit semantics caused by missing spaces is worth only 0.64 points, but it determines the usability of retrieval results.

## Main Contributions

- **A computationally efficient segmentation-free KWS complete system**: The scanning scheme of integral image summation plus binary search plus two-level pruning compresses naive $O(N_r^3)$ to engineering-usable millisecond-level per query (Table 1, Table 2 time columns).
- **A training scheme relying only on word-level annotations**: Training of character counting maps and counting models does not require document-level annotations, thus eliminating the need to generate synthetic data to cover word position variations—this is a direct rebuttal to the training costs of object detection-style routes.
- **Probability-Scale Decoupled Counting Representation**: Per-pixel character probability multiplied by implicitly learned scale map, summed within the box to obtain the counting histogram (Eq. 1), the representation form is naturally coupled with integral image acceleration, and supports full-page inference and boxes of arbitrary size.
- **Two-Way CTC Re-scoring with Box Correction**: The design of taking the optimal score of the last character of the query across all steps, plus endpoint over-estimation, plus reverse traversal, turns the "scoring module" into a "box correction module" simultaneously, solving neighboring word expansion errors (Figure 5 right).
- **IoW Overlap Metric**: Proposed and empirically validated an evaluation metric more aligned with word retrieval semantics than IoU, using Figure 7 and Table 3b to prove that metric mismatch systematically distorts method rankings.
- **Counter-example value of achieving SOTA with a simple model**: A pipeline of approximately 6M parameters, pure convolution, no detection head, surpasses complex end-to-end methods on GW5-15 and IAM at 25% IoU (Table 3a), arguing that "detection head plus predefined detection count" is not a necessary condition for segmentation-free KWS.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Box over-estimation is an inherent defect of the proposal stage**: The paper admits in the limitations section that the box proposal in Section 2.2 tends to over-estimate actual boxes; CTC re-scoring can sufficiently mitigate this, but incorrect boxes may push correct regions out of the top K before entering re-scoring—in other words, the scoring remedy acts after pruning, leaving blind spots for remedy.
- **Y-axis expansion directly leads to defeat on GW15-5**: Error analysis shows that proposal boxes on GW also expand in the y-direction, lowering overlap scores; this is the cost of the "coarse localization" design philosophy under high standards of sufficient data and tight opponent boxes (the main reason for the gap between 66.4 and 94.1 at 50% IoU in Table 3a).
- **Space characters are not modeled**: The method cannot learn to distinguish word separators; sub-word detection is both a feature and a problem—users cannot control "when sub-word hits are allowed." Quantitative impact: MAP rises from 88.98 to 89.62 (25% IoU) when sub-word hits are counted as correct.
- **Permutation ambiguity of counting representation cannot be resolved in the first step**: Errors like "and" vs. "dan" (Figure 5 left), "the+m" vs. "them" (Figure 8) can only be remedied by the second step CTC; the upper limit of first-step scoring is locked at the information content of bag-of-characters.
- **Horizontal writing prior limits generalization**: The decoder's 1×5 convolution explicitly assumes horizontal writing; vertically written, multi-directional, or mixed-layout documents are not covered by this method, and the paper does not report any experiments on non-English or non-horizontal writing.
- **Scale map lacks direct supervision**: The semantics of $S$ emerge entirely from the combination of losses, with weak controllability and interpretability, also meaning counting errors are difficult to attribute and fix directionally.
- **QbE queries not experimented**: The paper claims extending the method to QbE is straightforward (estimating character occurrences for example images as targets), but no experimental numbers are provided, and the paper does not report them.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Loss weight 10 is empirically set**: The authors explicitly state that the weights of $L_{CTC} + 10 \cdot L_{count}$ were not explored; the optimal ratio between counting and recognition losses is an open problem.
- **Absence of architecture search**: The authors admit they did not deeply explore the architecture of each component (not the core goal of the paper), and the capacity configurations of the backbone, decoder, and scaler may not be optimal.
- **Limited coverage of ablation**: Ablations were only performed on the IAM validation set, on a single network of approximately 6M parameters; key hyperparameters of CTC re-scoring (K=30, NMS IoU 0.2, vertical pooling kernel 3) did not have separate sensitivity experiments, which the paper does not report.
- **Efficiency comparison lacks same-table baselines**: Time is only reported as values per (image, query) pair for itself (Table 1, Table 2), without comparing runtime, parameter count, or computational cost with baselines like Ctrl-F-Net on the same hardware; the claim of "simple and efficient" lacks external quantitative anchors.
- **Comparison of x-IoU/IoW is not entirely fair**: The paper itself admits that numbers under the new metric are not directly comparable to baselines relying on IoU; the value argument for the new metric relies on qualitative attribution shown in "error analysis" plus curve shapes (Figure 7), without quantitative validation using a human-annotated "good detection" gold standard.
- **Narrow dataset scope**: Only two English datasets; the handling of masked erroneous annotations in IAM and the small scale of GW (only 20 pages) limit the extrapolation of conclusions; results on larger-scale libraries commonly used in historical literature retrieval are not reported by the paper.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Tighter box prediction**: The paper proposes designing specific model components to assist in tight box prediction, mitigating the blind spot where "correct regions are discarded before re-scoring."
- **Training with space characters included in the character vocabulary**: Allows users to optionally allow sub-word hits, balancing sub-word retrieval capability with the certainty of hit semantics.
- **GPU-ification of retrieval algorithm**: Operations like binary search are difficult to map to efficient PyTorch operators, which is the reason for the current CPU implementation; GPU implementation is expected to significantly compress time overhead.
- **Independent large model re-scoring**: The authors point out that the re-scoring step can be handed over to an independent larger model for further performance gains; this paper did not adopt it to adhere to efficiency principles, and the performance ceiling has not been explored.
- **Writing direction generalization**: Extending the 1×5 horizontal prior to vertical or multi-directional writing is a necessary path towards multi-language and multi-layout documents.
- **(Note perspective, for speech readers of this repository)**: There are two transferable inspirations from this paper for speech KWS: First, "counting-based relaxed objective"—replacing frame-by-frame alignment with an objective robust to temporal misalignment, such as character counting, which corresponds to the "phoneme counting/bag-of-features" route in speech, worth comparing with CTC strictly aligned routes in ablation studies; Second, the two-stage retrieval structure of "coarse localization first, then weighted scoring" (integral image level coarse screening plus CTC level fine ranking), which is isomorphic to the two-stage scoring system design in speech retrieval, and its "scorer also acts as boundary correction" two-way modification idea may have counterparts in speech endpoint detection.
