## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The task of Keyword Spotting (KWS) is to detect all occurrences of given keywords in an audio stream, operating in either streaming or non-streaming modes. Such systems are almost always deployed on user-end devices, inherently bearing three shackles: low latency, small memory footprint, and limited computational power. The paper places this problem in a broader context: machine learning is expanding in two opposite directions. On one end are large language models with hundreds of billions of parameters, requiring immense computing power for inference; on the other end are micro-models designed for specific tasks, deployed on devices with limited computing power such as smartphones or home appliances. KWS stands at the forefront of the latter direction.

Traditional KWS models are modeled only for a fixed number of keywords (e.g., wake words in contemporary voice assistants like "OK Google", "Alexa", "Hey Siri", "Hi Bixby"). These closed-set models can be compressed to be very small, even under 100 kB, because the output layer is simply a fixed-size classification head, and the keyword set is frozen during training. However, real user needs involve custom keywords: users want to change their wake words at will. This transforms the problem into open-vocabulary KWS—the model must support the recognition of potentially arbitrary keywords. Once the vocabulary is opened, the model capacity requirements change completely: it can no longer be a classifier but must possess the ability to transcribe any speech segment into a sequence of symbols, which is the core capability of Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR). ASR acoustic models typically have hundreds of millions of trainable parameters, which directly conflicts with the capacity budget for terminal deployment. The engineering question this paper addresses is: Can an ASR-based KWS system designed for open vocabulary be compressed into a smartphone and provide usable detection rates on public test sets?

It is worth noting that the paper explicitly limits the scope to non-streaming mode: audio is provided in full segments for decision-making, rather than streaming out detection events frame by frame. This limitation affects subsequent architecture choices and evaluation methods, and is a premise that is easy to overlook when reading this paper.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

In the related work section, the paper divides open-vocabulary KWS into two families: query-by-text (QbyT) and query-by-example (QbyE), and points out the shortcomings of existing solutions under the dual constraints of "terminal deployment + open vocabulary."

First, benchmark and task misalignment. The most popular test set currently is a subset of Google Speech Commands (GSC), but GSC contains only a small number of keywords, making it more suitable for classification problems with a fixed number of classes rather than open-vocabulary tasks. This misalignment is critical: models that perform well on GSC may not handle arbitrary custom words. The paper will prove this with its own experiments later.

Second, scalability defects of fixed output layer schemes. Many QbyT schemes (including attention models like Keyword Transformer) adopt a fixed-size output layer. Supporting new keywords means retraining the model. For terminal devices, retraining the model every time a wake word is changed is unacceptable, whether from the perspective of cloud computing power or user wait time.

Third, deployment difficulties of online adaptation schemes. Another route is model adaptation at the initialization stage: pre-training an embedding model on a corpus with a large number of classes, then adding a classification layer for specific keywords on top, and adapting with a few samples (similar to few-shot transfer learning). While theoretically elegant, the paper points out incisively: KWS systems are usually deployed on resource-constrained devices, and executing any form of model adaptation (even if only the last layer is modified) is cumbersome in engineering—adaptation implies the device must have the ability to train or update weights, involving a series of issues such as memory, numerical precision, and version management.

Fourth, user burden of QbyE schemes. Registration audio-based schemes require users to provide one or more "Enrollment" recordings at the initialization stage, meaning users must speak the keywords themselves. This introduces additional interaction costs, and the quality of the registration recordings directly determines the detection rate. Some QbyE schemes (such as DONUT, and end-side QbyE based on CTC) build keyword models at the phoneme level (FST graphs or N-best phoneme sequences with log probabilities). The paper points out that these methods essentially convert QbyE into QbyT, and their ideas can be借鉴 (borrowed).

In summary, the shortcomings of existing schemes can be归结 (summarized) in one sentence: either the model capacity conflicts with the terminal budget (complete ASR models are too large), or the openness is insufficient (fixed output layer), or the burden is shifted to the user or device (registration recordings, on-device adaptation). The paper's positioning is to find an engineeringly feasible narrow path among these constraints.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

The paper aims to simultaneously satisfy four mutually constraining requirements: (1) Open vocabulary—the model cannot rely on a fixed softmax classification head and must be able to handle any word unseen during training (including unconventional phrases such as named entities); (2) Small footprint—the entire system (model plus engine) must fit into smartphone-level devices, with the final number given by the paper being 3.2 MB for the model (Abstract and Section III-A, after 8-bit quantization); (3) Low latency—any improvement must not significantly slow down inference, with the Real-Time Factor (RTF) measured at 0.05 on contemporary smartphones (Abstract); (4) Usable detection rate—the True Positive Rate (TPR) reaches 73%–86% and the False Positive Rate (FPR) is below 24% on the public test set MOCKS (Abstract). Among these four requirements, (1) and (2) are naturally conflicting: open vocabulary requires large capacity, while edge-side requires small models; (3) and (4) also conflict: any means to improve detection rate (such as stronger language models, more keyword variants) usually increases computation or increases false alarms. All technical contributions of this paper—knowledge distillation, static language model weighting, TTS generation of multiple keyword variants—are about balancing on the knife's edge between these two pairs of conflicts.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The entire system (Fig. 1 Baseline, Fig. 2 Static LM version, Fig. 3 Multi-keyword version) is an "ASR-like" pipeline: audio processing (acoustic model generates frame-level scores) → beam search (beam search generates the optimal path) → converting ID sequences to subword hypotheses using the model vocabulary → keyword classifier compares the hypothesis with the given keyword, outputting a binary 0/1 decision.

The fundamental motivation for choosing this pipeline is stated at the beginning of Section III: the main hypothesis of KWS is that keywords can be any phrase, likely unseen during model training, so the model architecture cannot be based on a classifier with a fixed-size softmax output layer. ASR acoustic models naturally output symbol sequences, and vocabulary openness is determined by the output symbol set, independent of the number of classes. The cost is that ASR models usually have hundreds of millions of parameters for high accuracy, so the paper's compression strategy is "first train a large teacher model on the ASR task, then use knowledge distillation to transfer the capability into a student model, and finally perform post-training 8-bit quantization." Why choose distillation instead of directly training a small model? The paper's logic is: the KWS task itself is simpler than complete speech recognition, but requires a considerable vocabulary capability. Distillation allows the small model to retain the teacher model's behavior distribution for handling large or open vocabularies while reducing the number of parameters to about one-sixteenth—something difficult to stably obtain by directly training a small model from scratch.

The differences among the three versions focus on the initialization stage: the baseline version (Fig. 1) has no initialization and infers directly; the static LM version (Fig. 2) runs the BPE algorithm on keywords during initialization to construct LM vectors and set boost weights, and uses the LM to re-score acoustic scores during inference; the multi-keyword version (Fig. 3) synthesizes keyword speech using TTS during initialization, passes it through the AM with beam search to generate additional hypotheses, concatenates the original keywords into the hypothesis list, removes duplicates to form a "keyword list," and uses a multi-keyword classifier (Algorithm 2) for decision-making during inference. The key design principle is: all preparations related to new keywords are done during the initialization stage and only once, and the computation added to the inference path is deliberately compressed to nearly zero—this is the structural guarantee for the promise of "no added latency."

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Acoustic Model: MoChA Encoder-Decoder**. The AM is based on the monotonic chunkwise attention (MoChA) architecture (paper citation [23], Chiu & Raffel 2017), which is an encoder-decoder seq2seq model, with both parts composed of recurrent neural network layers. The encoder computes embedding vectors for each frame of the audio stream; the attention-based decoder combines and transforms these embeddings into subword sequences constituting the hypothesis. MoChA is a modification of soft attention, designed to solve real-time (online) processing problems. It consists of two attention layers: the first layer uses hard monotonic attention for each frame to determine whether to run the second layer; the second layer runs soft attention on a small chunk of frame embeddings (a "chunk," containing the current frame and several previous frames) to compute the context vector, where the chunk length is a model hyperparameter. Why this structure is important for this paper: ordinary soft attention requires global normalization over the entire history, making it non-streaming; hard monotonic attention ensures that the decoding position advances monotonically over time, while soft attention within the chunk retains local fine-grained alignment capabilities. This mechanism allows the same model to be compatible with online processing in principle and lays the foundation for latency control. The paper describes the algorithm in text and pseudocode, without providing attention formulas.

**Input Features and Output Symbols**. Both models take 40-dimensional power Mel-spectrograms as input, with an analysis window of 25 ms and a step size of 10 ms. Cepstral Mean and Variance Normalization (CMVN) is performed during both training and inference, with normalization statistics computed over all training samples (Section III-A). The output consists of 500 subwords, generated using BPE (byte pair encoding) adaptive word segmentation method (citation [26]), with the vocabulary generated from all transcriptions in the training and test sets. Why choose 500: this size keeps the output layer small while allowing more accurate recognition of rare words or out-of-vocabulary words—a vocabulary that is too large causes the output layer to expand, while one that is too small chops rare words into indistinguishable sequences. Additionally, 4 special subwords are added: `<s>` (sentence start), `</s>` (sentence end), `<unk>` (non-speech events or characters other than Latin letters), and `<blank>` (required for CTC training).

**Training Objective**. The teacher model is trained jointly using CTC loss and categorical cross-entropy (joint CTC/attention multi-task scheme from citation [25]): CTC acts on the encoder output, encouraging the model to learn monotonic alignment; the output softmax distribution uses 0.1 label smoothing. The total loss for the student model is a weighted sum of distillation loss and cross-entropy loss, with weights of 0.4 and 0.6 respectively, and the distillation temperature is set to 2 (making the teacher's predictions "softer" and carrying inter-class relative relationship information). Why joint CTC: pure attention decoding alignment can be arbitrarily unordered; the monotonicity prior of CTC is consistent with the temporal structure of speech, allowing the encoder representation to be more conducive to alignment, which is also the prerequisite for beam search to produce reasonable paths.

**Language Model Re-scoring**. The LM uses a minimalist 1-gram: the model is simply a vector of the same length as the AM output layer. The mathematical operation of re-scoring is the element-wise multiplication of AM scores and the LM vector (Section III-B). Let the subword score vector output by the AM at the decoding step be $s \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|}$ ($|V|=500$), and the LM vector be $w \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|}$, then the re-scored score is $s'_i = s_i \cdot w_i$. The static LM initializes the components of $w$ to one of two values: subwords contained in the keyword take the boost weight (hyperparameter), and other subwords take 1. The three boundary cases are clearly stated in the paper: when boost=1, no subword scores change, degenerating into the baseline (only AM scores participate in beam search); when boost<1, the probability of recognizing the keyword is reduced (reverse operation); when boost is too large, beam search is dominated by keyword subwords, pulling negative samples into keywords, causing false alarms to skyrocket. The ingenuity of this design lies in compressing "prior injection" into a single element-wise multiplication—no additional decoding graph, no FST, no neural network LM, only one 500-dimensional vector added to memory, with negligible latency increment.

**Keyword Classifier**. The logic of Algorithm 1: input keyword `keyword`, AM hypothesis `hyp`, recognition threshold `t`. First, take the word count `l` of the keyword, enumerate all continuous subsequences `sub` of length `l` (by word count) in `hyp`, and calculate the character-level normalized Levenshtein distance `dist(keyword, s)` for each subsequence; if there exists a subsequence satisfying `dist ≤ t`, return true (detected), otherwise false. Why enumerate subsequences by word length instead of comparing the entire hypothesis: the recording may contain more speech than just the keyword, and comparing the entire hypothesis would misjudge "longer utterances containing the keyword" as negative samples. Why use character-level normalized Levenshtein distance instead of exact matching: AM hypotheses inevitably have small recognition errors (one subword mistaken for an adjacent subword), and edit distance allows for a certain number of character deviations; normalization (dividing by string length) makes the threshold `t` comparable across keywords of different lengths. The specific formula for normalization is not given in the paper (whether divided by keyword length or the longer of the two, the paper does not report), defined in pseudocode form.

**Multi-keyword Classifier**. Algorithm 2 is an extension of Algorithm 1: the outer loop iterates through the keyword list `keywords` (original keyword + TTS-generated variants), and the inner loop similarly enumerates equal-word-length subsequences of the hypothesis. If the distance for any (keyword, subsequence) pair is less than the threshold, it is detected. Mathematically, this generalizes the decision from $\exists s: d(k, s) \le t$ to $\exists k \in K, \exists s: d(k, s) \le t$, where $K$ is the extended keyword set.

**TTS Synthesizer**. The TTS used to generate keyword speech is an internal end-to-end system: neural AM plus vocoder. The AM maps phoneme label sequences to acoustic features, and the vocoder converts features into audio. The phoneme label set contains English phoneme symbols, word separators, and sentence-end markers (these markers are stripped when synthesizing keywords); acoustic features are taken from F0 (interpolated in voiced regions), Mel-spectrograms, and aperiodicity, consistent with the WORLD vocoder; the vocoder architecture is based on LPCNet (citation [35]), the AM is similar to Tacotron 2 (citation [36], implemented according to the streaming scheme in citation [37]) and uses Mutual Information Loss MILoss (citation [38]). The entire system is trained on LJ Speech and Hi-Fi multi-speaker English TTS datasets; the vocoder is trained separately for each timbre, and the AM is first trained for 10,000 epochs on the entire training data, then for 450,000 epochs on the data for each timbre (Section IV-A). Why TTS is the correct solution to this problem: registration recording schemes require users to speak, while TTS schemes fully automate the "obtaining keyword pronunciation samples" process, with users only inputting text.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Small-Footprint Route of Knowledge Distillation + Post-Training Quantization

Teacher Model: The encoder is a 6-layer BiLSTM, with 512 units per direction and 0.3 dropout; max-pooling is applied after the first three BiLSTM layers to compress the time dimension; the decoder contains 1 layer of 1000-unit unidirectional LSTM, 621-dimensional embedding layer, 1000-dimensional readout layer, and two attention layers of 512 each; the chunk size for intra-chunk attention is 2. Total of 50.1 million trainable parameters (encoder 34.2 million, decoder 15.9 million, Section III-A). Student Model architecture is isomorphic but comprehensively scaled down: each layer BiLSTM has 124 units per direction and 0.3 dropout; decoder LSTM has 256 units, 156-dimensional embedding, 256-dimensional readout, and two attention layers of 124 each; total of 3.1 million parameters (encoder 2.0 million, decoder 1.1 million). Parameter compression is approximately 16 times.

The "why" behind the training details is quite chewy. The teacher is trained for 23 epochs, with an initial learning rate of 1e-4, decaying by 0.95 for every 10 consecutive validations without improvement, and validating every 5000 steps. During training, random Room Impulse Responses (RIR, simulating distances of 1–5 meters, reverberation times of 0.2–0.9 s) are added, and random noise (internal noise library, similar to MUSAN, plus AudioSet) is mixed in at SNRs of -2 to 12 dB, followed by SpecAugment masking a frequency block of size 8 and a time block of size 50. This set of augmentations is buying insurance for real acoustic environments on the edge side (far-field, reverberation, noise). During student model training, the paper observed that SpecAugment actually led to accuracy degradation, so this augmentation was skipped—an anomalous detail worth noting: the distillation target (teacher soft labels) already carries strong regularization information, and adding aggressive input masking causes a mismatch between the student's fitting target and the visible input. The student is trained for 22 epochs, with an initial learning rate of 4e-4, and the learning rate schedule is consistent with the teacher. Training uses greedy decoding, while inference uses beam search with a beam width of 4—the inconsistency between training and decoding here is an intentional engineering trade-off (beam search training is costly), with the cost absorbed by the post-processing layer.

The final step is post-training 8-bit quantization, compressing the model from 13 MB to 3.2 MB (Section III-A), with WER of 16.0 on LibriSpeech test-clean and 30.9 on test-other after quantization. Post-training quantization was chosen over Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) likely because the process is simple and requires no retraining; from the WER perspective, the quantization damage is controlled within an acceptable range (the WER for full precision is not reported by the paper).

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Static 1-gram Language Model and Boost Re-scoring

As mentioned earlier, the static LM is initialized with only two values: 1 and the boost weight. This "simple to the point of crudeness" design is the core experimental object of the paper. Its value proposition has three aspects: (1) extremely small memory footprint (a vector of output layer length); (2) extremely small latency increment (one element-wise multiplication); (3) initialization for each new keyword is done only once, not affecting inference latency (Section III-B).

The underlying principle can be understood as follows: beam search extends paths at each step based on cumulative scores. AM scores reflect acoustic evidence, but the AM is trained on general ASR corpora and has no prior for "the word the user cares about right now." The static LM is equivalent to multiplying the transition scores of keyword subwords by a constant gain during the decoding process, pushing the search towards paths containing keyword subwords. The larger the boost, the stronger the push: when moderate, it corrects recognition errors such as "pronunciation similar but not the keyword," pulling up the TPR; when too large, the search is completely hijacked by the prior, forcing negative samples to be decoded as keywords, causing FPR to spiral out of control. Therefore, boost is a typical prior-evidence trade-off knob. The paper scanned $\{2^n : n \in \{0,\dots,11\}\}$ (1 to 2048), totaling 12 values, using the EER system to find the sweet spot.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: TTS-Driven Multi-keyword Classifier (Registration-free Pronunciation Variant Expansion)

The core insight of the multi-keyword classifier (Section III-D): for keywords that are never seen (or extremely rare) during training—typical of unconventional phrases such as named entities—the AM is likely to return incorrect hypotheses. As long as these errors are similar across different samples (systematic), adding these incorrect hypotheses as pronunciation variants to the classifier can improve TPR. Essentially, this is equivalent to automatically supplementing a set of "variant spellings in the AM's eyes" for the keywords input by the user.

The cost analysis is clearly written in the paper: the impact of the multi-keyword scheme on memory and latency grows linearly with the number of TTS recordings, but each TTS recording adds at most one string, so the memory footprint is negligible; in terms of latency, the calculation of Levenshtein distance is much faster than ASR decoding, so the impact on inference latency is also negligible. Moreover, the construction of the keyword list occurs during the initialization stage and does not touch the inference path. The risk side is also disclosed: phrases that are similar in pronunciation but different from the keyword may be recognized as the same incorrect hypothesis, thereby increasing FPR—this risk is proven to be real in the experimental section and grows with the number of variants.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to QbyT schemes with fixed output layers (such as Keyword Transformer): this paper's model does not require any retraining when changing keywords, only one initialization (BPE tokenization + LM vector construction + optional TTS synthesis), which is an essential difference in deployment form. Compared to on-device adaptation/few-shot schemes (citations [15][16]): this paper completely does not update any weights on the device; "adaptation" is moved to the score space (LM re-scoring) and string space (keyword variant list), completely avoiding the engineering complexity of on-device training. Compared to QbyE schemes (citations [17][18]): no user registration recordings are required; registration data is automatically generated by TTS; in this sense, this paper is a pure QbyT scheme, only borrowing the idea of "expanding keyword representation with audio." Compared to LSTM-CTC schemes on the ASR route (citations [9][10]): those methods directly compare using minimum edit distance at the character or phoneme level, while this paper produces hypotheses at the subword level and calculates normalized edit distance at the character level, additionally stacking the explicit prior injection method of LM re-scoring; compared to citation [13] (streaming seq2seq KWS from ASRU 2017, with keyword encoder, attention guidance, and a phoneme-level n-gram LM), this paper simplifies the LM to a 1-gram two-value vector, replacing complex n-gram probabilities with a single adjustable boost weight, making "prior injection" an almost zero-cost operation—this is the most distinct technical personality of this paper.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

**AM Training Data** (Section III-A): LibriSpeech all training splits (960 hours) + 1779 hours of English Mozilla Common Voice 7.0 (sentences selected for testing are removed) + 4 hours of audio without speech (silence or light noise), totaling approximately 2743 hours, sampled at 16 kHz. Noise augmentation uses an internal noise library (similar to MUSAN) plus AudioSet; RIR is simulated (distance 1–5 meters, reverberation 0.2–0.9 s).

**Evaluation Data** (Section IV-A): The main test set is MOCKS 1.0 (Multilingual Open Custom Keyword Spotting testset, the authors' previous work, Interspeech 2023 in press, citation [32]), supplemented by GSC v2. Since the AM is trained only on English data, the evaluation takes three subsets of MOCKS: en_LS_clean, en_LS_other, and en_MCV. Each test case in MOCKS consists of two audio clips and one keyword: one serves as data for the initialization (registration) stage, and the other serves as data for the inference stage (called test audio); the static LM scheme skips the initialization recording (the goal is to limit user-device interaction), and the multi-keyword classifier scheme replaces the initialization recording with synthetic data. Each subset is divided into three parts: pos (test audio contains the target keyword), sim (test audio is a different phrase with similar pronunciation to the keyword), and dif (different phrase with large pronunciation distance). This three-part division is MOCKS' most valuable design for this paper's problem—it splits "false positives" into "difficult false alarms caused by similar pronunciation" and "easy false alarms from completely unrelated speech," making the structure of FPR observable.

**TTS Training Data** (Section IV-A): LJ Speech and Hi-Fi multi-speaker English TTS datasets. 10 recordings are synthesized for each keyword (all MOCKS and GSC); two types of multi-keyword experiments are initialized with 2 recordings (one male and one female timbre) and 10 recordings respectively; half of the experiments mix the synthetic audio with background noise and convolve RIR (noise and RIR types are consistent with AM training), and the other half use clean audio.

**Statistical Methods**: Confidence intervals use bootstrap resampling, with 200 resamples with replacement for each test set, keeping the training set and model fixed, and taking the [2.5, 97.5] percentile boundaries for the 95% confidence interval (Section IV-A).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The main metric is recognition accuracy, specifically presented as the acceptance rate curve varying with the keyword classifier threshold `t`, reported on the pos/sim/dif subsets respectively (Fig. 4–6). Rationale: the core trade-off of KWS is the TPR vs. FPR curve; the acceptance rate on pos is TPR, and the acceptance rates on sim and dif together characterize the composition of FPR. EER (equal error rate) is used to select the optimal boost: on each test set, FPR is obtained by summing sim and dif (Section IV-B1); the paper specifically notes that when boost is too large, FPR is always greater than FNR, and the two curves do not intersect; in this case, the larger of the two is taken as the EER (text related to Fig. 8)—this is an honest engineering treatment, but it also means that the EER figures in the large boost region are no longer equal error rates in the classic sense. On GSC, the community convention of accuracy (citation [33]) is followed, where negative samples should be recognized as the special classes `_unknown` or `_silence`; however, GSC evaluation is essentially a 12-class classification problem, which does not match the open-vocabulary setting of this paper. The paper's remedy is: for each positive keyword, build an LM and an extended keyword table, process the hypothesis to calculate the character-level Levenshtein distance with each positive keyword, take the minimum distance, and if it is less than or equal to the threshold, count it as a false positive, otherwise count it as a true negative (Section IV-A). Model-side metrics: WER (LibriSpeech test-clean/other), model size, RTF (measured at 0.05 on smartphone, Abstract). Absolute milliseconds for memory and latency are not reported by the paper.

The static LM cuts the EER of en_LS_clean from 30.05 to 15.18 (boost=32), close to halving it; en_MCV from 27.30 to 14.22; en_LS_other is the most difficult (heavier noise/accent), with the optimum at boost=64 of 20.34. The position where the minimum EER appears drifts with the test set (32 or 64), indicating that boost has no universal optimal value—the paper explicitly acknowledges this in the conclusion.

**Table III (MOCKS Acceptance Rate, %)** gives the most complete picture, excerpting key rows: under the boost 1 baseline, en_LS_clean pos/sim/dif = 48.39/1.28/0.00, en_LS_other = 28.56/1.12/0.00, en_MCV = 50.29/1.34/0.00—TPR is only 30-50%, far from usable. When boost 32 is used alone, it jumps to 84.15/22.01/4.79 (en_LS_clean), 73.05/21.10/4.96 (en_LS_other), 86.40/24.16/5.40 (en_MCV). This is the source of the abstract's "TPR 73%–86%, FPR below 24%" (sim column 21.10–24.16). The cost is equally striking: sim false alarms increase from about 1.3% to 22%–24%, trading a twenty-fold increase in false alarms for a doubling of TPR—this is the complete bill for prior injection.

**Comparison on GSC (Fig. 9, Table II, accuracy %)**: Baseline 84.60 → boost 32 reaches 95.97, and accuracy drops rapidly after higher boosts (large boost favors keyword subwords in beam search, causing negative samples to be recognized as keywords). After adding the multi-keyword classifier: boost 1 + multi 2×TTS = 85.13, boost 1 + multi 2×noisy TTS = 86.09, boost 32 + multi 2×TTS = 96.07, boost 32 + multi 2×noisy TTS = 96.75. Compared to the current leading architecture on GSC of 98.37% (citation [41], triplet loss method), there is a gap of about 1.6 points, but the paper's defense is powerful: GSC keywords are few, very short, and differ greatly from each other, with no challenging negative samples, whereas this paper's scheme is aimed at open-vocabulary tasks where keywords may be very similar to each other; the drop in GSC accuracy is the cost paid for generalization, and the results on MOCKS better reflect true capability (Section V).

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The main body of this paper's experiments is essentially two sets of ablations: boost weight scanning and multi-keyword classifier on/off.

**Finding 1: Three stages of the threshold curve (Fig. 4–6)**. When boost=1, the acceptance rate of the three subsets is constant within the threshold range [0, 0.05]—the reason is that MOCKS keywords are relatively short (phoneme transcription length p ≤ 16), and the normalized edit distance cannot distinguish differences in this interval. After the threshold exceeds 0.05, the acceptance rates of both pos and sim rise, but pos rises slower than sim—meaning the TPR increment gained by adding the threshold does not outpace the FPR increment. dif only starts to rise after the threshold > 0.4, because these negative samples differ too much from the keyword, and even if the AM's returned hypothesis does not match the correct transcription, it is far from the keyword. When boost>1, the pattern is the same, with the only exception being that the larger the boost, the earlier the dif curve takes off. Conclusion: taking the threshold as 0 is safe, and precisely because of this, subsequent experiments (Fig. 7) fix threshold=0 and only scan boost.

**Finding 2: Boost has a sweet spot, too much is as bad as too little (Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Table I)**. When boost ≤ 32, the acceptance rate of pos on all test sets increases faster than sim, and the acceptance rate on dif is small; after boost continues to increase, the relative growth rate of sim surpasses that of pos, and dif also surges—static LM starts to "do more harm than good." The EER curve (Fig. 8, boost ≤ 128) shows the minimum EER at 32 or 64, depending on the test set. When boost approaches 2048 (Fig. 7), the prior completely overwhelms the acoustic evidence.

**Finding 3: Multi-keyword classifier only has positive benefits in the low boost region (Fig. 10, Table III)**. The improvement on pos is greater than the increment on sim/dif; this condition only holds for low boost values; when using 2 TTS recordings, this difference is small, and when using 10, the improvement on pos is about 2 percentage points greater than sim (Section IV-B2). Verified from Table III: when boost 1, en_LS_clean goes from 48.39 (no multi) to 52.65 (multi 10×TTS), pos +4.26 pp, while sim goes from 1.28 to 3.96, +2.68 pp—indeed a difference of about 1.6–2 pp. But starting from boost=8, the situation reverses, and on all test sets, the increment of sim exceeds that of pos. The strongest configuration of boost 32 + multi 10×noisy TTS reaches pos 86.54 on en_LS_clean, but sim also rises to 29.22 (Table III)—a small gain in TPR accompanied by a larger proportional deterioration in FPR. Noisy TTS (mixed noise plus RIR) does not improve the situation on MOCKS: the increment of pos is actually smaller than sim. But the same noisy TTS is effective on GSC (Table II: 86.09 vs 85.13, 96.75 vs 96.07).

**Finding 4: Divergence of conclusions between the two test sets and their explanations (Section V)**. MOCKS and GSC give opposite verdicts on the multi-keyword classifier: on MOCKS, boost=32 plus 10 variants causes the FPR increment on sim to exceed the TPR increment on pos; on GSC, the same configuration still improves accuracy. The paper's explanation: GSC phrases are short, and negative samples differ greatly from keywords in character-level Levenshtein distance; MOCKS phrases are longer and contain a subset of pronunciation-approximate negative samples (sim), and adding variants simultaneously increases the probability of these difficult negative samples being falsely accepted. Thus, the engineering conclusion is: the multi-keyword classifier is an effective scheme in scenarios where high-challenge negative samples are not expected to be encountered. The empirical rule is that the number of variants should not exceed 10—the longer the list, the higher the probability of similar phrases being falsely accepted (Section V).

**Model-side numbers**: Student model quantized to 3.2 MB (13 MB before quantization), WER 16.0/30.9 (test-clean/test-other), RTF 0.05 (Abstract, Section III-A). Teacher model parameters 50.1 million, student 3.1 million (Section III-A).

## Main Contributions

First, a "open-vocabulary KWS on-device" route fully verified by experiments is provided: ASR-level MoChA acoustic model undergoes knowledge distillation (50.1 million → 3.1 million parameters) plus post-training 8-bit quantization (13 MB → 3.2 MB), achieving RTF 0.05 on contemporary smartphones, proving that open-vocabulary capability and edge-side footprint can coexist. Second, a boost re-scoring method for static 1-gram LM is proposed: a prior injection mechanism of a two-value vector and one element-wise multiplication, pulling GSC accuracy from 84.60% to 95.97% (Table II) and cutting en_LS_clean EER from 30.05 to 15.18 (Table I), while the memory and latency costs are nearly zero, and it is universal for any AM architecture. Third, a registration-free TTS multi-keyword classifier is proposed: automatically expanding keyword pronunciation variants using synthetic speech, specifically treating high-rarity keywords unseen during training (named entities), without requiring users to speak or on-device weight updates; bringing 0.78–2.3 pp accuracy increments on GSC and in the low boost interval (Section IV-B2). Fourth, the pos/sim/dif three-part evaluation first exposes the false alarm cost structure of two types of means: "boost prior" and "variant expansion"—the complete bill of FPR from 1.3% to 29.22% on the sim subset (Table III), which has warning value for all subsequent work on keyword prior injection. Fifth, the two improvements are independent, can be combined arbitrarily, and can be applied to any AM (Abstract), with strong engineering usability.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, boost is a global single hyperparameter and has no universal value: the minimum EER for en_LS_clean and en_MCV is at boost=32, and for en_LS_other at boost=64 (Table I, Section V). More troublesome is that the optimal value in this paper is selected post-hoc by scanning EER on the test set; when deploying to unknown keywords, one has no way of knowing its optimal boost—meaning the sweet spot parameter relies on evaluation set information. Second, the expressive ceiling of the static LM is extremely low: 1-gram, two-value weights, only expressing "these subwords are more important," unable to express subword collocations or phoneme-level pronunciation constraints (related work [13] uses a phoneme-level n-gram LM), which determines its powerlessness against false alarms on the sim subset. Third, the multi-keyword classifier has a negative net benefit in the high boost region and under MOCKS-type difficult negative samples (Table III); its applicable boundaries are clearly drawn by the paper itself, but the system itself has no mechanism to detect "whether the current scenario has crossed the boundary." Fourth, character-level Levenshtein matching cuts subsequences by word count, lacking robustness guarantees for AM hypothesis word segmentation errors (multiple words, merged words), which the paper does not analyze specifically. Fifth, the system is only evaluated in non-streaming mode (explicitly declared in Section I); although the MoChA architecture is designed for online processing, its behavior under streaming deployment is unverified; keyword classification relies on the entire hypothesis, and streaming transformation is not a simple平移 (translation). Sixth, the impact of 8-bit quantization on KWS decision metrics (TPR/FPR) is not reported separately (only WER is given); the changes in KWS metrics before and after quantization are not reported by the paper.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First, the subword vocabulary is "generated from all transcriptions of the training and test sets" (Section III-A)—test set transcriptions participate in vocabulary construction. Although labels are not leaked, this allows the output layer to have implicit adaptation to the test domain vocabulary. Strictly speaking, the evaluation protocol has slight contamination. Second, language and speaker coverage is narrow: the AM is trained only on English (LibriSpeech + Common Voice English), MOCKS is a multilingual test set but only three English subsets are used, and multilingual open-vocabulary capability is unverified; TTS experiments also use only one male and one female timbre. Third, comparison with SOTA is not on the same task plane: the opponent of 98.37% (citation [41]) is a method on fixed-vocabulary GSC, and the paper itself admits comparability is limited (Section V); on the more appropriate MOCKS, the paper lacks horizontal comparison numbers with other open-vocabulary KWS methods (the MOCKS predecessor was just published at the time). Fourth, absolute latency, peak memory, engine volume, and RTF test equipment and conditions (which model, how many threads) are not reported by the paper—the claim that "the entire system (model plus engine)" fits into a smartphone lacks engine-side number support. Fifth, TTS domain mismatch is not handled: TTS is trained on LJ Speech (single-speaker reading) and Hi-Fi data, and the acoustic difference from real user speech may partially explain why noisy TTS is ineffective on MOCKS, but the paper does not perform attribution analysis. Sixth, the keyword threshold `t` is also global, without per-word adaptation, and the impact of keyword length differences (p ≤ 16 but uneven distribution) on normalized distance is not discussed.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

The paper itself lists three directions in Section VI: a method for automatically selecting the optimal boost value (turning the process of finding the sweet spot by scanning into an algorithm); setting respective boost values for different keywords (using keyword rarity or pronunciation characteristics to adapt prior strength); and combining clean and noisy TTS recordings as an ensemble into the multi-keyword classifier, attempting to compress FPR without harming TPR. Based on this, combined with the problems exposed by this paper, extensions can be made: upgrading the 1-gram two-value LM to a small phoneme-level or subword-level n-gram LM (following the idea of [13] but controlling volume), which may improve the false alarm structure on the sim subset; diversifying TTS speaker/recording conditions to narrow the synthetic-real domain gap; introducing confidence weighting of multi-keyword variants (rather than a binary list) into the decision; and verifying on all multilingual subsets of MOCKS, and supplementing horizontal comparisons with contemporaneous open-vocabulary methods. These directions jointly point to one goal: turning the "benefit of prior injection" and "false alarm cost" from global knobs into continuous control surfaces that can be adjusted per word and per scenario.
