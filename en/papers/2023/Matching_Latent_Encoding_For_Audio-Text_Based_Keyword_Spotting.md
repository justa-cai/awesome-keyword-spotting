## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Voice assistants on devices such as smartphones and smart speakers have become infrastructure for daily life, with their entry points almost invariably relying on Keyword Spotting (KWS): determining whether a user has spoken a specific trigger phrase or voice command within a continuous stream of input audio. Section 1 of the paper divides the KWS task into two categories: fixed-vocabulary KWS, where the model serves only a fixed list of keywords defined during training; and flexible KWS, where the model must handle any user-registered keywords that were never seen during training. Flexible KWS is a "highly challenging task" fundamentally because the model must discriminate within an open set, with the reference point appearing only at registration time.

There are two paths for users to register custom keywords: recording audio samples, i.e., query-by-example (QbE) KWS; or providing text directly. This paper chooses the latter, a decision understandable from both engineering and user experience perspectives: text registration does not require users to read sample phrases multiple times to the device, lowering the registration threshold; text itself is a symbolic, noise-free supervisory signal that does not introduce quality fluctuations in registration audio. However, text registration transforms the problem into a true cross-modal comparison problem—determining whether the spoken content of an audio segment matches a given text sequence. This "seemingly simple" comparison harbors a set of structural contradictions at the embedding representation level: the audio encoder outputs a frame-level sequence of hundreds of frames (this paper uses 80-channel filter bank features with a 25ms window and 10ms stride; the Conformer has internal subsampling layers, so the number of encoded frames $n$ is less than the original frame count $n'$), while the text encoder outputs a sequence of a few word-level tokens (in this scenario $m \le 4$). The two sequences differ not only in length but also in the semantic granularity of their indices: one index in the audio sequence carries acoustic information for approximately 25 milliseconds, while one index in the text sequence represents a complete word. The abstract succinctly points out the core challenge: how to achieve semantic alignment between audio and text embeddings under conditions of multi-word, unequal-length sequences, a problem that remains largely unsolved.

Further deconstructing the pain points, there are three layers. First, the length gap and granularity gap exist simultaneously: even if the audio sequence is compressed to match the length of the text, it remains unknown which frames should occupy each "slot." Second, word duration is naturally uneven: as given in Section 4.3 of the paper, the total duration of the positive example audio is 550ms, where "the" occupies 90ms, "old" occupies 230ms, and "man" occupies 230ms, with relative proportions of approximately 0.16:0.42:0.42—any naive scheme of "equally dividing the audio sequence" is physically incorrect, as the duration of short function words and long content words can differ by nearly three times. Third, the two embedding spaces are heterogeneous: the audio encoder (Conformer trained for ASR) outputs a 144-dimensional representation with acoustic bias, while the text encoder (DistilBERT trained for NLU) outputs a 768-dimensional representation with semantic bias. With different dimensions and optimization objectives, directly comparing distances is meaningless.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

Section 2 of the paper organizes existing technical routes for flexible KWS into four categories, each deficiency pointing to the core issue of "comparing unequal-length audio-text sequences":

- **DTW-based methods** (References [8,9,10]): Use Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) to measure the distance between two unequal-length embedded sequences for KWS. DTW finds the optimal match between two sequences, and its alignment relationship is bidirectional one-to-many—any index in one sequence can be aligned with multiple indices in the other sequence. This is reasonable for homogeneous modality comparison (audio vs. audio) because indices at both ends represent the same entity (e.g., both are 25ms frames). However, audio vs. text is a heterogeneous modality: the correct alignment relationship should be asymmetric, i.e., one word token on the text side corresponds to multiple frames on the audio side, but not vice versa (one audio frame cannot simultaneously belong to two words). DTW's symmetric one-to-many mechanism cannot express this constraint; as the paper states, it "becomes inapplicable or inaccurate."
- **Projection-based methods** (References [4,11,12]): Project both audio and text sequences to a fixed sequence length to simplify distance calculation, at the cost of losing critical semantic context during the projection (which is also a dimensionality reduction) process. These schemes learn word-level discrimination better than DTW-based methods but perform poorly in multi-word keyword detection—precisely the scenario where sequence length information is most crucial.
- **Phoneme-based methods** (Reference [13], Sacchi et al. 2019): Audio and text encoders output variable-length embedded sequences at the phoneme level, which are then aggregated into single embedding vectors by GRUs respectively for comparison. Phoneme-level embeddings fail to effectively distinguish words because they cannot capture word-level semantics—the discriminative power of a word often comes from the word as a whole rather than a bag of phonemes.
- **SOTA Baseline** (Reference [5], Shin et al. Interspeech 2022, "Learning Audio-Text Agreement for Open-vocabulary KWS"): Measures the correspondence between audio and text, reporting results superior to CTC-based and attention-based methods, serving as the direct baseline for this paper. The paper points out two specific flaws: First, the text encoder is constructed based on grapheme and phoneme sequences, lacking understanding of the semantics of text keywords, thus "being weak against words with similar pronunciation"—a negative example with similar pronunciation but completely different semantics is hardly distinguishable by grapheme/phoneme representations. Second, it explicitly learns the pattern between text and audio embeddings using a monotonic matching loss, compromising alignment flexibility, "potentially resulting in suboptimal alignment."

### Key Challenges to be Solved

The paper formalizes the problem to be solved into three constraints in Contribution 1: comparing two sequences of different lengths, (a) content monotonic alignment—the order of words in audio matches the text; (b) one-to-many relationship from short sequence (text) indices to long sequence (audio)—one word corresponds to several consecutive audio frames; (c) one-to-one relationship from long sequence audio frames to short sequence—one audio frame must belong to and only to one word. Under these constraints, find the optimal partition of the audio sequence and build an end-to-end trainable discriminative model around it, while being able to reuse two independently pre-trained encoders with different output dimensions (144 and 768). These four tasks collectively constitute the technical proposition of the paper.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The proposed model is called EMKWS (Encoding Matching based KeyWord Spotter). The overall architecture is shown in Figure 1, consisting of three modules connected in series: encoder, projector, and audio-text aligner, which jointly drive the contrastive loss function. The input is a triplet $(a', t', l')$: audio $a'$ is a sequence of $n'$ frames, text $t'$ is a sequence of $m'$ tokens (for clarity, the paper loosely refers to text as a word sequence), and $l'$ is the ground truth binary label, where positive pairs are 1 and negative pairs are 0. The output is a binary judgment of "whether the audio content matches the text."

Each link in the three-stage architecture has a clear design motivation:

- **Why encoders are frozen and reused rather than jointly trained from scratch**: The audio side uses a tiny version of Conformer (hyperparameters given in Sections 3.1 and 4.1: 6 encoding layers, encoding dimension 144, kernel size 3, 4 attention heads), first trained for ASR tasks (output layer is a 29-dimensional character linear layer, CTC loss). After training, the linear layer is removed, and the entire model is frozen. The reason for choosing Conformer is stated directly in the paper: it combines self-attention to learn global interactions and convolution to efficiently capture local correlations, covering both the sequential structure and global context of speech. The text side uses DistilBERT—the distilled smaller version of BERT, which naturally outputs embeddings oriented towards semantic understanding. Both encoders are well-validated assets in their respective fields. Freezing and reusing them saves training compute and brings a deployment dividend: the text embeddings of registered keywords can be pre-computed offline, and the text encoder is not needed at all during inference (Section 4.2: EMKWS uses pre-computed embeddings during inference, with a total parameter count of 3.7M).
- **Why the projector must exist**: Audio embedding dimension $d_1=144$, text embedding dimension $d_2=768$ (Section 4.2). One is acoustically biased, the other semantically biased, so they cannot be compared directly. The role of the projector is to transform both into the same latent space dimension $d=144$. The essential difference from existing projection schemes [4,11,12] is that the projection here **does not change the sequence length**, only the embedding space transformation—audio enters with $n$ frames and exits with $n$ frames of $d$-dimensional vectors, text enters with $m$ tokens and exits with $m$ $d$-dimensional vectors. The semantic context carried by the sequence is not lost due to length compression.
- **Why the aligner is designed as a "partition + aggregation" two-step process**: Partition the $n$-frame audio embeddings into $m$ continuous blocks, take the mean within each block to aggregate into $m$ vectors, and compare the L2 distance with the $m$ word vectors bit-by-bit. The brilliance of this design lies in transforming the continuous optimization problem of "aligning unequal-length sequences" into a combinatorial optimization problem of "continuous integer partitioning," which happens to have a classic dynamic programming solution.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

**Formalization of Sequence Partitioning** (Section 3.3): Partition the audio embedding sequence $a=(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ into $m$ continuous subsequences denoted as $p=(p_1,\dots,p_m)$, represented by $m+1$ indices $i_0,i_1,\dots,i_m$ ($i_0=1$, $i_m=n+1$), where $p_k = a_{i_{(k-1):i_k}}$. The notation $a_{i:j}$ represents the subsequence $(a_i, a_{i+1}, \dots, a_{j-1})$. The aggregation step takes the mean of each subsequence (denoted with an overline in the paper), resulting in the output sequence $(\bar{p}_1,\dots,\bar{p}_m)$—$m$ $d$-dimensional vectors that can be directly compared with the text embedding $t=(t_1,\dots,t_m)$. The distance metric is the average pairwise L2 distance: $(1/m) \cdot \sum_{k=1}^{m} \|\bar{p}_k - t_k\|_2$.

**Optimal Partitioning Objective** (Equation 1):

$$z(a,t) = \min_{p \in \text{Partition}(a,m)} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{k=1}^{m} \|\bar{p}_k - t_k\|_2$$

where $\text{Partition}(a,m)$ is the set of all possible partitions of $a$ into $m$ blocks. Note that this min is embedded in the training objective: at each training step, for each sample, the optimal partition is re-solved for the current embeddings, and the distance under the optimal partition participates in loss backpropagation. Alignment is not a priori fixed (unlike the monotonic matching loss of baseline [5]), but is determined by the current representation state and data—this is the source of "alignment flexibility."

**DSP Dynamic Programming Solution** (Algorithm 1): Define $D \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, where $D[k][i]$ stores the optimal loss for the subproblem "partitioning subsequence $a_{i:(n+1)}$ into $k$ blocks and aligning with the last $k$ tokens of the text." Boundary condition $D[1][i] = \|\text{mean}(a_{i:(n+1)}) - t_m\|_2$, i.e., "treat everything from $i$ to the end of the sequence as one block and align with the last word." The recurrence is: for $k=2,\dots,m$, $i=1,\dots,n-k+1$, first calculate all $\delta_j = \|\text{mean}(a_{i:j}) - t_{(m-k+1)}\|_2$ ($i<j \le n-k+2$), then take $D[k][i] = \min_{i<j \le n-k+2} (\delta_j + D[k-1][j])$. The final output is $D[m][1]/m$, i.e., the optimal average loss of partitioning the entire audio into $m$ blocks to align with $m$ words. The root of DP correctness is that the partitioning problem has optimal substructure: the optimal partition of a prefix must be extended from the optimal partition of a smaller prefix; exhaustive search of all partitions is a combinatorial explosion, while DP compresses the search to polynomial complexity using an $m \times n$ table. The paper provides complexity analysis: time $O(mn^2)$, but since $m$ is extremely small in the keyword scenario (in this paper $m \le 4$), it degenerates to $O(n^2)$ in practice; space $O(n)$.

**Contrastive Loss** (Equation 2, cited from Hadsell/Chopra/LeCun 2006):

$$L = l' \cdot \max(z - m_{\text{pos}}, 0) + (1-l') \cdot \max(m_{\text{neg}} - z, 0)$$

$l'$ is the ground truth label, $z$ is the distance calculated by DSP, and $m_{\text{pos}}$, $m_{\text{neg}}$ are the margins for positive and negative examples respectively. Positive examples push $z$ below $m_{\text{pos}}$, negative examples push $z$ above $m_{\text{neg}}$, with zero gradient outside the hinge—positive examples do not need to be infinitely close, negative examples do not need to be infinitely far, avoiding excessive stretching of the representation space. Section 4.2 gives hyperparameter search: $m_{\text{pos}} \in \{0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.25, 0.4, 1.0\}$, $m_{\text{neg}} \in \{2, 2.5, 4, 7, 10, 15\}$, with the optimal combination $m_{\text{pos}}=0.2$, $m_{\text{neg}}=7$. The reason $m_{\text{neg}}$ is an order of magnitude larger than $m_{\text{pos}}$ is easy to understand: negative pairs (especially near-homophone negatives in Libriphrase Hard) share a large amount of acoustic content, so L2 distances are naturally small, and the negative margin must be large enough to push the decision boundary to a usable position.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Dynamic Sequence Partitioning (DSP) Algorithm

DSP is the core increment of the paper, and its "novelty" deserves thorough explanation. First, it clearly diagnoses the root cause of DTW's inapplicability: DTW's symmetric one-to-many alignment is an incorrect inductive bias for heterogeneous modalities (frame-level audio vs. word-level text); the correct constraint should be unidirectional "word-to-frame one-to-many, frame-to-word one-to-one." DSP's partition-based modeling (each frame must fall into and only into one block, blocks correspond to words in order) is the most direct mathematical expression of this asymmetric constraint. Second, it forms a methodological contrast with the monotonic matching loss of baseline [5]: [5] "learns the alignment pattern to death" in the parameters using an explicit matching loss, sacrificing alignment flexibility; DSP, on the other hand, seeks the global optimal partition under the current representation at each training step, with alignment implicitly evolving with the representation. Theoretically, every step sees the "best possible mapping perspective achievable at that moment" (original quote from Section 3.3: the optimal mapping provides the best possible mapping perspective throughout the training process, and results in Figure 2 show this approach is particularly effective for hard samples). Third, it has strict optimality guarantees—dynamic programming finds the exact optimal solution to Equation 1 rather than an approximation, which is superior to heuristic alignment.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Length-Preserving Projector and Dual-Frozen Heterogeneous Encoders

The structure of the projection block is extremely simple: two LayerNorms plus two linear layers (right side of Figure 1), transforming audio embeddings of dimension $d_1=144$ and text embeddings of dimension $d_2=768$ into a common space of dimension $d=144$ (Section 4.2). Choosing the target dimension 144 to match the audio encoder is meaningful: "compressing" the text side from 768 dimensions into the audio latent space's dimension is equivalent to distilling large-model semantics into a low-dimensional space friendly to edge devices, while the audio path does not need dimensionality expansion, adding no edge-side compute. More importantly, the setting is "two independently pre-trained encoders can also be paired to work"—ASR encoders and NLU encoders have completely different pre-training objectives, different dimensions, and have never seen each other. They are pulled into the same metric space relying only on this lightweight projector and DSP aligner. Contribution 2 lists this as a separate item because this setting implies that any existing strong encoder can be "plugged" into this framework without joint pre-training. The配套 design on the inference side is pre-computation of text embeddings: calculate word embeddings once during registration and store them; during runtime, only the audio path remains, with the entire model having 3.7M parameters (Section 4.2), which is a substantial good news for edge-side deployment.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Discriminative End-to-End Training Unifying Single-Word and Multi-Word Keywords

Contribution 3 of the paper is unifying single-word and multi-word keywords into a discriminative setting: the model does not learn generation or reconstruction, but directly learns the binary discrimination of "whether this audio-text pair matches," with the training signal being the contrastive loss of Equation 2. In the single-word case $m=1$, DSP degenerates to aligning one block of the entire segment with one word; in the multi-word case, DSP automatically undertakes word boundary segmentation. The entire trainable part (projector + differentiable path involving the aligner) is trained for 165 epochs with the Adam optimizer, transformer learning rate scheduling plus 2k warm-up steps (Section 4.2), with both encoders frozen throughout. This setting transforms "multi-word" from a special case into a naturally covered norm, which is a structural advantage relative to phoneme-based methods [13] (GRU aggregates into a single vector, losing word count information) and projection-based methods [4,11,12] (fixed-length projection, poor performance on multi-word).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compare the four existing routes with EMKWS dimension by dimension. Compared to DTW-based methods [9,10]: the alignment relationship changes from bidirectional one-to-many to unidirectional one-to-many plus one-to-one, extending from homogeneous modalities to heterogeneous modalities, and distance calculation changes from deformation path cost to partition mean L2. Compared to projection-based methods [4,11,12]: projection changes from "changing sequence length, compressing semantics" to "keeping sequence length, only performing space transformation," recovering multi-word capability due to the preservation of sequence structure. Compared to phoneme-based methods [13]: the modeling unit is elevated from phonemes to words (subwords), with semantics on the text side carried by DistilBERT rather than phoneme sequences, enhancing word-level discrimination. Compared to baseline [5]: the text encoder changes from graphemes/phonemes to a semantically pre-trained model, enhancing resistance to near-homophone negatives (confirmed by improvements on the Hard set in subsequent experiments); alignment changes from explicit monotonic matching loss to implicit optimal solving by DSP, preserving alignment flexibility. Additionally, among all comparison objects, only this paper explicitly provides the edge-side deployment form of "text encoder leaves during inference, 3.7M parameters."

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The data stack has two layers (Section 4.1). The bottom layer is LibriSpeech: the ASR pre-training of the audio encoder uses the train-clean-100 and train-clean-360 subsets, with character-level tokenization (Section 4.1). The upper layer is Libriphrase—a phrase-level dataset generated from LibriSpeech according to the method of baseline [5]: the training set is generated from train-clean-100 and train-clean-360, the evaluation set is generated from train-others-500; the content consists of phrases of 1, 2, 3, and 4 words; each sample is a triplet (audio, text, target), where target=1 when audio and text content match. Negative examples are divided into easy and hard categories, based on the Levenshtein distance between the audio content and the paired text—small distance (near-homophone, near-shape) constitutes hard negatives, large distance constitutes easy negatives. To ensure strict comparability with [5], the authors constructed the same evaluation sets Libriphrase Easy (LE) and Libriphrase Hard (LH), containing 4391, 2605, 467, and 56 episodes respectively for word lengths 1/2/3/4, with each episode containing 3 positive pairs and 3 negative pairs (Section 4.1). Audio features are 80-channel filter banks, 25ms window, 10ms stride (Section 4.1). The total number of training set episodes is not reported by the paper. The implementation environment is PyTorch, x86 Linux, and NVIDIA V100 GPU (beginning of Section 4).

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Two metrics: AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve) and EER (Equal Error Rate). The rationale for selection can be explained from the perspectives of task nature and comparison fairness. Flexible KWS is essentially a binary verification task of "whether the registered text matches the test audio." The acceptance threshold during deployment is determined by product strategy (cost of missed wake-up vs. cost of false wake-up). At the paper stage, using a threshold-independent metric is fairer—AUC measures the overall quality of positive/negative ranking under all possible thresholds, unaffected by single-point threshold selection. EER is the error rate when the false acceptance rate equals the false rejection rate, compressing two types of errors into one number, which is a historical convention for detection tasks. Both metrics are reported by baseline [5], directly aligning to eliminate口径 differences.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

Main results are in Table 1, averaging three training runs, with full numbers as follows. Baseline [5]: AUC 96.7%, EER 8.42% on LE; AUC 73.58%, EER 32.9% on LH. This paper's three runs: run1 is LE AUC 97.83 / LH AUC 84.21 / LE EER 7.26 / LH EER 23.37; run2 is 97.84 / 84.18 / 7.34 / 23.29; run3 is 97.81 / 84.24 / 7.48 / 23.42; average is 97.83 / 84.21 / 7.36 / 23.36. The relative improvements (Improvement row) reported by the paper: LE AUC +1.2%, LH AUC +14.4%, LE EER −12.6%, LH EER −28.9%.

Two phenomena are worth digging into. First, the improvement on the hard set is much larger than on the easy set: on LH, AUC absolute value increases from 73.58 to 84.21 (relative +14.4%), EER drops from 32.9 to 23.36 (relative −28.9%), while on LE, AUC only increases from 96.7 to 97.83 (absolute 1.13 points). Why: easy negatives in LE differ significantly from keywords, and any decent representation can separate them; the baseline is already at 96.7%, showing a clear ceiling effect. Hard negatives in LH are near-homophone words with small Levenshtein distance, which is precisely the target of baseline [5]'s flaw—"grapheme/phoneme text encoder cannot grasp semantics, weak against near-homophone words." This paper's replacement with a semantic text encoder (DistilBERT) plus optimal partitioning alignment makes the discrimination power truly visible on hard samples. Second, the variance across three runs is extremely small (LH AUC fluctuates between 84.18 and 84.24), indicating the results are not due to lucky seeds. At the same time, one must maintain clarity of口径: the paper reports relative percentages; the 1.2% improvement on LE corresponds to an absolute 1.13 points, so do not exaggerate when interpreting.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper performs three groups of analyses with different depths. The first group is partitioning scheme ablation (Figure 2): fixing other parts, only changing the audio sequence partitioning algorithm—random partitioning, equal partitioning, DSP—DSP is consistently optimal in AUC and EER on both LE and LH. The paper frankly admits that multi-word samples in the evaluation set are limited (episode counts for each word length 4391/2605/467/56, with 3-word and 4-word samples being scarce). This self-statement has two sides: on one hand, it weakens the direct verification strength of the core selling point "DSP focuses on multi-word alignment"; on the other hand, it shows that even on an evaluation distribution dominated by single words, optimal partitioning still provides stable gains relative to random/equal partitioning—because even within a single word, the attribution of frames to blocks is not uniform (silence, low-energy segments should not be weighted equally with semantic core frames).

The second group is embedding correlation visualization (Figure 3): performing text-to-audio and audio-to-audio correlation analysis on the audio/text embeddings output by the projector. The positive example "The Old Man" (Figures 3a, 3b) presents three strong monotonic correlation blocks, each aligning with one word; meanwhile, audio frames within each word block are completely self-correlated. More hardcore evidence is the duration accounting: the input audio for this example is 550ms, with "the", "old", "man" pronounced for 90ms, 230ms, 230ms respectively, with relative proportions of approximately 0.16:0.42:0.42, while the partition sizes given by DSP are (2, 5, 5)—2/12≈0.17, 5/12≈0.42, matching the true pronunciation proportions (the paper marks these with dashed vertical lines in Figures 3a, 3b). The significance of this accounting is that throughout the process, there is no word-level timestamp supervision; DSP purely recovers linguistically meaningful word duration structures from the "optimal L2 distance" objective, moving alignment quality from numerical metrics to interpretable evidence. The four-word positive example "Of The United States" (Figures 3c, 3d) presents the same pattern.

The third group is hyperparameter grid (Section 4.2): search of six levels of $m_{\text{pos}}$ × six levels of $m_{\text{neg}}$, with optimal (0.2, 7). Looking at the ablations not done also provides information: encoder selection (e.g., replacing DistilBERT back with a phoneme encoder, or switching to full BERT), projector depth, aggregation method (replacing mean with attention pooling) have no control experiments—the paper does not report these, so these attributions can only remain at the level of reasonable inference.

## Main Contributions

The paper lists three contributions at the end of Section 1, which can be summarized into four points including experimental support:

1. **DSP Algorithm Itself**: Formalizes a class of unequal-length sequence comparison problems—content monotonic alignment, one-to-many from short sequence indices to long sequence, one-to-one from long sequence indices to short sequence—and provides an exact optimal solution based on dynamic programming, with time complexity $O(mn^2)$ degenerating to $O(n^2)$ in scenarios where $m \le 4$, and space $O(n)$. This formalization goes beyond the single KWS task, applicable to any "fine-grained sequence to coarse-grained sequence" alignment problem (e.g., video frames to sentences, characters to words).
2. **Flexible Embedding Matching Setting**: Proves that two independently pre-trained encoders with different optimization objectives and different dimensions (144 and 768) can be mapped into the same 144-dimensional space via a lightweight projector (two LayerNorms plus two linear layers) and paired effectively, with audio sequence embeddings of the same text clustering together. This breaks the implicit assumption that "cross-modal pairing must be jointly pre-trained."
3. **Discriminative End-to-End Training Unifying Single-Word and Multi-Word Keywords**: The contrastive loss centered on DSP distance covers both single-word (degenerate case $m=1$) and multi-word, combined with frozen encoders and pre-computed text embeddings, forming an inference form with 3.7M parameters.
4. **SOTA Results**: Relative to baseline [5], AUC improved by 14.4% (LH), EER reduced by 28.9% (LH) on public LE/LH datasets (Table 1), and interpretability evidence for alignment is provided via correlation visualization in Figure 3.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Strict monotonic continuous partitioning assumption is strong**: DSP requires continuous coverage of the entire audio segment, with block order consistent with word order. Pauses between words in real speech are forced into adjacent word blocks; scenarios like users saying "ok ok, please..." involving repetition or inserted filler words violate the monotonic assumption; stuttering or inversion directly disqualifies the sample. The paper does not discuss these boundary cases.
- **Mean aggregation erases intra-word temporal structure**: Each block is compressed into a mean vector for comparison, and the dynamics within the word (sequence of phonemes, coarticulation) do not participate in distance calculation. This is the necessary cost for making the objective function solvable by dynamic programming, but it means that confusion pairs with "different intra-word content, same word mean" cannot be detected by the model.
- **Frozen encoders cap the upper limit**: Acoustic embeddings are optimized for ASR, text embeddings for natural language understanding; neither is a representation optimized for KWS discrimination. The entire learnable capacity to bridge the cross-modal gap is only the two LayerNorms and two linear layers of the projector. If there are regions of fundamental misalignment between the two frozen spaces, the projector is powerless.
- **Training-time $O(n^2)$ overhead**: A DP solution is required for every forward pass. In this paper's scenario, audio is short (e.g., 550ms in the example), so $n$ is small; if extended to long audio or finer-grained streaming segmentation, the quadratic term will become apparent.
- **Subword problem is "loosely handled"**: Section 3.1 explicitly states that the DistilBERT tokenizer may split a word into multiple subword tokens (thus $m \ge m'$), and the paper "loosely refers to text as a word sequence for clarity." That is, the actual alignment unit is subwords rather than words, creating a gap between the claim of "word-level semantics" and the implementation, and the impact of subword granularity on performance is not analyzed separately.
- **Simple metric structure**: L2 distance plus double hinge, embeddings are not normalized, and the actual scale of the margin depends on the distribution of embedding norms. Hyperparameters will likely need re-tuning when transferring across datasets.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

- **Narrow data domain**: LibriSpeech is a clean, near-field English corpus of read audiobooks, lacking far-field, noisy, reverberant, and accent variables, with a significant domain gap from the real acoustic environment of smart speakers; the paper performs no robustness experiments.
- **Weak multi-word validation**: The paper itself admits that multi-word samples in the evaluation set are limited—only 56 episodes for 4 words, while the core selling point of DSP is precisely multi-word alignment. The mismatch between the core claim and validation strength is the biggest gap in the experimental design of this paper.
- **Single baseline**: Only compares numbers with one baseline [5]; [4], [13], and QbE-based methods have no direct comparison, making it impossible to locate this method's relative position in the entire flexible KWS landscape.
- **No efficiency measurements**: Only reports 3.7M parameters; inference latency, real-time factor, peak memory, and edge-side hardware benchmarks are all absent (training on V100 does not represent edge-side verification)—the paper does not report these. Whether DSP overhead can be streamed during inference is also not discussed.
- **Improvement口径**: The Improvement row is relative percentage (e.g., LE AUC +1.2% corresponds to an absolute 1.13 points); the headline 14.4%/28.9% all come from the hard set. While reasonable (the hard set better distinguishes methods), absolute quantities need to be converted when reading.
- **No word-length stratified detail table**: Results only have LE/LH summaries plus curve trends in Figure 2, without tabulated numbers stratified by 1/2/3/4 words; although three runs are stable, there is no statistical significance test.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Unfreeze or adapterize encoders**: Use parameter-efficient fine-tuning like LoRA or adapters to allow both encoders to moderately adapt to the KWS discrimination objective, breaking the upper limit of "fully frozen" while preserving most of the value of pre-trained assets.
- **Learnable aggregation replacing mean**: Weighted aggregation such as attention pooling can preserve intra-word temporal structure at the edge of maintaining DP solvability (or switch to approximate search), directly addressing the information loss of mean aggregation.
- **Subword-aware alignment**: Explicitly model the subword structure and word boundaries on the text side, or use a word-level tokenizer encoder, eliminating the gap of "loose handling."
- **Construct large-scale multi-word and real far-field evaluation sets**: Increase the sample size of 3-word and 4-word samples from 467/56 to the same order of magnitude as 1-word, adding far-field noisy conditions, to solidly verify the multi-word claim and practical value of DSP.
- **Streaming and low-latency**: Current DSP solves the entire segment offline; researching incremental DP (updating only affected table entries when new frames arrive) is the inevitable path towards real-time wake-up.
- **Stronger negative example engineering**: Semantic text embeddings have proven effective against near-homophone negatives (improvement on LH); further introducing semantic near-negatives like synonyms or same-initial words, or using hard negative mining to strengthen contrastive signals, is expected to further reduce EER.
- **Multilingual extension**: DistilBERT is a monolingual English model; cross-lingual requires multilingual text encoders and multilingual data, which is a mandatory question for the method to move towards products.

Comprehensive Evaluation: This is a short paper with a clean problem definition and a method that fits the problem seamlessly—converging "audio-text unequal-length sequence alignment" into a dynamic programming problem with an exact optimal solution, leveraging two frozen pre-trained encoders with the fewest learnable components (projector), and achieving a significant improvement on the hard negative set. Its shortcomings are also typical: the multi-word claim lacks multi-word data, single baseline and single dataset, no edge-side verification. For engineering readers, two transferable judgments are: first, for heterogeneous modality alignment, clarifying the inductive bias of "one-to-many direction" is more critical than stacking model capacity; second, the combination of freezing large encoders plus a lightweight projection head is a realistic template for edge-side cross-modal tasks under compute constraints.
