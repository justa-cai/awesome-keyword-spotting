## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) involves recognizing predefined keywords from a continuous audio stream, serving as the entry point for voice interaction between mobile devices and virtual assistants (Section 1). Unlike general speech tasks, KWS audio streams are typically processed locally on user devices to save computational and communication overhead (Section 2 of the original text)—this inherently makes KWS an edge-side task: models are deployed on devices such as smart speakers and earbuds, and the most valuable training data is generated precisely on these devices.

Real-world industrial KWS training faces a data dilemma with two opposing ends. On one end, labeled data is expensive and scarce: speech annotation requires manual transcription; in binary classification tasks like wakeword detection, positive samples (segments where the wakeword was actually spoken) are extremely rare in real traffic, making collection difficult. The internal wakeword dataset used for training in this paper contains approximately 14K hours (Section 4.1), and relying on manual annotation for this scale is infeasible in an industrial setting. On the other end, there is a massive amount of unlabeled audio streams on devices: smart speakers continuously capture audio, generating huge volumes of data, but users will not label this data, and under privacy constraints, raw audio cannot be uploaded to the cloud for centralized processing.

Federated Learning (FL, a distributed training paradigm where parameters are uploaded instead of data) seems to fill this gap: the abstract states that FL can leverage the computing power and privacy-sensitive data of a large number of distributed devices to train models. However, existing FL methods have a fatal premise mismatch—the abstract quotes: they "typically require devices to possess accurate ground-truth labels, which is both expensive and impractical when dealing with local audio data." Users do not label the audio recorded by their own speakers; this assumption collapses directly in the KWS scenario.

Thus, the problem structure of this paper is very clear: the server has a small amount of labeled data (which can be generated by annotation teams or high-confidence transcription pipelines), and the devices have a massive amount of heterogeneous unlabeled data. How can the two collaborate to train a KWS model—without letting the participation of unlabeled devices drag the model down? Note that the heterogeneity of interest here is specifically label-skewed: the distribution of keywords differs across devices (Section 1), which is the most difficult type of data heterogeneity in FL.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

First, pioneering works on FL for KWS assume that devices have labels. Leroy et al. (ICASSP 2019, Reference [8]) were the first to prove that FL is beneficial for KWS and can utilize local data and computing power, but their setting relies on clients being able to provide annotations for local data. In real deployments, devices only have unlabeled audio streams, so the premise of this route does not hold.

Second, state-of-the-art Semi-Supervised Learning (SSL) is centralized and cannot be directly applied. Methods like FixMatch (Reference [6]) are built on weak/strong augmentation consistency training and can achieve performance close to fully supervised learning, but they require all data to be centralized into a single training pool—this directly conflicts with audio privacy constraints, meaning the largest portion of data on devices can never enter the training pool.

Third, a naive parallel combination of SSL and FL will fail. Updating supervised training on the server and unsupervised training on devices separately, then directly aggregating parameters (referred to as Parallel training in this paper), leads to significant performance degradation when devices execute multiple local epochs, as cited in References [2][7] in Section 3. Mechanistically, the optimization directions of supervised loss and pseudo-label unsupervised loss are inherently different. Under Non-IID conditions, device models drift individually. Forcing an average of these "solutions with inconsistent training objectives" in parameter space results in mutual destruction rather than mutual complementarity. Table 1 in this paper confirms this with numbers: under the most heterogeneous Non-IID K=2 split, parallel FL achieves only 68.2(1.7)/77.9(2.4) accuracy (V1/V2), whereas the same data with alternating training reaches 98.3(0.1)/97.6(0.0).

Fourth, there is a lack of augmentation strategies for speech SSL. The engine of SOTA SSL is strong data augmentation (emphasized in both the abstract and Section 3), but existing augmentation methods for KWS—time shifting, resampling, adding background noise (Rybakov et al. [5]), and SpecAugment-style time-frequency masking (Park et al. [4])—are regularization spices designed for supervised training. After treating mel-spectrograms as images, whether computer vision strong augmentations designed for SSL, such as RandAugment [9] and Mixup [10], are suitable for the time-frequency representation of speech, and which combination is optimal, has previously lacked a systematic answer.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

This paper aims to answer three interlinked questions. First, under the setting where devices are completely unlabeled and the server has only a small amount of labeled data, can a usable KWS model be trained—supporting both training from scratch (using the public Speech Commands dataset) and transferring a KWS model pre-trained on a large cloud labeled set to a new data domain (internal wakeword dataset). The practical significance of the latter is that launching new categories of wakewords does not require large-scale re-annotation. Second, under label-skewed heterogeneity (different keyword distributions across devices, with experiments covering three levels of heterogeneity: K=2, Dir(0.1), Dir(0.3)), how to prevent the pseudo-labeling mechanism from being amplified into systematic errors by the skewed distribution; a mechanistic solution is needed rather than leaving it to chance. Third, finding a strong augmentation combination truly suitable for mel-spectrograms for semi-supervised KWS training, which is the key variable determining whether SSL can replicate its success in CV for speech.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall scenario is shown in Figure 1: the server possesses expert models and a small amount of labeled data, while devices only have unlabeled data. The two sides collaborate through the downward broadcast and upward aggregation of model parameters. The core mechanism of semi-supervised learning is the dual-branch structure shown in Figure 2: unlabeled data is first passed through a weak augmentation into the KWS model to obtain predictions, which serve as pseudo-labels (the example in the figure is "YES"); the prediction of the same data after strong augmentation is then constrained to be consistent with the pseudo-label. In other words, the model "tests itself": using a view close to the real distribution to pose questions, and using a strongly perturbed view to answer.

The federated skeleton is the classic FedAvg-style averaging: there are M devices in total, and in round t, M_t active devices upload locally trained models θ_m^t. The server aggregates them into θ^t = (1/M_t) Σ_{m=1}^{M_t} θ_m^t (Section 3). In the experiments, 100 devices are fixed, with an active device ratio of C = 0.1 per round (Section 4.1), meaning approximately 10 devices participate in training per round.

On top of the aggregation skeleton, the key design of this paper is the alternate training main loop, rather than parallel training. The process is: the server first fine-tunes the aggregated model using labeled data with supervised loss, then sends the fine-tuned model to the active devices of this round; devices use the received model to generate pseudo-labels for local unlabeled data, train for several local epochs with unsupervised loss, and then upload; the server aggregates again, fine-tunes again, and enters the next round. Supervised and unsupervised updates alternate in time, connected by "downlink—uplink."

The design motivation comes directly from the findings of the same group's previous work, SemiFL (Reference [7], NeurIPS 2022): if the server and devices train separately and directly aggregate parameters, performance degrades significantly when devices perform multiple local epochs (citing [2][7] in Section 3). Alternate training separates these two tasks: the server's small chunk of IID labeled data performs a "supervised calibration" on the aggregated model every round, pulling the weight drift accumulated by Non-IID local updates back to the anchor point; meanwhile, the pseudo-label generator sent down is always the model just calibrated by supervised data, improving pseudo-label quality from the source. Figure 4 provides a schematic comparison of parallel and alternate training methods.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

Supervised loss (Equation 1): L_sup = ℓ(f_θ(A_w(x_l)), y_l). Here, f_θ is the parameterized KWS model, ℓ is cross-entropy, A_w is the weak augmentation function, and x_l, y_l are the server's labeled data. A notable detail is: supervised data on the server only applies weak augmentation. In the pseudo-label framework, weak augmentation serves as the "reliable prediction view." Since the true labels of supervised data are already reliable, strong perturbation is not needed.

Unsupervised loss (Equation 2): L_unsup = ℓ(f_θ(A_s(x_u)), y_u), where the pseudo-label y_u = f_θ(A_w(x_u)), and this sample is included in training only if max(y_u) ≥ τ. A_s is the strong augmentation function, and τ is the confidence threshold. This is essentially a transplant of the FixMatch consistency regularizer into the federated setting. Each of the three elements has its "why": First, pseudo-labels are generated by the weak augmentation view—weak augmentation is close to the real data distribution, making predictions more credible. Using a strongly perturbed view to pose questions and answer them would only introduce noise. Second, the loss is applied to the strong augmentation view—forcing the model to maintain prediction consistency under strong perturbation is equivalent to defining equivalence classes via augmentation, expanding each unlabeled data point into a pair of mutually constrained training signals, which is the main source of unsupervised signals. Third, the confidence threshold τ filters low-confidence samples—pseudo-label errors would self-reinforce through the self-training loop (confirmation bias); the threshold is the gate that keeps pseudo-label noise out of the gradients. The specific value of τ is not reported in the paper.

Pre-training transfer objective (Section 3): θ̂ = argmin_θ̃ ℓ(f_θ̃(A_w(x_l)), y_l) + ℓ(f_θ̃(A_s(x_u)), y_u). Classic SSL/SSFL assumes N_L ≪ N_U and trains from scratch; this paper points out that when a pre-trained model θ̃ trained on a large labeled set exists, SSFL can serve as a domain adaptation tool—on the basis of θ̃, fine-tuning is performed using the sum of "server weak-augmentation supervised + device strong-augmentation pseudo-label supervised," transferring the pre-trained KWS model to a new data domain. The wakeword experiments (Table 2) verify exactly this route. It should also be noted that the aggregation formula is a naive arithmetic mean, without weighting or personalization terms.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Alternate Training to Resolve Label-Skewed Heterogeneity

This is the most core contribution at the mechanism level of the entire paper, with evidence in Table 1. Under the most heterogeneous Non-IID K=2 split (each device holds data for only 2 keyword categories, constructed according to References [3][7]): parallel FL accuracy is only 68.2(1.7) (V1) and 77.9(2.4) (V2); switching to alternate training FL reaches 98.3(0.1) and 97.6(0.0), an absolute improvement of 30.1 and 19.7 percentage points—the original text (Section 4.2) describes this as "by 30% and 20% in Accuracy." SSFL with completely unlabeled devices on the same level of heterogeneity and ≈1% labeled data on the server achieves 88.6(0.3) (V1)/86.9(0.4) (V2), which is 20.4 and 9.0 percentage points higher than the parallel combination.

Why alternate training can solve Non-IID can be broken down into three levels. First, suppression of weight drift: under Non-IID, local multi-epoch training on devices causes local models to deviate severely from the global optimum (the source of drift pointed out in Reference [2]); directly averaging these drifted models yields a "mean of drifts"; in alternate training, the server inserts a supervised fine-tuning step on IID labeled data after aggregation and before downlinking every round, effectively pulling the model back to the supervised anchor point every round, so drift does not accumulate across rounds. Second, a positive cycle of pseudo-label generator quality: devices receive a model just calibrated by supervised data. The learning curves in Figure 3 show that after filtering by confidence threshold, pseudo-label accuracy (Threshold Accuracy) continues to rise with training, and the proportion of samples passing the threshold (Label Ratio) also increases—the latter means the overall entropy of model predictions is decreasing, pseudo-labels become more accurate with use, forming a positive feedback loop rather than a vicious cycle of confirmation bias. Third, avoiding conflict in averaging: the parallel scheme forces an average of supervised and unsupervised solutions in parameter space; when their optimization directions differ, the averaged solution may be neither. The alternate scheme lets the two updates act serially on the same model, stacking at the gradient level rather than mixing solutions.

The conclusion given by the paper (Section 4.2) is worth emphasizing separately: a small amount of IID labeled data on the server can effectively resolve the Non-IID data split problem under the FL setting. The implication for engineering practice is that for federated KWS, there is no need to demand labels on the device side; concentrating the annotation budget on a small chunk of server-side IID data, combined with alternate training, is sufficient.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Mel-Spectrogram Image-based Augmentation System and MixAugment

This paper treats mel-spectrograms as images (Section 3), thereby introducing the CV augmentation arsenal into speech SSL: RandAugment [9] is applied directly, and Mixup [10] mixes labeled and unlabeled data using random convex combinations (i.e., MixMatch-style MixAugment). The experimental setup is: weak augmentation A_w is fixed using BasicAugment (time shift, resampling, random background noise, from Rybakov et al. [5]), while strong augmentation A_s compares SpecAugment (time-frequency masking [4]), RandAugment, RandAugment(Selected) (a selected subset of image operators), MixAugment, and their combinations. This "weak augmentation poses questions, strong augmentation answers" pairing design stems from the ablation tradition of FixMatch. This paper completes the systematic comparison on KWS for the first time (Table 3), with conclusions in the ablation section: speech-native SpecAugment is significantly superior to image-transferred RandAugment, MixAugment is the only component that can beneficially stack with SpecAugment, and their combination achieves the best overall performance.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: SSFL for Pre-trained Model Transfer (Domain Adaptation)

This is the second usage of SSFL beyond "saving annotations." When a KWS model trained on a large labeled set already exists on the cloud, the conventional approach to entering a new deployment domain (new wakewords, new acoustic environments) is to re-annotate a large amount of new domain data; the alternative solution proposed by this paper is to use 2500 labeled samples on the server plus massive unlabeled data on devices for SSFL fine-tuning. The numbers in Table 2 (internal wakeword dataset, server ≈3% supervised data, lower Relative FRR@FAR is better): Fully Supervised 0.80, Partially Supervised 0.90, Semi-Supervised 0.81, Parallel FL under highly imbalanced device data 0.85, Alternate FL 0.81, Alternate SSFL 0.85. The relative metric uses the pre-trained baseline model as 1.0 (definition in Section 4.1: ratio of test model FRR to baseline model FRR, fixed at the baseline FAR operating point). Section 4.2 summarizes: 2500 labeled wakeword streams improve performance relative to the pre-trained model and Partially Supervised by approximately 0.2 and 0.1, respectively (checking against Table 2 numbers: 1.0 → 0.81 improves by 0.19, 0.90 → 0.81 improves by 0.09, consistent with the text). That is, without large-scale re-annotation, Relative FRR@FAR can be pulled from 1.0 to the range of 0.81–0.85.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to Leroy et al. [8] (FL for KWS): [8] assumes devices have labeled data, whereas this paper has zero labels on devices, with all labels concentrated on the server, fitting real privacy and annotation cost constraints. Compared to FixMatch [6] (centralized SSL): the loss forms are同源 (weak-augmentation pseudo-label + strong-augmentation consistency + confidence threshold), but the training topology is distributed from single-machine, data does not leave devices, and this paradigm is verified on KWS and mel-spectrogram representations for the first time. Compared to the same group's previous work SemiFL [7]: the alternate training mechanism is directly inherited from [7]; the incremental contributions of this paper are threefold—task domain transferred to KWS and mel-spectrograms, systematic strong augmentation ablation (Table 3), and the addition of a pre-training transfer setting and verification on an industrial-grade internal wakeword dataset (Table 2). Compared to supervised data augmentation works [4][5], the role of augmentation has fundamentally changed: in supervised training, augmentation is merely a regularization spice; in this paper's SSL/SSFL, the difference between weak/strong augmentation views directly defines the unsupervised supervision signal itself, making augmentation selection a prerequisite for the method's validity rather than just icing on the cake. Essentially, this is a research paper that empirically implements and selects methods for SSFL mechanisms verified in CV within the speech KWS domain; its value lies mainly in empirical conclusions rather than brand-new algorithms.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

The public benchmarks use Speech Commands V1 and V2 [11] (Section 4.1): 12 classes, including 10 words ('yes', 'no', 'up', 'down', 'left', 'right', 'on', 'off', 'stop', 'go') plus 'silence' and 'unknown' classes; silence is randomly sampled from background noise, and unknown is composed of equal amounts of other keywords from the dataset. The total sample size of the dataset is not reported in the paper; the conversion relationship given in Table 1 is 250 labeled samples ≈1%, 2500 samples ≈10%. The backbone model is TC-ResNet18 [12] (Time-Convolutional Residual Network, a lightweight architecture designed for real-time mobile KWS); its parameter count and computational cost are not reported in the paper.

Federated simulation setting (Section 4.1): 100 devices, active ratio per round C = 0.1; IID split distributes data equally; balanced Non-IID adopts the method from References [3][7], setting K = 2 to simulate label skew (each device has only 2 classes); unbalanced Non-IID samples from Dirichlet distribution Dir(α), with Table 1 covering α = 0.1 and 0.3; each setting runs with 4 different random seeds, standard deviations are in parentheses in the tables, and error bars are shown in the figures.

Internal wakeword dataset (Section 4.1, de-identified): training set approx. 14K hours, test set approx. 1.5K hours, used for wakeword detection—a binary classification task where the on-device model recognizes a specified keyword to trigger device activation (outputting "wakeword detected/no wakeword"). Audio representation is mel-spectrogram, and the model is a CNN with multiple convolutional layers plus max pooling; the specific structure is not reported in the paper. Evaluation uses Relative FRR@FAR, with 4-seed standard deviation within 0.01.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Speech Commands experiments use Accuracy (Table 1). Rationale: 12-class relatively balanced classification task; accuracy is the common metric for this public benchmark, facilitating horizontal comparison with existing works like TC-ResNet [12], Rybakov [5], etc.

Wakeword experiments use Relative FRR@FAR (definition given in Section 4.1): under the premise of fixing the baseline model's FAR, the ratio of the test model's FRR to the baseline model's FRR, where less than 1 is better. There are three layers of reasons for choosing this metric, all stemming from task characteristics: First, wakeword detection is an extremely imbalanced binary detection task, with positive samples being extremely rare; accuracy would be drowned out by the massive number of negative samples and lose discriminative power. Second, the true cost of a detector is determined by the operating point (trade-off between FAR and FRR); fixing FAR rather than FRR is the product perspective—the tolerance for false wake-up rate is defined by the product, and different models should not be allowed to pick favorable operating points. Third, using relative values rather than absolute FRR eliminates the impact of absolute FAR level drift between different experiments and directly answers the core question of the transfer setting "how much better than the pre-trained baseline"—the baseline itself is 1.0, making all numbers instantly readable.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

Strong augmentation ablation (Table 3, N_L = 250, Accuracy, format V1/V2; weak augmentation A_w uniformly BasicAugment):

| Strong Augmentation A_s | Speech Commands V1 | Speech Commands V2 |
|---|---|---|
| Weak Only (BasicAug) | 86.1(0.6) | 85.2(0.6) |
| BasicAug + RandAug | 90.4(0.2) | 89.1(0.2) |
| BasicAug + RandAug (Selected) | 89.8(0.8) | 89.9(0.3) |
| BasicAug + SpecAug | 95.2(0.3) | 95.8(0.2) |
| BasicAug + SpecAug + RandAug (Selected) | 94.4(0.3) | 94.0(0.1) |
| BasicAug + SpecAug + MixAug | 95.7(0.2) | 96.5(0.1) |

Four findings and their "whys." First, strong augmentation itself is effective: any strong augmentation is better than using only weak augmentation, with improvements ranging from 3.9 to 10.6 percentage points—the perturbation intensity of consistency training directly determines the quality of unsupervised signals, reproducing the core experience of CV SSL. Second, speech-native augmentation outperforms image-transferred augmentation: SpecAugment (95.2/95.8) is approximately 5–7 percentage points higher than RandAugment (90.4/89.1) and its selected version (89.8/89.9); the original text explicitly states "image-based augmentation methods do not outperform SpecAugment." The reason can be understood from the physical meaning of the representation: SpecAugment's time/frequency masking is an "remove information" operation, forcing the model to infer masked content from context; the perturbation method complements the time-frequency structure of speech and naturally preserves label semantics. RandAugment's image operators (contrast, sharpening, geometric transformations, etc.) applied to mel-spectrograms destroy the physical meaning of energy distribution—mel-spectrograms are not natural images; "pixel" neighborhoods lack visual semantics, so perturbations are large but do not produce equivalence classes that preserve labels, causing the consistency constraint between pseudo-labels and strong views to distort. Third, blind stacking is harmful: SpecAug + RandAug(Selected) (94.4/94.0) is actually lower than SpecAug alone (95.2/95.8); stacking two strong augmentations may make the task too difficult, exceeding the effective interval of consistency training. Fourth, MixAug is the only beneficial stack: SpecAug + MixAug achieves 95.7/96.5, the best overall, increasing by 0.5/0.7 percentage points over SpecAug alone; the original text states "MixAugment can be combined with SpecAugment to further improve performance." The reason is that Mixup mixes labeled and unlabeled samples using random convex combinations, equivalent to linear interpolation regularization in the input space, smoothing decision boundaries; its perturbation mode is orthogonal to SpecAugment's "remove information," allowing stacking without mutual destruction. This ablation provides directly reusable engineering conclusions for subsequent speech SSL work: use traditional signal-level perturbations for weak augmentation, use SpecAugment as the main component and Mixup as a supplement for strong augmentation, and do not introduce image augmentation operators.

## Main Contributions

First, the first systematic verification of the effectiveness of SSL, FL, and SSFL paradigms on KWS (dual declaration in the abstract and Section 1): centralized 1% labeled data plus unlabeled data pulls accuracy from 62.4/60.2 to 95.7/96.5 (Table 1); under the most heterogeneous K=2 split on the federated side, device-unlabeled SSFL reaches 88.6/86.9, far exceeding the parallel combination's 68.2/77.9.

Second, introducing the alternate training mechanism into KWS federated training and empirically demonstrating its Non-IID mitigation capability: under K=2, alternate FL is 30.1/19.7 percentage points higher than parallel FL (Table 1, Section 4.2), and a transferable judgment is distilled—server-side small amounts of IID labeled data combined with alternate training can effectively resolve the Non-IID problem in FL, and annotation budgets should be concentrated on the server side rather than demanding labels on the device side.

Third, providing strong augmentation selection conclusions for KWS semi-supervised training (Table 3): SpecAugment is the most effective strong augmentation, MixAugment can be stacked for further improvement, image-domain augmentation is not advantageous, and blind stacking is harmful. This is the first SSL augmentation systematic ablation completed on KWS/mel-spectrograms.

Fourth, expanding the usage of SSFL as a domain adaptation tool (Table 2): on the internal wakeword dataset (14K/1.5K hours), 2500 server-side labeled samples plus device unlabeled data can improve the pre-trained model's Relative FRR@FAR from baseline 1.0 to 0.85 (SSFL), with centralized SSL and alternate FL reaching 0.81—launching new domains does not require large-scale re-annotation.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

There is a structural gap between SSFL and "devices with labels": in Table 1, SSFL is lower than alternate FL in all scenarios (IID 95.3 vs 99.5, K=2 88.6 vs 98.3); in Table 2, SSFL's 0.85 does not beat alternate FL's 0.81. The source of the cost is pseudo-label noise plus supervised signals needing an extra round of "downlink—pseudo-label—uplink" propagation path; the paper does not quantify the convergence behavior of this gap over communication rounds.

Pseudo-label confirmation bias relies on a single threshold defense: τ is the only quality gate, and its value is not reported in the paper; under K=2 label skew (each device has data for only 2 classes), device-side pseudo-labels will inevitably systematically bias towards these 2 classes; the paper performs neither class-level pseudo-label quality analysis nor class-balanced or class-aware aggregation processing. Furthermore, aggregation is a naive arithmetic mean, without weighting, personalization, or heterogeneous computing power adaptation—the capabilities of the same group's HeteroFL [3] are not combined. Privacy guarantees remain at the implicit level of "data does not leave devices," without differential privacy or secure aggregation, and resistance to attacks such as membership inference is not discussed. Finally, the computational and energy overhead of executing strong augmentations like RandAugment/Mixup on devices is not reported, and KWS devices are often low-power MCUs/DSPs, raising doubts about edge-side feasibility.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

Large areas of hyperparameters and training details are missing: threshold τ, number of communication rounds, number of local epochs, learning rate, optimizer, batch size, and mel-spectrogram parameters (frame length, frame shift, number of filter banks) are not reported, making independent reproduction difficult. The wakeword dataset is internal de-identified data, the CNN structure is not given, and the absolute FAR/FRR operating points of the baseline models are not reported, limiting the external verifiability of Table 2.

There are ambiguities and inconsistencies in table presentation: the FL rows in Table 1 have only one value per dataset, spanning the two supervision ratio columns, and the setting is not explicitly stated; its meaning can only be inferred by the fact that under IID, 99.5 approaches the centralized upper bound of 99.7; the text in Section 4.2 "approximately 40% improvement" does not match the numbers in Table 1 (absolute improvement of 33.3/36.3 percentage points with 250 samples), and the dimension is unclear; the standard deviation for Parallel FL under Dir(0.1) in V2 is unclear in print. The ablation only covers one level of N_L = 250 (≈1%) (Table 3); it is unknown whether strong augmentation conclusions change with labeling ratio; the backbone network is only TC-ResNet18, and the robustness of conclusions to other architectures is not verified. Direct numerical comparison with external methods is missing—comparison objects are all in-house baselines (Fully/Partially Supervised, own parallel variants), with no horizontal numbers against Leroy 2019 or other SSFL methods. Figure 3 learning curves only provide qualitative descriptions, and pseudo-label accuracy and proportions are not listed numerically.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Mechanism upgrades for label skew are the most direct next step: class-aware aggregation or server-side class-balanced constraints to suppress class bias in pseudo-labels under K=2 scenarios; dynamic threshold scheduling (adaptively tuning τ based on pseudo-label quality) to replace fixed thresholds. The combination space at the system level is also large: combining with model compression, heterogeneous federated learning (such as the same group's HeteroFL [3]) to cover the real edge-side computing power spectrum; introducing differential privacy and secure aggregation to upgrade "data does not leave devices" to provable privacy guarantees. At the augmentation level, speech-native learnable strong augmentations (searching in SpecAugment parameter space or differentiable augmentation) may be superior to continuing to transplant image operators—Table 3 already shows that image augmentation is not advantageous; finding equivalence class definitions within the speech domain is the correct path. At the task level, extending to streaming/online KWS, larger vocabularies, multi-lingual scenarios, and verifying communication and energy overhead on real devices rather than 100 simulated clients are necessary steps from empirical research to deployable systems.
