# Online Continual Learning in Keyword Spotting for Low-Resource Devices via Pooling High-Order Temporal Statistics

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Umberto Michieli, Pablo Peso Parada, Mete Ozay (Samsung Research UK)
- **Date**: July 2023 (arXiv v1 submitted on 2023-07-24, Interspeech format)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.12660 (Data split open source: github.com/umbertomichieli/TAP-SLDA)
- **Keywords**: online continual learning, keyword spotting, temporal aware pooling, streaming LDA, high-order statistics, embedded edge devices, catastrophic forgetting

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Keyword Spotting (KWS) serves as the voice entry point for virtual assistants in smart speakers, earbuds, and smartphones: it detects wake words or command words within a continuous audio stream to activate the assistant or execute instructions. Deep neural networks have been widely deployed on these user devices, achieving excellent performance on standard benchmarks. However, a practical requirement has long lacked an engineering solution: users expect devices to learn newly defined custom words. Under traditional paradigms, adding a new word implies re-collecting data, re-training, and re-deploying the model. While this workflow is tolerable in the cloud, it is neither realistic nor necessary on edge devices.

Continual Learning (CL) is designed precisely for scenarios where "the label space is unknown a priori and the model needs incremental adaptation." Its core difficulty is catastrophic forgetting: learning new classes destroys the discriminative boundaries of old classes. CL holds special value for edge devices due to privacy considerations. Many CL methods require replaying old data to combat forgetting, but speech is typical sensitive personal data. Storing such data on device storage poses compliance and security risks; thus, "not storing samples" is not just about saving storage but is a hard privacy constraint.

However, most CL methods conflict directly with the resource profile of edge devices: regularization methods based on the Fisher Information Matrix (e.g., EWC) require additional second-order statistical computations; replay-based methods require maintaining sample buffers; knowledge distillation-based methods require maintaining an additional teacher model. These overheads are unavailable on resource-constrained devices (supported by Pellegrini et al.'s research on real-time training on edge devices). Furthermore, the standard CL training paradigm involves multiple iterations with large batches, which contradicts the demand for rapid adaptation where "one data point arrives, is used, discarded, and takes effect immediately."

The paper tightens these constraints step-by-step to define a stricter problem setting: first, using single-epoch training (one epoch, i.e., Online CL, OCL) to avoid storing current task samples; second, using small batches with a frozen backbone network (i.e., OCL for Embedded systems, EOCL) to satisfy the rapid adaptation needs of low-resource devices. The final focused scenario is **Example-Free EOCL KWS**: the KWS model receives samples one by one from a non-repeating data stream with a batch size of 1, performing only partial updates. Section 1 explicitly states that the experiments in Section 4 adopt the hardest setting—each task contains only one class, meaning classes arrive sequentially, and once a class is learned, it is never seen again until testing.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper reviews three existing lines of work and points out their misalignment with the EOCL setting one by one:

- **Non-online CL on KWS**: Rainbow Keywords (Interspeech 2022) combines data augmentation, replay, and knowledge distillation for incremental keyword learning, but it uses large-batch training and pre-trains the model on half of the keywords. Another online continual learning work in ASR uses "online" in a different sense than the current literature, actually running for 10 epochs. Another class of progressive CL schemes trains different sub-networks for different keyword sets and uses shared memory to store learned features for knowledge transfer, but it assumes the task index is known at inference time to select the sub-network—this does not hold in real KWS deployments, where wake word detectors face open audio streams without task labels.
- **Standard Regularization-based CL (EWC, SI) is generally unavailable**: The paper lists four reasons: they require large-batch training; they require storing samples to run many epochs (infeasible for edge devices processing sensitive data); they require updating the entire feature extractor with a large number of trainable parameters; and they require one or more additional classification models for parameter regularization (e.g., distillation). The literature review indicates that these methods perform poorly in the EOCL scenario, so they are directly excluded from experimental comparison.
- **Two routes of Visual OCL have not been transferred to KWS**: OCL has been systematically studied in image classification, mainly divided into memory-based and class-prototype-based routes, but EOCL for KWS was a literature gap before this paper.

In summary, the shortcomings of existing methods can be归结 to a set of constraints that cannot be satisfied simultaneously: either they require storing data (privacy, storage), or they require large computation (regularization terms, multiple iterations), or they require extra information at inference time (task index), or they require updating large models (computing power). EOCL requires avoiding all four simultaneously.

### Key Challenges to be Solved by This Paper

The core is a triangular contradiction: **Resource Constraints (no sample storage, no gradient calculation, no backbone updates) × Rapid Adaptation (single sample, single pass, immediate effect) × Anti-Forgetting (accuracy for both new and old words)**. Under these triple constraints, the adjustable design space is compressed to an extremely narrow range: after the backbone network is frozen, the only thing that can be modified is "how to compress the temporal feature matrix output by the backbone into a classifier input vector" (i.e., pooling) and "how the classifier models online." The key question the paper aims to answer is: under the setting of batch=1, epoch=1, and no memory, can we design a representation enhancement method with almost zero parameter overhead, allowing a simple online classifier to maintain plasticity (fast learning of new words) and stability (not forgetting old words)?

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The method (Figure 1 in the paper) consists of three parts: Feature Extractor G(·), Pooling Mechanism P(·), and Classifier F(·). The training objective is to incrementally train F(P(G(x))) to output predictions in a supervised OCL manner over K sequential tasks. The trade-offs for the three components each have a clear "why":

- **G(·) Frozen Pre-trained Backbone**. The experiments use six speech recognition backbones: Wav2Vec2-B/L, HuBERT-B/L/XL, Emformer-B, all pre-trained on LibriSpeech via the torchaudio pipeline. The motivation for freezing is twofold: computationally, edge devices cannot afford multi-epoch gradient updates for large models; information-theoretically, self-supervised pre-training has already learned general speech representations, so recognizing a new word does not require re-learning features, but only establishing a statistical model of the class in the new feature space. The value of pre-training is quantified in ablation studies (Table 5): on W2V-B, SLDA increases from 42.3 without pre-training to 82.4, indicating that the premise of this route is indeed high-quality frozen features.
- **P(·) is the only representation degree of freedom**. The backbone output is $g = G(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times d}$ (t time frames × d-dimensional features), while the classifier consumes fixed-length vectors. The pooling layer determines which information along the time dimension is retained. Traditional average pooling directly averages the time dimension, equivalent to assuming "the word identity information is in the mean of frame-level features." However, the discriminative information of words actually lies in the evolution of features over time (the dynamics of phoneme sequences). Since the backbone cannot be trained, the paper focuses all attention on "making the pooling itself carry richer temporal statistical information without introducing trainable parameters"—this is the niche of TAP.
- **F(·) uses SLDA (Streaming Linear Discriminant Analysis)**. Each class maintains a streaming-updated mean vector (class prototype), and all classes share a covariance matrix, updated according to the online estimation method of multivariate Gaussian in the literature; at inference time, samples are assigned to the nearest Gaussian class model. The reason for choosing it is stated plainly by the paper: the shared covariance matrix can characterize the common relationships between the temporal statistics of different words, which have transfer value for recognizing unseen new words; meanwhile, the memory overhead is only "number of classes × d prototypes + one shared covariance of $d^2$," and the update is a closed-form statistical accumulation, requiring no gradients.

### Mathematical Principles of the Core Algorithm

Definition of TAP (Equation 1 in the paper):

$$P(\mathbf{g}) = \left[ \boldsymbol{\mu},\ \left(\mathbb{E}_G[(\mathbf{g}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^2]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\ \left(\mathbb{E}_G\left[\left(\frac{(\mathbf{g}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^2}{\|\mathbb{E}_G[(\mathbf{g}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^2]\|^{1/2}}\right)^{r}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{r}} \right]_{r=3}^{R}$$

Translated into plain language item by item: The first term $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is the first-order moment, i.e., the mean of each feature dimension over time (which is the output of average pooling); the second term is the square root of the second-order central moment, i.e., the standard deviation along the time dimension, measuring the amplitude of frame-to-frame fluctuations for each feature dimension; each term from $r=3$ to $R$ first normalizes the squared centered deviation by the standard deviation, then takes the $r$-th moment and the $r$-th root—it measures the "shape" of the time distribution of each feature dimension: how extreme the heavy tails and deviations from the mean are. All expectations are taken over the time dimension within a single word. Finally, $P(g) \in \mathbb{R}^{R \cdot d}$, concatenating the first $R$ moments along the feature dimension, where this paper takes $R=5$.

Two mathematical details are worth expanding upon because they directly determine whether the method is usable:

- **Why use central moments instead of raw moments**: The comparative Lp-norm pooling calculates the p-th non-central moment, where first-order information (mean) leaks into every order, leading to high redundancy between orders. TAP places the mean separately in the first term, and the remaining orders are calculated after centering, measuring the distribution shape rather than position, resulting in better orthogonality of information between orders.
- **Why perform standard deviation normalization**: The numerical scale of higher-order moments expands by the $2r$-th power of the deviation with increasing order. Without normalization, the numerical value of the fifth order would completely overwhelm the first and second orders in the concatenated vector. Dividing by the square root of the second-order moment brings each order back to a comparable dimension. This point is particularly critical for SLDA—the covariance matrix is estimated directly on the concatenated vector. Scale imbalance would cause one order to dominate the covariance structure, eliminating the cross-class transfer value of the shared covariance.

Mathematics of SLDA: The class-conditional distribution is modeled as Gaussian, with all classes sharing the covariance matrix $\Sigma$. The discriminant function thus degenerates into a linear form (which is the origin of the name LDA). Inference only requires calculating the linear discriminant score for each class and taking the argmax; on the training side, a streaming algorithm updates the class mean and shared covariance online. For each incoming sample, a constant-time accumulation is performed, with no learning rate and no backpropagation. The only "hyperparameter" in the entire system is essentially the order $R$.

The intuitive evidence supporting this design is Figure 2 in the paper: for two different classes $c_1 \neq c_2$, the distributions of the first-order moments overlap significantly, while the distributions of higher-order moments are clearly separated. The quantitative analysis in Section 4 confirms this using the average pairwise Wasserstein distance between classes: the distance for the first-order moment is only $0.09 \pm 0.02$, while the average distance for the second to fifth-order moments is $0.22 \pm 0.07$—classes are entangled in the mean space but separable in the higher-order moment space. This is the most core empirical finding of the paper and the foundation for the term "high-order."

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Temporal Aware Pooling (TAP)

The essence of TAP is to redefine "pooling" from a compression operator to a "sufficient statistic extractor for time distributions." Traditional pooling (AVG, MAX) answers "what is the average/peak of the features in this audio segment," while TAP answers "what does the distribution of each feature dimension look like over time in this audio segment." The innovation does not lie in the moments themselves—higher-order statistics have precedents in speaker verification: some studies point out that third and fourth-order moments are useless when used alone, and other works use $R=4$ for auxiliary tasks. The innovation lies in **concatenating the first five central moments directly as input features for the online classifier** and validating its effectiveness under the extreme sample-scarce setting of EOCL (one pass per class, one sample at a time). Its additional cost is close to zero (Table 4: relative to backbone parameters, it only increases by 0.12%, and training/inference time increases by an average of 2.1%/2.6%), because the calculation of moments is pure forward statistics, with no trainable parameters.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: The First KWS Solution for the EOCL Setting, TAP-SLDA

The second innovation is the problem setting and system combination itself: the paper explicitly proposes and formalizes EOCL for KWS (single sample, single epoch, frozen backbone, zero example memory) and provides the combination of TAP + SLDA as the first solution. The logic of this combination is synergistic: the enriched feature space produced by TAP requires a classifier that can utilize its statistical structure, and SLDA's shared covariance exactly models "the common relationships between the temporal statistics of different words." The two mesh at the level of "statistics." One piece of evidence is that the backward transfer (BwT) of TAP-SLDA in Table 1 is even higher than the final accuracy (e.g., 91.8 vs. 89.9 on W2V-B): learning later classes makes the shared covariance estimation more accurate, which in turn improves the discriminative boundaries of earlier classes—this is a unique positive backtracking effect of generative statistical modeling, which gradient fine-tuning methods cannot exhibit (inferred by this paper).

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Differences from predecessors in KWS incremental learning: does not require task index at inference time (friendly to open audio streams); does not require pre-training the classification head on half of the keywords; does not require replay buffers. Differences from EWC/SI: does not update the backbone, does not calculate Fisher information, does not require a teacher network, shifting "anti-forgetting" from parameter-space regularization to feature-space statistical modeling. Differences from iCaRL: zero example memory (double benefit of privacy and storage), and batch size does not double (iCaRL requires one replay sample for each new sample). Differences from SQDA/SNB: all classes share one covariance matrix, rather than one full covariance per class (SQDA) or one diagonal covariance per class (SNB), improving sample efficiency by an order of magnitude. Differences from pooling predecessors: TSTP only concatenates first and second-order moments, TSDP only uses second-order moments, iSQRT-COV only performs second-order covariance pooling, Lp-norm uses non-central moments—TAP pushes the moment order to five and performs centering and scale normalization. Differences from visual OCL prototype methods: NCM compares means using Euclidean distance, while SLDA uses Mahalanobis distance whitened by shared covariance, and the temporal statistics provided by TAP make the "mean" itself more informative.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

Two datasets. **GSC v2** (Google Speech Commands v2): approximately 100,000 English speech clips, 35 words, with unbalanced sample counts per word, ranging from 1.5k to 4k; according to the paper's setting, each task contains samples of only one class (i.e., 35 sequential tasks), and the dev and test sets are filtered to contain only words seen during training. **MSWC** (Multilingual Spoken Words Corpus): 23.4 million spoken words, 50 languages, over 340,000 different words; the paper selects the most representative 5 languages (the text lists en, fr, ca, rw, while Figure 3 actually shows en, de, fr, ca, rw, indicating a minor discrepancy between the text and the figure), constructing 3 micro-sets for each language with word counts N of 25, 50, and 100 respectively, randomly sampling 1k samples per word. The default evaluation setting is a class-ordered stream with independent and identically distributed (IID) samples within classes (referred to as class-IID in the paper: samples are sorted by class, shuffled within classes). All results are averaged over 5 different class orders; standard deviations are not always displayed to ensure readability, and offline upper bounds are not displayed because the F(·) structures of different methods are different, leaving no common upper bound to mark.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

Four metrics characterize different aspects of continual learning, all calculated on the test set after training: **Acc** (final accuracy, higher is better) measures the final performance on all classes; **BwT** (backward transfer, higher is better) tracks the impact of learning new tasks on the performance of previous tasks; **Forg** (forgetting, lower is better) calculates the average difference between the accuracy of each class at the last step and its historical best accuracy; **Pla** (plasticity, higher is better) is the average accuracy of each class immediately after it is learned. The rationale for selection is clear: the final accuracy of a continual learning method is a compromise between plasticity and stability. Looking only at Acc would mix together two types of failures: "learns fast but forgets completely" and "remembers well but cannot learn." They must be examined separately. The paper also defines a relative gain conversion: $(Acc_2 - Acc_1)/(100 - Acc_1)$, i.e., the gain relative to the "available improvement space." When reading Section 4, pay special attention to the口径: the relative numbers in the text, such as 37.8%, 35.0%, 29.3%, etc., conform to this conversion formula (e.g., for TAP-SLDA average 85.5 vs. SLDA 76.7, $(85.5-76.7)/(100-76.7) = 37.8\%$), while the 11.3%, 9.9% given in the abstract and introduction are another common relative ratio口径, and the two cannot be directly compared.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The main comparison is in Table 1: a complete matrix of 10 OCL methods × 6 backbones on GSC (9 columns in the table, CBCL is merged because its results are identical to NCM under default parameters). Several layers of findings:

- **Gradient fine-tuning methods completely collapse under EOCL**. The final accuracy of FT and PRCP is only $2.5 \pm 1.0$ (the random guessing level for 35 classes is approximately 2.9%, meaning they effectively degenerate into blind guessing), plasticity 100, forgetting 98.5—perfect plasticity, zero memory, a textbook demonstration of catastrophic forgetting. This confirms the analysis in the problem statement section: without replay and regularization, single-pass gradient training learns only "the last word."
- **Gradient differentiation in centroid and statistical methods**. SNB (diagonal covariance) and SOvR achieve moderate results on HuBERT-B/XL relying on centroid inference (SNB achieves 77.4 on HB-B), but collapse on W2V and Emf-B (SNB is only 3.7 on W2V-B, SOvR drops as low as 1.8), indicating that the diagonal covariance assumption heavily depends on the geometry of the feature space. NCM/CBCL are comprehensively in the middle (HB-B 83.9). SLDA is the strongest baseline (HB-B 94.2), with the gain of shared covariance relative to Euclidean distance prototypes clearly visible. SQDA has one covariance per class, with the lowest forgetting (5.7 on W2V-B) but worse plasticity than SLDA (81.2 vs. 87.0) and larger variance (67.4±4.4 on HB-L)—the paper's diagnosis is that storing one covariance per class not only consumes storage but also makes statistics unreliable when data is scarce. This comparison directly argues that "sample efficiency" is the first constraint of EOCL.
- **TAP-SLDA is comprehensively optimal**. First place on all six backbones, average accuracy 85.5 vs. SLDA's 76.7 (relative improvement of 37.8% by available improvement space); reaches $95.7 \pm 0.0$ on HuBERT-B, on which the paper claims a new SOTA for EOCL KWS. Moreover, the standard deviation is $\pm 0.0$ for five of the six backbones (only Emf-B $\pm 0.3$), showing extreme stability to class order, whereas SQDA's standard deviation ranges from 2.5 to 4.4. In terms of metric structure, it improves both plasticity and forgetting (W2V-B: Pla 93.7 vs. SLDA 87.0, Forg 5.6 vs. 8.0), not trading one for the other.
- **Memory replay is not a free lunch**. iCaRL achieves an average score of 72.3 (HB-B 93.7) using a 1k buffer, which is competitive, but the paper lists three reasons why it loses: batch size doubles (new sample paired with replay sample), storing sensitive speech data raises privacy concerns, and final accuracy is still significantly surpassed by the memory-free TAP-SLDA (85.5 vs. 72.3).

**Generalizability of TAP** (Table 2): Adding TAP pooling to every OCL method, 7 exceptions out of 54 combinations, almost universally improving results; the largest single-point gain appears in SOvR + W2V-L: increasing from 1.8 to 60.9, a jump of about 60 percentage points. SLDA + TAP increases by an average of 8.8 points. This shows that TAP is not a technique bound to SLDA, but a universal feature enrichment—any classifier based on prototypes or distance inference can benefit from it, because it pulls entangled classes apart at the feature space level (corresponding to the quantitative analysis in Section 1: first-order moment 0.09 vs. higher-order moments 0.22).

**Cross-lingual Personalization** (Figure 3): Using the HB-B backbone pre-trained only on unlabeled English data, online adaptation to words in other languages. FT's average accuracy collapses across the board to between 1.4 and 1.7; TAP-SLDA exceeds the SLDA baseline in every language and every class count setting (25/50/100 classes), with an average relative gain of 12.8%, and 12.6% in the hardest 100-class scenario. The paper simultaneously acknowledges that the overall accuracy is lower than on GSC (MSWC is harder) and points out that multi-lingual pre-training can further improve it (citing first-line work like UniSpeech), but even with a single-language pre-trained backbone, the method remains stable and effective under domain shift—an important property for real-world deployed KWS systems.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

**Comparison of Pooling Strategies** (Table 3, fixed SLDA): AVG 76.7, MAX 81.2, MIX 80.4, STOCH 68.4, L2 72.2, L3 71.4, RAP(10%) 81.0, AVGMAX 83.8, iSQRT-COV 80.4, TSDP 78.9, TSTP 82.0, **TAP 85.5** (all are averages over six backbones). Several informative secondary conclusions: random pooling and non-central moments (STOCH, L2/L3) are worse than AVG, indicating that "introducing non-linear sampling without centering" yields no benefit; MAX is stronger than AVG, indicating that peaks in time carry discriminative information; combining variance with mean (TSTP) is better than using variance alone (TSDP), and also better than using AVG alone—this progressive chain exactly paves the way for TAP's argument: adding one more meaningful central statistic to the pooling raises the accuracy by another level. TAP is first on all backbones except Emf-B (on Emf-B it ranks second with 50.8, losing to the second-order covariance pooling iSQRT-COV's 55.1). By the available improvement space口径, TAP improves by 37.8% relative to AVG, 10.5% relative to AVGMAX, and 19.4% relative to TSTP.

**Order R and Overhead** (Table 4, metrics are averages over six backbones): The accuracies for R=1 to R=6 on SLDA are 76.7, 82.0, 84.4, 85.2, 85.5, 84.9 respectively—R=5 is exactly the peak for all three methods (FT, NCM, SLDA), and it starts to drop at R=6. In terms of parameter increase, TAP-SLDA increases only 0.12% parameters relative to the backbone (SLDA is 0.10%, a difference of 0.02 percentage points), and training and inference time increase by an average of only 2.1% and 2.6%. The asymmetry between accuracy and overhead is extremely large, which is the fundamental reason it is suitable for edge devices.

**Contribution of Pre-training** (Table 5, rows for W2V-B/W2V-L): Without pre-training (random initialization), SLDA on W2V-B is only 42.3, TAP-SLDA is 47.2; with pre-training, they are 82.4 and 89.9 respectively; NCM increases from 13.0 to 67.5. Two conclusions: first, the entire route relies heavily on self-supervised pre-training, and the value of the frozen backbone comes from the quality of pre-training; second, even without pre-training, TAP alone still improves accuracy (42.3 to 47.2, NCM 13.0 to 20.2)—the scarcer the data, the greater the value of rich statistics, because the information of each sample is utilized more fully.

**Sensitivity to Stream Shape** (Table 6, IID setting without class order): When samples arrive completely randomly, FT surges from 2.5 to 44.6 (the same class appears repeatedly throughout training, equivalent to implicit replay, naturally alleviating forgetting), and TAP-FT improves by an average of 29.3% relative to FT (same口径 as before). However, prototype and covariance-based methods remain almost unchanged: NCM is identical to class-IID (difference 0), SLDA differs by only 0.1, TAP-SLDA differs by 0—class statistics are sufficient statistics independent of order, immune to the non-stationary structure of the data stream. This is the structural advantage of such methods in continual learning (summarized by this paper). Even though forgetting is alleviated, FT's 44.6 to 86.8 is still comprehensively lower than TAP-SLDA's 90.0 to 95.7, indicating that the sample efficiency of single-pass gradient training is far lower than statistical modeling. There are individual exceptions: on Emf-B, TAP-FT (10.4) is lower than FT (13.9), and on HB-L, TAP-NCM (44.5) is lower than NCM (46.7).

**"Is it just about having large dimensions?"** (Table 7): The paper uses the feature dimension multiplication factor $\Delta_{fs}$ for comparison. RAP takes top-5%/10%/20% features, expanding dimensions by 26.8/53.5/107 times, with accuracies of 80.3/81.0/80.5, showing almost no gain; MAXW2/5/10 (5/11/21 times) around MAX can only match MAX; extreme FLAT flattens all features (535 times), achieving only 78.2, which is lower than TAP. TAP's R=2 to R=6 only expands dimensions by 2 to 6 times, achieving 82.0 to 85.5. The conclusion is clear: the gain comes from the "quality" of the statistics, not the "quantity" of dimensions. Brute-force dimension expansion cannot replace explicit modeling of temporal dynamics.

## Main Contributions

1. **Pioneering the Problem Setting**: Formalized and solved for the first time the KWS task for Embedded Online Continual Learning (EOCL)—single-sample batch, single-epoch training, frozen backbone, zero example memory, aligning with the triple constraints of storage, computing power, and privacy of edge devices; the paper explicitly points out that the literature was blank regarding this combination of settings.
2. **TAP Pooling**: Proposed Temporal Aware Pooling, concatenating the first five central moments (including standard deviation normalization) to form enriched temporal statistical features, with no trainable parameters, parameter overhead of 0.12%, and time overhead of the order of 2% (Table 4), providing interpretability evidence using pairwise Wasserstein distance between classes (first-order moment 0.09 vs. higher-order moments 0.22).
3. **TAP-SLDA System**: Combined TAP with Streaming Linear Discriminant Analysis with shared covariance, achieving 95.7% on GSC (HuBERT-B, Table 1), claiming a new SOTA for EOCL KWS; validated robustness in a grid of 10 OCL methods, 12 poolings, 6 backbones, 2 datasets, 5 languages, and 3 class-count scales.
4. **Open Source and Reproducibility**: Data split scripts are publicly available in the author's GitHub repository.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **"Low-resource" only covers the adaptation phase, not the inference phase**. The backbone is a self-supervised large model with hundreds of millions of parameters, such as Wav2Vec2-L and HuBERT-XL, requiring a full forward pass every time during inference. The paper only reports the percentage increase in parameters (0.12%) and relative time increase (2.1%/2.6%) of TAP relative to the backbone. Absolute computing power, peak memory, power consumption, and latency are not reported. The feasibility of this pipeline on microcontroller-level devices has not been touched upon.
- **Mismatch between efficient backbones and the method is not explained**. Emformer-B, designed specifically for low-latency streaming, performs the worst under all methods (average Acc in that column of Table 1 is only 15.1), SLDA only reaches 23.2, and TAP only pulls it to 50.8. Moreover, in the pooling ablation, it is the only backbone where TAP ranks second (losing to second-order covariance pooling iSQRT-COV, Table 3). This suggests that the method's benefits depend on the temporal statistical structure of the backbone features, but the paper does not analyze the reason (inferred by this paper: the frame-level features produced by streaming local attention have a flatter time distribution, diluting the discriminative power of higher-order moments, requiring second-order full covariance modeling first).
- **Robustness of higher-order moments is not verified**. Higher-order moments are naturally sensitive to outliers. A few sentences of background noise, channel distortion, or clipping can change the third, fourth, and fifth-order statistics. The paper does not perform robustness experiments under noise, reverberation, or far-field conditions, which are exactly the norms in real wake word scenarios.
- **R=5 is an empirical value**. Ablation shows a drop at R=6 (84.9 vs. 85.5), but the paper does not give the mechanism for the drop (is it overfitting noise or variance of moment estimation), nor does it discuss whether R should be adjusted according to the number of classes or sample size.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **The main setting is a class-ordered stream with single-class tasks**, which is the hardest but also the "cleanest" setting; Table 6 shows that FT can increase from 2.5 to 44.6 under a random-order stream, indicating that method rankings are sensitive to stream shape. Although prototype methods are stable under both stream manifolds, real user registration of custom words is a sporadic, sparse, and potentially domain-shifted stream, which the paper does not cover.
- **There is a jump in the selection of comparison methods**: Regularization methods like EWC and SI are excluded with the cited reason that "they perform poorly in this setting," without quantitative data; offline upper bounds (joint training accuracy) are not reported with the reason that F(·) structures differ, so readers cannot measure how far 95.7% is from the ceiling.
- **Statistical disclosure is incomplete**: Results are averaged over 5 class orders, but standard deviations are not always displayed; cross-lingual results for MSWC are only in bar charts (Figure 3) without numerical tables and variances; there is a clear discrepancy between the languages listed in the text (en, fr, ca, rw) and the figure (en, de, fr, ca, rw).
- **The number of samples per word is fixed at the thousand level** (GSC 1.5k–4k, MSWC 1k per word), without exploring the limits of few-shot scenarios (dozens of registration data), which is the true data scale for user-defined wake words.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Multi-lingual and multi-domain pre-trained backbones**: The paper itself points out that using multi-lingual pre-training (such as UniSpeech-like schemes) can further improve cross-lingual personalization accuracy, which is the most direct incremental improvement.
- **Combination of backbone compression and TAP**: Combining lightweight backbones with quantization or distillation with TAP-SLDA to verify whether statistical pooling remains effective on true low-bit, true small-model features, truly closing the "edge device" story.
- **Robustness expansion**: Perform robust estimation of higher-order moments in weighted or truncated forms (e.g., moments after winsorization), and supplement evaluations under noise/reverberation/far-field conditions to test the attenuation curve of the method in real acoustic environments.
- **Hybrid schemes with restricted replay**: In scenarios where privacy allows a small amount of local buffering, explore whether the combination of TAP-SLDA and small-capacity replay can further suppress forgetting.
- **Real-device deployment verification**: Measure power consumption, latency, and memory usage on real embedded hardware to answer the engineering question of "whether EOCL can really complete online adaptation within the power budget of always-on wake word listening."
