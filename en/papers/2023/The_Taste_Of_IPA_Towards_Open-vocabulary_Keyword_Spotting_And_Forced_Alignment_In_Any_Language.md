# The taste of IPA: Towards open-vocabulary keyword spotting and forced alignment in any language

- **Authors/Affiliations**: Jian Zhu, Changbing Yang, Farhan Samir, Jahurul Islam (Department of Linguistics / Natural Language Processing Group, University of British Columbia)
- **Date**: November 2023 (arXiv:2311.08323; v2 updated on April 1, 2024)
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08323 (Code, data, and pre-trained models are open source: https://github.com/lingjzhu/clap-ipa)
- **Keywords**: Open-vocabulary keyword spotting, Forced alignment, International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), Contrastive learning, Cross-lingual zero-shot generalization, Phoneme representation, Multilingual speech retrieval

## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

The diversity of human languages is the most fundamental obstacle facing multilingual speech processing systems. Recent mainstream evidence suggests that scaling up multilingual data significantly improves system performance (e.g., cross-lingual representations in wav2vec 2.0, XLS-R, Whisper, and the 1,000+ language MMS). However, there are thousands of languages in the world, and collecting large-scale annotated data for each one is extremely difficult, if not impossible. Therefore, an increasingly critical question is: Can we build speech processing systems that generalize directly to any unseen language, rather than repeating the cycle of data collection and training for every language?

The answer in this paper is built upon a phonetic fact: although languages appear vastly different on the surface, human speech is constrained by the hard anatomical limits of the vocal tract—the vocal tract structure is identical for all humans (Gick et al. 2013), meaning the degrees of freedom in human speech are extremely limited. Typological studies show that almost all human speech can be represented by approximately 150 phonemes and diacritics (Phoible database, Moran et al. 2014; Gordon 2016). Based on this, phoneticians crafted a universal symbol system: the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA). In other words, IPA is naturally a "cross-lingually shared modeling unit": any word in any language can be transcribed into a sequence within the same symbol system. If a model learns to map speech signals and IPA sequences into the same embedding space, then theoretically, switching to a new language only requires changing the IPA string; the model itself requires no retraining.

The paper applies this problem to two classic tasks. The first is Keyword Spotting (KWS), which involves identifying specific keywords in streaming speech, a core component of applications like voice assistant wake-up. The second is Forced Alignment, which aligns the time intervals of speech signals to given phoneme/IPA sequences, widely used in speech synthesis, pronunciation assessment, language documentation, and corpus construction. Both tasks have significant practical demand, yet there is no universal system that covers all languages.

Additionally, there is a data-level pain point: the vast majority of speech corpora available are "audio-text" pairs, while corpora with phoneme-level (IPA) transcriptions are extremely scarce. Transcribing speech into IPA requires years of phonetic training, making large-scale production difficult. This directly leads to a long-term lack of large-scale training data support for the route of "using IPA as a modeling unit."

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

- **KWS research is English-centric**: Mainstream works (Chen et al. 2014's small DNN, Tang & Lin 2018's residual networks, Rybakov et al. 2020's KWT, Berg et al. 2021) are almost exclusively conducted in English. Recent multilingual keyword spotting systems (Mazumder et al. 2021's few-shot approach, Lei et al. 2023, Reuter et al. 2023) rely on few-shot learning to adapt to new words or languages—requiring several audio examples for the target language/word, making zero-shot impossible.
- **Inherent defects of orthographic text as a modeling unit**: Most existing systems use text as the modeling unit. However, orthography varies by language and often fails to accurately reflect actual pronunciation. A language with an unseen writing system (e.g., Tamil, Vietnamese tone marks, Japanese, Arabic, Cantonese) directly breaks the generalization ability of text-based models—the model has never seen such "character-pronunciation" mappings during training. The paper proves this through controlled variable experiments later on.
- **The modeling unit in contrastive learning frameworks is still text**: CLAP (Wu et al. 2023) and subsequent CLARA (Noriy et al. 2023) use language-audio contrastive learning for retrieval, but the matching target is captions/text rather than phoneme sequences; semantic-based speech retrieval (Duquenne et al. 2021, SAMu-XLSR, Khurana et al. 2022) matches discourse semantics rather than surface phonetic values, which is a different task objective. The contrastive learning paradigm has been proven to enable open-vocabulary KWS (CED, Nishu et al. 2023), but it has not yet been combined with a "cross-lingually shared symbol system."
- **Forced alignment systems are still dominated by monolingual HMMs**: The most popular aligners currently (MFA, WebMAUS, FAVE) are based on HMMs and require training acoustic models for the target language, essentially operating in a monolingual setting. Neural aligners (Kelley & Tucker 2018, Kürzinger et al. 2020's CTC-segmentation, Schulze-Forster et al. 2020, Teytaut et al. 2021/2022, Zhu et al. 2022c) generally outperform HMMs in performance, but almost no work focuses on "one model serving multiple languages simultaneously," let alone alignment systems that require no adaptation for unseen languages.
- **Phoneme-level annotated data cannot be scaled**: G2P (Grapheme-to-Phoneme conversion) only provides "dictionary pronunciation" rather than "actual pronunciation," and many languages lack pronunciation dictionaries, text transcriptions, and basic NLP tools (e.g., Khmer lacks even a word segmentation tool), making the construction of phoneme corpora itself an engineering bottleneck.

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

1. **Scaling and Quality Control of Phoneme Corpora**: How to construct a corpus (IPA PACK) of over 1,000 hours, covering over 100 languages, with phoneme transcriptions and linguist spot-checks, while handling specific engineering issues such as incomplete G2P system coverage, lack of word boundary markers in Asian languages, and inconsistent transcription standards.
2. **Zero-Shot Open-Vocabulary KWS**: How to design a model that can map any speech signal and any phoneme sequence into the same embedding space for matching, allowing keywords to be retrieved via IPA strings without any audio samples of the target language/word and without retraining.
3. **Time Alignment Without Alignment Labels**: How to enable the model to produce "phoneme-speech frame" temporal correspondences without frame-level annotations, and obtain a cross-lingually generalizable forced aligner, IPA-ALIGNER, by fine-tuning with an alignment loss.
4. **Validating the Core Hypothesis that "Phonemes Are More Beneficial for Cross-Lingual Knowledge Transfer Than Text"**: This requires strict controlled variable experiments (same data, same parameter count, same hyperparameters, only changing the modeling unit) and quantitative analysis of the correlation between training duration and performance.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

CLAP-IPA (Contrastive Language-Audio Pretraining with IPA) follows the dual-tower contrastive learning framework of CLAP: a phoneme encoder processes phoneme sequences, and a speech encoder processes speech features. Each undergoes average pooling to obtain fixed-length embeddings, and a contrastive loss pulls positive pairs (the same speech and its phoneme transcription) closer while pushing negative pairs apart. The choice of a dual-tower architecture over a single-tower cross-attention architecture is motivated by the fact that KWS is essentially a retrieval task: keywords are encoded offline into the database in IPA sequence form, and during runtime, only the cosine similarity between the speech embedding and candidate embeddings needs to be calculated. This naturally supports open-vocabulary (any keyword can be expressed as an IPA string) and scalable retrieval.

The specific design of the two towers has clear engineering considerations:

- **Speech Encoder**: Directly adopts the Whisper encoder architecture and initializes it with Whisper pre-trained encoder weights, discarding its decoder. The motivation is to reuse the strong multilingual speech representations learned by Whisper, saving the cost of pre-training from scratch. The paper also makes a detailed correction: the original Whisper encoder does not accept attention masks, but padding tokens would introduce bias during average pooling. Therefore, the authors pass the attention mask into the speech encoder, performing average pooling only on non-padded hidden states. SpecAugment is applied for data augmentation during training according to Whisper's default hyperparameters.
- **Phoneme Encoder**: Uses a BERT architecture with mean pooling of the final layer's hidden states. Since the vocabulary and language model distribution of generic BERT are completely unsuited for IPA symbol sequences, the authors pre-trained a phoneme BERT from scratch on pure phoneme transcription data: the data pool aggregated IPA PACK, CharsiuG2P pronunciation dictionaries, and VoxCommunis, totaling 11 million samples from over 110 languages; the masking probability was set to 30% (higher than the standard BERT's 15%), on the grounds that the complexity of phoneme sequences is lower than natural text, requiring stronger perturbation to learn effective representations.

Three model sizes were released, with hyperparameters (number of layers, hidden dimensions, number of attention heads) aligned with Whisper encoder's tiny/base/small tiers: CLAP-IPA-tiny has 4 layers, 384 dimensions, 6 heads, and 16M parameters; base has 6 layers, 512 dimensions, 8 heads, and 28.5M; small has 12 layers, 768 dimensions, 12 heads, and 96.2M (Table 7). The speech tower and phoneme tower structures are symmetric.

The accompanying **phoneme tokenizer** was trained using sentencepiece's unigram algorithm, with a vocabulary size of 450, including byte-fallback to handle unknown characters, covering all basic IPA symbols and diacritics (including tone marks, stress markers, and tie bars for affricates). A notable decision: the authors observed that IPA transcriptions vary across languages (e.g., affricates are sometimes written as [tS] and sometimes as [t͡S] with a tie bar; stress markers are language-specific phenomena), but **deliberately did not normalize them** to preserve data diversity—this is consistent with their "linguist spot-check" data philosophy, at the cost of introducing some noise.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**The contrastive loss uses SigLIP instead of CLIP's softmax loss** (Equation 1):

$$L=-\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i=1}^{|B|}\sum_{j=1}^{|B|}\log\frac{1}{1+e^{z_{ij}(-t\,x_i\cdot y_j+b)}}$$

where $x_i, y_j \in \mathbb{R}^D$ are the embeddings of the $i$-th speech and $j$-th phoneme sequence in the batch after encoding and mean pooling (normalized); $z_{ij}=1$ for positive pairs and $-1$ for negative pairs; $t$ and $b$ are learnable scalars during training, initialized as $t=\log 10$ and $b=-10$ following Zhai et al. (2023). The reason for choosing SigLIP is that it is simpler and has been proven equally effective as the softmax-based CLIP loss (each pair is independently discriminated via sigmoid, without relying on batch-wise softmax normalization, making it more robust to batch size and negative sample composition).

**Mathematics of Alignment**: Let the token-wise hidden states of the speech encoder and phoneme encoder be $H_s \in \mathbb{R}^{T\times D}$ and $H_p \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$, respectively. First, perform adaptive average pooling (see below), then normalize row-wise:

$$H'_s=\text{Normalize}(M_s H_s,\ \text{dim}=-1),\quad H'_p=\text{Normalize}(M_p H_p,\ \text{dim}=-1)$$

$$D = H'_s H_p^{\prime\top}/\tau$$

where the pooling masks are $M_p \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N'}$ and $M_s \in \mathbb{R}^{T\times T'}$, and $\tau$ is a fixed temperature, defaulting to 0.05. The similarity matrix $D \in \mathbb{R}^{T'\times N'}$ is then used with Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) to derive a monotonic alignment path between speech frames and phoneme units—**no frame-level alignment labels are needed at any stage**, which constitutes zero-shot forced alignment.

**Forward-Sum Fine-tuning**: IPA-ALIGNER is fine-tuned on top of CLAP-IPA using $L = L_{ForwardSum}(D)$. This loss originates from the forward algorithm of classic HMMs: maximizing the "likelihood of the phoneme sequence given the speech sequence" under a strict monotonic constraint. It is a monotonic alignment learning loss validated by RAD-TTS (Shih et al. 2021) and One TTS Aligner (Badlani et al. 2022). The paper explicitly states that this loss requires good prior alignment to converge to meaningful results—training from random initialization cannot yield effective alignment. Therefore, it must be initialized with CLAP-IPA (which also explains why alignment ability must first "emerge" from contrastive training).

### Key Technical Innovation 1: IPA PACK—Engineering Methodology for Scaling Phoneme Corpora

IPA PACK covers 115 languages (spanning multiple language families), totaling over 1,000 hours (the sum of subsets in Table 1 is approximately 1,412 hours). It is composed of three existing multilingual corpora transcribed via G2P and spot-checked by linguists:

- **FLEURS-IPA** (77 languages, 779.54 hours, of which 544.02 hours are for training): Transcribed using two multilingual G2P systems, Epitran and CharsiuG2P. Their covered language sets partially overlap, and they are used in combination to maximize language diversity. During preprocessing, texts containing Arabic numerals or code-switching were removed because G2P cannot handle them correctly. A dedicated pipeline was created for Asian languages that do not use spaces to mark word boundaries: Mandarin first generates Pinyin via G2pW before mapping to IPA; Thai uses PyThaiNLP for word segmentation and G2P; Japanese uses Fugashi for word segmentation before transcription.
- **MSWC-IPA** (36 languages, 613.44 hours, 2.3 million word instances; Table 9 shows all samples are padded to 1-second segments): MSWC itself is a word-level corpus, so transcription can be done directly via G2P on the word list. To balance diversity and quantity, the sample limit for each word was set to 50—high-frequency words only randomly retain 50 instances to prevent them from dominating the training set.
- **DoReCo-IPA** (44 languages, 18.99 hours): The original DoReCo consists of hour-long long recordings, cut into utterances based on sentence boundaries in the annotations. Its transcription originally used X-SAMPA symbols, which have a one-to-one mapping with IPA, allowing for direct conversion. Utterances with incomplete transcriptions or high background noise were discarded.

**Quality verification** is the core of the data work: two trained linguists (the authors) randomly listened to at least 10 samples per language to judge transcription quality. A relatively loose standard was adopted: as long as the speech signal and transcription matched by more than 80%, it was considered valid. The authors admit that the premise that "each utterance has a single correct transcription" is itself flawed (speech reduction and coarticulation lead to variants; even trained phoneticians often disagree), so the 80% threshold is a pragmatic choice, and transcription errors still exist in the dataset. Additionally, due to licensing compliance, languages with CC Non-Commercial licenses in DoReCo only entered the test set, not the training set, and data for each language is acknowledged item-by-item in Table 11.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Emergent Temporal Alignment in Contrastive Training

An important observation in the paper: using only sequence-level contrastive loss (pulling the entire utterance embedding closer to the entire phoneme sequence embedding), the model spontaneously learned token-level temporal correspondences. By calculating the cosine similarity matrix using the token-wise hidden states of both encoders, a blurry but clearly existing monotonic alignment structure can be seen (Figure 2 upper, Evenki utterance example). This provides the foundation for zero-shot forced alignment and is the first large-scale demonstration of the phenomenon that "contrastive representations inherently contain fine-grained alignment information" in a cross-lingual setting.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Adaptive Average Pooling—Matching Alignment Granularity to Natural Phonological Units

The hidden states output by the tokenizer correspond to character/byte units rather than natural phonological units (a phoneme $tʃʰ$ might be split into three tokens: t, ʃ, ʰ). Direct alignment would result in incorrect granularity. The paper defines an adaptive average pooling mask $M_p$ to average continuous hidden states belonging to the same phoneme (or word) into a single vector, so that each output state after pooling corresponds to a natural phonological unit. Figure 1 provides an intuitive example: the similarities of five tokens t, ʃ, ʰ, ɔ, ɪ are 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.7 respectively. After aggregation by the pooling matrix (first three columns each 1/3, last two columns each 1/2), $tʃʰ$ gets 0.3 and $ɔɪ$ gets 0.6—exactly the weighted average. A similar $M_s$ can be defined for the speech side to compress frame resolution: word-level alignment does not require high temporal resolution, so during inference, average pooling with a window of 3 and stride of 2 is used to compress the speech hidden state length. This design allows the same alignment algorithm to apply to any granularity (phoneme-level/word-level) with adjustable temporal resolution.

When fine-tuning IPA-ALIGNER, phoneme-side uses phoneme-level pooling, while the speech side maintains original resolution ($M_s=I$); when performing phoneme alignment during inference, the same settings are used; when performing word alignment, the phoneme side pools by word, and the speech side uses the aforementioned window pooling.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

- **Difference from CLAP/CLARA**: The modeling unit is changed from text/captions to IPA phoneme sequences. This brings an essential change—in the text embedding space, "unseen writing systems" are out-of-distribution inputs, whereas the IPA symbol set is shared across all languages, so an "unseen language" is merely a new combination of existing symbols, remaining within the distribution.
- **Difference from open-vocabulary KWS models like CMCD/PhonMatchNet/CED**: These models are trained on the LibriPhrase training set, essentially being English (monolingual) models; CLAP-IPA is a multilingual model that has never seen the LibriPhrase training set, constituting an out-of-domain evaluation yet still achieving comparable levels. Like CED, it uses contrastive learning, but the pairing target is phonemes rather than text.
- **Difference from few-shot multilingual KWS (Mazumder et al.) and query-by-example (Reuter et al. 2023)**: No audio examples need to be prepared for the target language/word; keywords are provided in symbolic form (IPA strings), combining the flexibility of text input with the accuracy of phonetic matching.
- **Difference from HMM aligners (MFA/WebMAUS/FAVE)**: There is no need to train acoustic models or pronunciation dictionary alignment pipelines for the target language; one neural model directly serves multiple languages in a zero-shot manner. The difference from neural alignment baselines like W2V2-FC/FS is that the latter rely on alignment label fine-tuning (CTC/FC), whereas IPA-ALIGNER's alignment ability emerges from contrastive pre-training and is sharpened using the label-free Forward-Sum loss.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

**Training Data** (Tables 1, 7): IPA PACK training set plus filtered subsets of VoxCommunis—FLEURS-IPA training 544.02 hours (77 languages, testing 162.06 hours), MSWC-IPA training 485.35 hours (36 languages), DoReCo-IPA training 13.70 hours (44 languages), VoxCommunis 803.84 hours (38 language subsets, Table 10), totaling approximately 1,847 hours of training speech. The pre-training corpus for the phoneme BERT consisted of 11 million phoneme sequences from over 110 languages. Training was completed on a single 32GB V100: CLAP-IPA took 17 hours (tiny) to 41 hours (small) for 100,000 steps; IPA-ALIGNER fine-tuning (Table 8: initial learning rate 1e-5, cosine schedule, 100-step warmup, max 10,000 steps, batch size 128, float16 mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, early stopping based on highest F1 on TIMIT training set) took 5 to 12 hours before early stopping.

**Evaluation Data**: (1) LibriPhrase Easy/Hard—out-of-domain KWS benchmark for English (the model was not trained on its training set); (2) Five typologically diverse unseen languages (Vietnamese vie, Tamil tam, Hausa hau, Georgian geo, Oriya ori); FLEURS-IPA directly used its test set, while MSWC-IPA merged the three splits into a harder benchmark due to data scarcity; (3) UCLA Speech Corpus 95 languages (of which 81 were unseen—note the discrepancy between the abstract's "95 unseen languages" and Section 5.2's "95 (81 unseen)"); (4) 14 unseen languages in DoReCo-IPA (compared to 30 seen languages, see the train/test split column in Table 11); (5) TIMIT as the English forced alignment benchmark.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

- **LibriPhrase uses EER (Equal Error Rate) and AUC**: Completely consistent with previous works like CMCD, PhonMatchNet, and CED, ensuring comparability.
- **Unseen language retrieval uses Hit@1 and mAP**: To measure the ranking quality of phoneme-to-speech retrieval. The paper only reports results for the phoneme→speech direction, on the grounds that speech→phoneme and speech→speech retrieval results are of the same magnitude, avoiding redundancy.
- **Forced alignment uses F1 and R-Value**: Following the standards of previous alignment research (Räsänen et al. 2009; Kreuk et al. 2020; Zhu et al. 2022c). F1 uses a tolerance judgment—predicted boundaries falling within the tolerance of true boundaries are counted as hits; phoneme start point tolerance is 20ms, word start point tolerance is 100ms (since each boundary marks both the start and end of adjacent phonemes, only start points are evaluated). R-Value is a comprehensive segmentation quality metric. Note the footnote in Table 4: FAVE/MFA/Gentle/WebMAUS have a temporal resolution of 10ms, while other neural models have 20ms, giving HMM baselines a natural advantage.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**English KWS (Table 2, LibriPhrase)**: On the Easy set, CLAP-IPA-small achieved an EER of 0.56% and AUC of 99.97%, outperforming CED (1.7%, 99.84%) and PhonMatchNet (2.80%, 99.29%), which were trained on the LibriPhrase training set; tiny/base also achieved EERs of 0.68%/0.63% respectively. On the Hard set, the situation reversed: CLAP-IPA-small's EER of 18.62% was slower than CED's 14.4% (PhonMatchNet 18.82%, CMCD 32.90%). The authors judged that for distinguishing difficult cases, language-specific fine-tuning/training is still necessary; however, considering CLAP-IPA had never seen the LibriPhrase training set (out-of-domain), this gap may be largely due to domain mismatch.

**Unseen Language Retrieval (Table 3)**: On MSWC-IPA, CLAP-IPA models achieved Hit@1 of approximately 82-84%; on FLEURS-IPA, it reached as high as 97.6-99.2% (base was best: 99.20/99.27); on the UCLA corpus, 51-55%; on DoReCo-IPA, tiny/base reached 95.46/96.84 and 96.54/97.77. The text model CLAP-IPA-IPA-TEXT collapsed comprehensively: MSWC 13.51, FLEURS 8.48 Hit@1. Two patterns are worth noting: (1) Utterance-level retrieval (FLEURS, DoReCo, average duration around 10 seconds) is significantly easier than word-level retrieval (MSWC 1-second segments, UCLA); the authors' explanation is that longer phoneme sequences are easier to distinguish in the candidate pool; (2) Model scale is positively correlated with performance on seen languages (Table 2 English), but not correlated with cross-lingual generalization ability (in Table 3, tiny/base/small have mixed results across unseen language benchmarks).

**English Forced Alignment (Table 4, TIMIT)**: Zero-shot CLAP-IPA-tiny achieved a word-level F1 of 84.37 (R-Val 86.66), exceeding all reported baselines (FAVE/MFA/Gentle/WebMAUS did not report word-level results, marked as "-"); phoneme-level zero-shot was weaker (tiny F1 40.46). After Forward-Sum fine-tuning, IPA-ALIGNER-tiny's word-level F1 rose to 86.84, and the phoneme-level F1 improvement was most significant: tiny increased from 40.46 to 57.31, base reached 60.86 (R-Val 66.67), approaching MFA's 63.0 but still lower than WebMAUS's 70.0. It must be emphasized that TIMIT was not in its training data; this is an out-of-domain result. Another unexplained phenomenon: zero-shot alignment quality is negatively correlated with model scale (tiny 84.37 > base 78.61 > small 74.18 word-level F1); the paper provides no mechanistic analysis.

**Low-Resource Language Alignment (Table 5, DoReCo-IPA)**: There was no significant difference in performance between seen/unseen languages (e.g., after fine-tuning, tiny seen word F1 74.18 vs unseen 76.33; phoneme-level 47.08 vs 48.96), indicating that generalization is real rather than memorization. However, the authors themselves noted that there were only 14 unseen languages compared to 30 seen ones, and sample asymmetry might introduce bias. In Table 5, IPA-ALIGNER-base's seen word R-Val of 91.47 deviates significantly from other values in the same column (its F1 is only 78.30, unseen word R-Val is 83.52), suspected to be a typesetting error, which the paper does not explain.

**Qualitative Evidence**: Figure 2 shows the similarity matrix heatmap for an Evenki (unseen language) utterance—blurry monotonic bands in zero-shot, becoming a clear diagonal structure after fine-tuning. Table 6 shows retrieval results for query [étá]: the most similar candidates (Étá, éta, lāta, ìtaà, ait:a, etc.) are highly similar to the query in phonetic features and syllable structure, while the least similar candidates (bỌ̃ti, sorNgi, mbùrùù, etc.) are completely different, indicating that similarity in the embedding space is highly consistent with human speech perception.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper designed three sets of controlled variable experiments, all conducted on the same data subset (FLEURS-IPA + MSWC-IPA), with the same parameter count and training hyperparameters:

1. **Phoneme vs. Text (CLAP-IPA-PHONE vs CLAP-IPA-TEXT)**: This is the most critical cut in the entire paper. On LibriPhrase-Easy, EER was 1.3% vs 6.0% (Table 2); the gap on unseen languages was an order of magnitude—MSWC Hit@1 79.28 vs 13.51, FLEURS 86.4 vs 8.48 (Table 3). The scatter plot by language in Figure 3 shows that the phoneme model significantly outperformed the text model in **every language**, especially in languages with less training data. The text model failed significantly for unseen writing systems (Tamil, Vietnamese, Japanese, Arabic, Cantonese), but generalized well to Hausa (Latin alphabet). Conclusion: Cross-lingual generalization ability comes from "phonemes as the modeling unit" itself, not from data or capacity.
2. **Number of Languages vs. Training Duration (CLAP-IPA-FLEURS vs CLAP-IPA-VC)**: VoxCommunis's duration is almost twice that of FLEURS-IPA, while the number of languages is about half. Their performance on most metrics in Tables 2 and 3 was close (e.g., Easy EER 0.81 vs 0.95; MSWC Hit@1 84.38 vs 83.48), and on DoReCo, VC was actually significantly better (90.56 vs 73.91). Combining this with correlation analysis—the text model's Hit@1 was moderately correlated with language training duration (Spearman ρ=0.42, p≤0.0002), while the phoneme model was not (ρ=0.14, p=0.22, Figure 3)—the authors drew an important practical inference: **when modeling with phonemes, stacking duration on a dozen relatively high-resource languages is more cost-effective than stacking the number of languages**, because the former is much easier under real-world data distributions. This provides a path for using large-scale data from a few high-resource languages to serve many low-resource/zero-resource languages.
3. **Zero-Shot vs. Fine-Tuning (CLAP-IPA vs IPA-ALIGNER)**: Forward-Sum fine-tuning brought consistent improvements, with the main benefits at the phoneme level (tiny phoneme F1 40.46→57.31, Table 4); word-level improvements were smaller (84.37→86.84). Notably, the fine-tuning data deliberately excluded MSWC-IPA—the paper does not explicitly state the reason, but a reasonable explanation is that MSWC consists of 1-second padded word-level segments (Table 9 note), lacking the continuous phoneme structure within utterances, providing limited help for learning temporal alignment (this is an inference).

## Main Contributions

1. **IPA PACK Dataset**: The first large-scale, linguist-spot-checked multilingual phoneme transcription corpus, covering 115 languages and over 1,000 hours (totaling approximately 1,412 hours in Table 1), with complete per-language statistics (Tables 9-12) and licensing compliance handling (non-commercial languages only entered the test set), and open-sourced for redistribution. This fills the data gap for the "phoneme modeling unit" route.
2. **CLAP-IPA Model**: The first demonstrated multilingual phoneme-audio contrastive embedding model capable of zero-shot open-vocabulary KWS generalizing to 95 unseen languages, simultaneously providing cross-lingual phoneme↔speech retrieval capabilities (Tables 3, 6).
3. **IPA-ALIGNER Model**: Through the three steps of "alignment emerging from contrastive training + adaptive average pooling + Forward-Sum fine-tuning," an adaptation-free cross-lingual forced aligner was obtained, reaching levels comparable to mainstream HMM aligners on out-of-domain TIMIT (Table 4), and performing similarly on 14 unseen low-resource languages as on seen languages (Table 5).
4. **Empirical Conclusions**: Proved via controlled variable experiments that phoneme modeling units enable knowledge transfer across languages, decoupling performance from single-language training duration (ρ=0.14 and insignificant vs. text model ρ=0.42); the scaling strategy judgment that "scaling for duration is better than scaling for number of languages" can directly guide the construction of multilingual systems.
5. **New Task Baselines**: Open-source comparable baselines for multilingual unseen language KWS and forced alignment were almost non-existent prior to this work; the paper's models and results themselves constitute the starting point for subsequent research.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Contradiction between computational efficiency and edge deployment**: The model parameter count is 16M-96.2M (Table 7), while the mainstream deployment scenario for KWS is compute-constrained mobile devices; the authors explicitly state in Limitations that the parameter count is still too high, and speech sequences are far longer than text sequences, making quadratic-complexity self-attention potentially an unsuitable architecture for processing speech. This directly conflicts with the long-standing "small-footprint" traditional demand in the KWS field and is the biggest obstacle to productization.
- **Still inferior to specialized monolingual models on high-resource languages**: On LibriPhrase-Hard, EER of 18.62% is slower than CED's 14.4% (Table 2); on TIMIT, phoneme-level F1 of 60.86 is lower than WebMAUS's 70.0 (Table 4). The cost-effectiveness of multilingual general models on high-resource languages has not yet turned around, although part of the gap can be attributed to domain mismatch (the model was not trained on LibriPhrase/TIMIT training sets).
- **Uncontrollable propagation of G2P noise**: Phoneme transcription reflects "dictionary normative pronunciation" rather than "actual pronunciation," and the verification standard was relaxed to 80% match for passing, meaning transcription errors and Unicode encoding issues still exist in the data; how biases and noise in G2P predictions propagate to downstream multilingual tasks is acknowledged by the paper as unclear.
- **New language access depends on G2P pipelines**: Keywords must be input in IPA form, and a language must have a pronunciation dictionary, text transcription, and word segmentation tool to enter the corpus—languages like Khmer were excluded due to the lack of segmentation tools. The so-called "any language" is actually limited by the coverage of the G2P ecosystem.
- **Noise introduced by not normalizing**: Language-specific annotations such as affricate tie bars and stress markers were intentionally retained, increasing the information content of the training data, but also introducing redundant variants within the symbol system.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- **Early stopping criterion has slight leakage**: The early stopping criterion for IPA-ALIGNER is "highest F1 on the TIMIT training set" (Table 8). Although the training data does not contain TIMIT, the model selection process used the training set of the evaluation benchmark, constituting a mild optimistic bias for the TIMIT results in Table 4.
- **Unseen language sample size is asymmetric and small**: On DoReCo, there are 14 unseen languages compared to 30 seen ones; the authors themselves hinted that the conclusion of "seen ≈ unseen" might be affected by this bias; the inconsistency between the abstract's "95 unseen languages" and Section 5.2's "95 (81 unseen)" in the UCLA languages was not clarified in specific terms.
- **Missing baselines in some comparisons**: In Table 4, HMM baselines have no word-level results (all marked as "-"), meaning word-level alignment was actually only compared among their own models; in Table 3, the two ablation models TEXT/PHONE were not evaluated on UCLA and DoReCo (marked as "-").
- **Main comparisons lack significance testing**: Except for the Spearman test on training duration correlation, no confidence intervals or significance levels were reported in the main tables; some gaps (e.g., tiny vs. base on UCLA: 51.71 vs 52.17) might be within the noise range.
- **Unclarified suspicious data**: In Table 5, IPA-ALIGNER-base's seen word R-Val of 91.47 is clearly inconsistent with the pattern of other values in the same table, suspected to be a typo; the phenomenon of zero-shot alignment quality decreasing as model scale increases (Table 4) has no analysis.
- **Confounding variables in correlation analysis**: Training duration is strongly correlated with language family, writing system, recording conditions, etc.; the comparison of ρ did not control for these factors.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Efficiency routes**: Knowledge distillation, low-bit quantization, linear attention, or streaming architectures to compress the dual-tower models starting from 16M into the power and latency budgets of edge KWS—this is also a direction where mature tools from the KWS community (e.g., binarization, MobileNet series) can be grafted.
- **Alignment quality**: Finer temporal resolution (current speech side is 20ms), hybrid CTC/stronger monotonic alignment losses to narrow the gap with WebMAUS in English phoneme-level; explain and utilize the phenomenon that "smaller models have better zero-shot alignment."
- **Data expansion**: Include more endangered and low-resource languages; improve G2P and Unicode normalization processes; construct manually gold-standard subsets for some languages to replace the loose 80% spot-check.
- **Task expansion**: The multilingual phoneme recognition, speech synthesis (aligner directly serving TTS frontends), and endangered language field recording (aligner automatically splitting phoneme boundaries for linguists) envisioned by the authors are direct downstream applications of IPA PACK.
- **High-resource language hybrid strategies**: General multilingual base + lightweight language-specific adaptation (adapter/LoRA), balancing difficult cases like LibriPhrase-Hard with zero-shot generalization.
