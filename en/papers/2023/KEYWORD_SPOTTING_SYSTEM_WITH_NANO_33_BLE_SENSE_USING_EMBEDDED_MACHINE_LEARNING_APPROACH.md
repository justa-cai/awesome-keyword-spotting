## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

This paper does not address the research question of "how to push KWS accuracy up by another percentage point," but rather an engineering implementation problem: how to fit a keyword spotting model capable of understanding voice commands entirely into a few-dollar microcontroller development board, and verify its usability in real-world environments (across different times of day and different speakers).

The background chain provided at the beginning of the paper is clear. Machine learning inference is typically offloaded to the cloud due to abundant computing power there; however, this offloading comes at a triple cost—latency, energy consumption, and privacy (as listed in the introduction: latency, energy, and privacy)—and requires a constantly connected network such as Wi-Fi. For smart home scenarios (the paper lists examples such as smart switches, smart plugs, light bulbs, fans, heaters, and curtains), a voice command must first leave the home, go to the cloud, and then return to turn on the light. This path is slow, expensive, and insecure. Although commercial products like Alexa and Siri offer mature experiences, the paper directly points out their limitations: privacy issues, dependence on the internet, dependence on the cloud, large memory footprint, and high latency.

TinyML is the path chosen by the paper. According to the definition cited in the paper, TinyML integrates extremely constrained hardware, software, machine learning algorithms, compilers, and tools to run a machine learning model compressed to the level of a few kilobytes of memory. Its core value lies in three aspects: first, data remains on the device for inference and is not transmitted externally, reducing privacy risks; second, there is no need to continuously transmit data to the cloud, saving storage and infrastructure costs, with only relevant data retained after inference; third, all computations are completed on the device, eliminating model latency issues. KWS is exactly one of the three classic applications of TinyML (the other two being visual wake words and anomaly detection).

The constraints on the hardware side are rigid: the Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense selected by the paper has only 1 MB of onboard Flash and 256 KB of SRAM (Section 2.5). On resources of this magnitude, the model must simultaneously satisfy three conditions: small size, fast inference, and usable accuracy. The abstract explicitly acknowledges the contradiction here—"maintaining accuracy and performance while reducing model size is difficult." This sentence is the core problem of the entire paper.

### Specific Deficiencies of Existing Methods

The paper systematically reviews the spectrum of existing solutions in the introduction and points out their deficiencies one by one:

- **Traditional HMM Solutions**: The classic method for KWS is based on Hidden Markov Models, but the paper points out that this method requires huge memory, a vast vocabulary, and high-intensity computation to run machine learning (Introduction: huge memory, a vast vocabulary, and a high computation), making it completely infeasible on microcontrollers.
- **Cloud Deep Learning Solutions**: Although deep learning offers high accuracy, cloud dependence brings four problems: privacy, network, latency, and memory, making it unrealistic for always-on wake-up scenarios.
- **SOTA Architectures in Literature Have Their Costs**: The 2018 attention-based CRNN small KWS model achieved a 1.02% false rejection rate under 1.0 false alarms per hour, with only 84K parameters (Introduction, Reference [13]), but such work does not provide an end-to-end reproduction path on cheap, general-purpose development boards; the MAX78000 low-power accelerator solution uses neural architecture search to adapt to hardware constraints (Reference [12]), relying on dedicated accelerator chips rather than a general MCU route; although DS-CNN (Depthwise Separable Convolution) and DCN (Deformable Convolution) architectures are designed for embedded KWS, the paper does not reproduce them under equal data and hardware conditions for comparison.
- **Energy Consumption Controversy of MFCC**: Citing the conclusion of Reference [12], the paper points out that systems with an MFCC frontend consume more than three times the energy of the KWS system proposed in that reference and cause high latency. This point actually constitutes an implicit criticism of this paper—this paper exactly adopts the MFCC frontend, indicating that the authors value process completeness and reproducibility more than extreme energy efficiency.
- **Pioneering Work on Quantization Deployment**: Reference [10] deploys DNNs to microcontrollers, using 16-bit integer models to reduce inference time and memory footprint, and points out that inference time and memory footprint can be further improved but will come at the cost of a small amount of accuracy. This paper pushes this route to 8-bit.

In summary, the gap identified by the paper is: the lack of a complete, reproducible implementation report covering the full process from "public dataset to cabbage-price development board," including statistical validation in real-world scenarios. Existing architecture papers either pursue metrics or rely on dedicated hardware, while the "copy-and-run" end-to-end recipe needed for engineering education scenarios and small teams is absent.

### Key Challenges to Be Solved by This Paper

The key challenges to be solved by this paper can be summarized in three aspects: first, under the resource constraints of 1 MB Flash / 256 KB SRAM, construct a four-class (On, Off, Noise, Unknown) keyword spotting model using MFCC plus CNN, and achieve usable accuracy of over 80%; second, install the model into the microcontroller through quantization and compiler optimization to achieve network-free, low-latency, low-power local inference; third, prove the time-invariance and speaker-invariance of this system—for this purpose, the paper specifically designs two experiments: measuring 200 times at three time points in a day, and measuring 50 times for each of six speakers of different ages and genders, and providing confidence intervals using Student's t-test. The third point is the key that distinguishes this paper from ordinary "run a demo" articles; it transforms "system usability" from a slogan into a testable proposition with statistical intervals.

## Methodology

This is an embedded implementation paper, and the methodology is organized along three main lines: hardware platform, toolchain, and model deployment.

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall workflow of the system is shown in Figure 1 (Figure 1 Project workflow) of the paper, divided into five stages: data collection, model design (including two modules: MFCC feature extraction and Keras neural network classifier), training, testing, and deployment. The data flow is: microphone collects audio, unified to a 1-second window and 16 kHz sampling rate, features are extracted via MFCC and fed into a 1D Convolutional Neural Network, outputting probabilities for four classes (On, Off, Noise, Unknown), and the onboard LED indicates Off with red and On with green (Section 2.5), forming a complete feedback loop from sound to light.

The "whys" behind several design decisions are worth expanding on. First, why four classes instead of two: a model that directly performs On/Off binary classification will misjudge all non-command words as command words, so it is necessary to introduce a noise class (pure environmental sound) and an unknown class (other speech words) as rejection options. This is exactly the meaning of the cascade architecture mentioned in the introduction—the role of KWS in the system is a trigger, not a general speech recognizer; it only needs to reliably answer "is this sound my command word?" Second, why training is done on the cloud and inference on the edge: the workflow design of Edge Impulse is cloud training and edge deployment (Section 1.0 explicitly states training is done on the cloud and the trained model can be deployed on the edge device). This decouples the constraints of both sides—training is not limited by device computing power, and deployment is not limited by network. Third, why real background noise is mixed in when constructing the dataset (emphasized in both Section 1.0 and Section 2.3): to enhance the model's performance in the presence of noise, i.e., data augmentation plays the role of domain robustness, rather than simply expanding the sample size.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**MFCC Feature Extraction**. Feeding the raw audio signal directly into the neural network without any processing yields poor results. The reason is stated directly in the paper: the raw data signals of KWS and speech recognition contain a large amount of irrelevant perturbation, so signal features must be extracted first (Section 2.3). The processing chain of MFCC is: windowing each frame of the signal for spectral analysis, passing it through a set of Mel-frequency filter banks, taking the logarithm of the filter bank outputs, and applying a discrete cosine transform (DCT) to each filter bank (Section 2.3 original text: a discrete cosine transform is applied to each filter bank) to obtain cepstral coefficients. The Mel frequency scale simulates the human ear's non-linear perception of frequency—high resolution at low frequencies and low resolution at high frequencies, which aligns with the statistical characteristics that formant information for vowels and discriminative information for consonants are mainly concentrated in the mid-to-low frequency range of speech. The role of DCT is to compress the energy distribution of the log-Mel spectrum into a few low-order coefficients, reducing dimensionality significantly while decorrelating, making it "easier for the subsequent classifier to analyze the data" (Section 2.3 original text). It should be noted that the paper does not report specific parameters for MFCC—the number of filter banks, the dimension of cepstral coefficients, and frame length and shift are not given (not reported by the paper), which affects the precision of reproduction.

**1D Convolutional Network Forward Calculation**. The model structure is shown in Figure 2 (Figure 2 Neural network architecture): the input layer receives MFCC features, followed by three 1D convolutional layers with filter counts of 8, 16, and 32 respectively, and a kernel size of 3; a dropout layer with a ratio of 0.25 follows each convolutional layer; finally, a flatten layer flattens the multi-dimensional data into one dimension, connected to a four-class output. 1D convolution slides along the time axis, looking at a small segment of MFCC frames each time, naturally capturing both short-term and long-term relationships (the introduction's evaluation of the combination of MFCC and 1D convolution). The doubling of convolutional layer channels from 8 to 16 to 32 layer by layer is a classic approach: shallow layers learn local spectral patterns, and deeper layers combine them into word-level features, while controlling the number of parameters to a small network scale.

**Dropout Regularization**. The paper provides the usage interval and reasons for selection (Section 2.3): dropout ratios are usually between 20% and 50%, with 20% being a suitable starting point; if the probability is too low, it has almost no effect; if too high, the network cannot learn enough. Finally, 0.25 is fixed after each convolutional layer because "it gives high performance." Dropout randomly cuts off some network connections during training, reducing the risk of the model overfitting to the dataset.

**8-bit Quantization and EON Compiler**. In the deployment phase, the model is converted into an Arduino library. During the conversion process, the EON compiler is enabled to quantize the model into an 8-bit network to improve on-chip performance (Section 2.5). The general principle of quantization is to compress 32-bit floating-point weights and activation values to 8-bit integers through affine mapping. Microcontrollers do not have efficient floating-point units, so 8-bit integer operations save memory bandwidth and reduce Flash/RAM usage. The paper's expression regarding the impact of quantization accuracy is cautious and vague: after quantization, the accuracy may "slightly increase" compared to the unoptimized model, but optimization "may also reduce the accuracy of the training model" (Section 2.5). The paper does not report the specific quantization scheme (symmetric/asymmetric, layer-wise/channel-wise) or the change in model size (not reported by the paper).

**Confidence Interval Formula**. The results section uses Equation (1) (the only numbered formula in the paper) for interval estimation of experimental means: $x \pm t \cdot \sigma/\sqrt{n}$, where $x$ is the sample mean, $\sigma$ is the sample standard deviation, $n$ is the number of samples, and $t$ is the critical value obtained from the t-distribution table for a given confidence level and degrees of freedom (Section 3.1). The paper selects a one-tailed test at a 95% confidence level, with the reason being: there is no need to repeat the same experiment to know the estimated mean of the results; a 95% confidence level means that if the experiment is repeated, the estimated data will almost certainly be consistent with the sample results.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Selection of Hardware Platform—Why Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense

The deployment hardware selected is the Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense. The reason given by the paper is that it comes with an onboard microphone for data collection, eliminating the need to design peripheral audio circuits (Section 2.5); at the same time, it conforms to the characteristics of miniature embedded devices: 1 MB onboard Flash and 256 KB SRAM. The main controller of this board is the nRF52840 (ARM Cortex-M4F). The paper does not elaborate on the main controller specifications, but its resource level happens to fall in the "sweet spot" where TinyML can run but there is no room for waste—boards smaller than it (such as the classic ATmega328 with 2 KB RAM) cannot even hold the MFCC buffer, while platforms larger than it lose the significance of miniaturization. The onboard tri-color LED (red, green, blue) is reused as a classifier result indicator: red corresponds to the Off keyword, and green corresponds to the On keyword, completing the input-output loop at zero cost. The entire system does not rely on an internet connection, has low latency, and low power consumption (Section 2.5). These three points are a positive response to the three sins of cloud solutions criticized in the introduction.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Edge Impulse End-to-End Toolchain—Bridging the Gap Between Training and Deployment

Another pillar of the paper is the Edge Impulse development platform. The definition cited in the paper states that Edge Impulse is used to develop algorithms that reduce system energy consumption, thereby generating systems suitable for resource-constrained devices (Section 1.0). It splits KWS development into five standardized steps: data collection, model design, training, testing, and deployment (Figure 1). Specifically for this work: uploaded audio files are automatically divided into training and test sets in an 80:20 ratio on the platform (Section 2.2); the signal processing module uses MFCC, and the learning module uses the NN Keras classifier (Section 2.1); after training is completed on the cloud, the impulse can be exported as a library or a firmware can be built, directly deployed to the Nano 33 BLE Sense (Section 1.0). The training configuration fixes three hyperparameters: 100 training epochs, a learning rate of 0.005, and a validation set ratio of 20% (Section 2.3). The learning rate controls how fast the network learns; if overfitting occurs, the learning rate needs to be lowered (the diagnostic logic given in Section 2.3). Data augmentation is performed synchronously during training: adding low-level random noise to each spectrogram to prevent the model from overfitting over more training epochs, while using the noise present in the spectrogram to evaluate network performance (Section 2.3). The significance of this toolchain route lies in its extremely low reproduction threshold—any engineer with this development board and a free account can walk through the entire process. This is exactly how the paper's positioning (an economically viable alternative) is realized.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Statistical Validation Protocol for Real-World Scenarios

The most distinctive part of the paper is the two evaluation designs after deployment. The first is the time-invariance experiment: at 9 AM, 3 PM, and 9 PM, the same person repeats On and Off 200 times each, totaling 1200 inferences (Section 3.1, Table 5). The design motivation is clearly stated in the paper: environmental external noise differs at different times of the day; if the model's performance drifts with time, it is unusable. The second is the speaker-invariance experiment: six people (two females, four males, aged 16 to 52) each repeat the two keywords 50 times during the day, totaling 600 inferences (Section 3.2, Table 6). The motivation is that differences in voice timbre among different people will lead to model misclassification; a high-performance system should respond to commands from any gender and age. The results of both experiments are reported with confidence intervals using Student's t-test at a 95% confidence level (Equation 1), upgrading "system performance stability" from a qualitative description to a quantitative interval. This protocol is rare in similar "development board implementation" papers and represents a true incremental contribution at the methodological level.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the various routes梳理 in the introduction, the difference of this paper lies in deliberate simplification. Compared to traditional HMM solutions, using MFCC plus a small CNN replaces the large-vocabulary acoustic model, reducing memory requirements from "huge" to the KB level. Compared to cloud solutions, inference is completely localized, and the three problems of privacy, network dependence, and latency disappear simultaneously. Compared to SOTA in literature: no attention mechanism (CRNN route), no neural architecture search (MAX78000 route), no depthwise separable convolution (DS-CNN route), no deformable convolution (DCN route), only the simplest three-layer 1D convolution—the paper's logic is to first establish the entire toolchain and validation protocol; architectural innovation is not the goal of this paper. Compared to the 16-bit quantization deployment in Reference [10], this paper advances to 8-bit and further optimizes using the EON compiler. Compared to all cited papers, this paper uniquely has the link of "post-deployment real-world statistical validation": others report dataset metrics, while this paper reports board-side measured metrics from three time points and six live humans. The cost is also obvious: the accuracy metric (84.51%) is significantly lower than that of research papers, and the paper does not reproduce any baseline on the same dataset for direct comparison (no external baseline comparison values under the same settings are reported by the paper).

## Experimental Results

### Dataset Used and Its Scale

The data all come from the Google Speech Commands database V1 (Section 2.2). V1 contains 65,000 single-speaker word recordings, covering 30 different words, contributed by 1,881 speakers, ensuring speaker diversity (Introduction, Reference [18]); V2 expands to 35 words. Following KWS conventions (References [12], etc.), the dataset is often re-annotated into 12 classes (ten command words plus silence and unknown), with a common split ratio of 80:10:10—this paper does not use this 12-class scheme but trims it down to four classes: On, Off, Noise, Unknown, with the split ratio changed to 80:20 (Section 2.2).

The specific scale is shown in Table 1 (Table 1): the training set has 1,858 samples of On (30 min 22 sec), 1,860 of Off (30 min 35 sec), 1,131 of Noise (18 min 51 sec), and 1,123 of Unknown (18 min 43 sec), totaling 5,972 samples and 1 hour 38 min 31 sec; the test set has 454 samples of On (7 min 27 sec), 469 of Off (7 min 42 sec), 263 of Noise (4 min 23 sec), and 267 of Unknown (4 min 27 sec), totaling 1,453 samples and 24 minutes; the two parts together total 7,425 samples and 2 hours 26 min 31 sec. The sources of the noise class are extremely wide (fully listed in Section 2.2): dishwasher, cat meowing, exercise bike, air conditioner, airport announcements, human chatter, copier, chewing sounds, door closing, typing, vacuum cleaner, as well as bus, café, car, field, corridor, kitchen, living room, subway, park, restaurant, chair dragging, station, road, etc., over twenty types of real environmental sounds. Each audio file is guaranteed to be at least 1 second long before entering training (Section 2.2), and the inference window is unified to 1 second, 16 kHz (Section 2.3). A technical flaw must be pointed out: Section 3.0 states "the sampling rate of each sample is 16 Hz," which contradicts the 16 kHz in Section 2.3; this is obviously a typo, and 16 kHz should be taken as the standard.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

The paper uses four layers of metrics. The first layer is overall accuracy and loss: training accuracy 88%, loss 0.36 (Table 2). The second layer is per-class accuracy, confusion matrix, and F1 score (Figure attached to Table 2 gives F1 for each class), which can expose短板 classes like unknown that are masked by the overall mean. The third layer is board-side metrics: after deployment, model accuracy is 84.51%, and inference latency is 26 ms (Table 4)—latency is the critical metric for embedded KWS, and 26 ms means real-time performance is more than sufficient. The fourth layer is statistical metrics: wrapping the board-side measured mean with the t-test confidence interval of Equation (1), with the reason for selection being that it allows estimating the range of the population true value without repeating the experiment (Section 3.1). The deep reason for choosing this metric system is the paper's engineering validation positioning: it cares about "whether this model is usable for real humans on this board," not "what rank this architecture achieves on the leaderboard." Therefore, it lacks the standard metrics of KWS research literature—the pair of operating point metrics, False Rejection Rate (FRR) and False Alarms per Hour (FA/hour), does not appear at all (not reported by the paper), which is actually a more appropriate measure for wake-word scenarios and represents a clear gap in the metric system.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

It must be stated truthfully: the paper does not set up any external baseline comparison experiments on the same dataset and hardware. All cited methods (1.02% FRR for the CRNN attention model, NAS solution for MAX78000, DS-CNN, DCN) only appear in the literature review of the introduction, and none of them are reproduced and compared (no comparison values are reported by the paper). The comparison chain truly provided by the paper is internal and longitudinal, divided into four parts.

The first part is the gap between training and testing: training accuracy is 88% (Table 2), testing accuracy is 82.4% (Table 3), a drop of 5.6 percentage points, reflecting a certain degree of overfitting, despite the use of dropout 0.25 and spectrogram noise augmentation. The second part is the disparity in per-class accuracy: on the test set, Noise is 91.6%, Off is 90.6%, On is 90.3%, while the Unknown class is only 55.4% (Table 3); on the training set, the corresponding values are Noise 98.2%, Off 93%, On 93.9%, Unknown 60% (Table 2 attached confusion matrix). The paper provides specific attribution for the confusion source of the Unknown class collapse: 12.4% of unknown words are judged as Off (similar pronunciation), and 21.8% are judged as On—for example, the pronunciation of One and On is too close, and the model biases classification towards On (Section 3.0). When the model gives similar output values for two classes simultaneously, it falls into uncertainty. For example, when the true word is House, the outputs for Unknown and Off are almost equal, and the model simply outputs uncertainty rather than the class with the higher value. The third part is the unexpected "rise" in quantization deployment: the 8-bit optimized model has an accuracy of 84.51% and a latency of 26 ms (Table 4), which is 2.11 percentage points higher than the unoptimized 82.4% (Table 3). This contradicts the intuition that quantization usually brings a slight loss in accuracy. The paper's own expression only says "it may slightly increase or decrease," without explaining the mechanism; a reasonable suspicion is that there are differences in the two evaluation protocols or data splits, but this anomaly is not explained by the paper. The fourth part is a numerical inconsistency: the conclusion states "training and testing accuracies are 88% and 84.24% respectively," which is inconsistent with 84.51% in Table 4 and the abstract. Three numbers appear for the same quantity (82.4%, 84.51%, 84.24%), and self-verification is needed during reproduction.

The results of the two real-world experiments on the board are as follows. Time experiment (Table 5, Figure 3): at 9 AM, On 174/200, Off 176/200 (accuracy 87%, 88%); at 3 PM, On 177/200, Off 186/200 (88.5%, 93%); at 9 PM, On 173/200, Off 175/200 (86.5%, 87.5%). The mean for On is 174.67, standard deviation 2.082; the mean for Off is 179, standard deviation 6.083. The average accuracy is On 87.33%, Off 89.5% (Section 3.1). The t-test (degrees of freedom 2, t=4.303) gives the interval for On as 174.67±5.172 (upper bound 179.84, lower bound 169.50), and for Off as 179±15.11 (upper bound 184.11, lower bound 173.88). The paper's explanation for the highest accuracy at 3 PM is that the external noise in the experimental environment is less at this time—this is a subjective attribution, without noise measurement verification. Personnel experiment (Table 6, Figure 4): among the six people, Person 2 and Person 4 have the highest On accuracy (94%), Person 1 and Person 3 have the highest Off accuracy (94%), and the lowest is Person X's Off at 88%; the mean for On is 46.33 (standard deviation 0.5164), the mean for Off is 45.67 (standard deviation 1.2111). The average accuracy is On 92.67%, Off 91.33% (Section 3.2). The t-test (six samples, t=2.571) gives the interval for On as 46.33±0.542 (45.79 to 46.87), and for Off as 45.67±1.271 (44.4 to 46.94). Looking horizontally at the literature SOTA, the dataset accuracy of 84.51% is far lower than that of research works (which are generally above 95%), but those works neither ran on this 1 MB Flash board nor performed board-side statistical validation with six people across three time points—the "comparability" of the two sides was never established to begin with, which is the expectation that must be set correctly when reading this paper.

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper does not have a formal ablation experiment table, but there are five scattered pieces of ablation-style decision evidence. First, network depth: fixed to use three convolutional layers, with the reason being that fewer or more layers would lead to degradation in classification accuracy (Section 2.3)—but the paper does not report specific accuracy numbers for different numbers of layers, so this conclusion cannot be verified. Second, dropout ratio: provides the usage interval of 20%–50% and the qualitative judgment of "too low is ineffective, too high cannot learn," finally selecting 0.25 because of its high performance (Section 2.3), again without scanning data (not reported by the paper). Third, before and after quantization: unoptimized model 82.4% vs. post-quantization 84.51% (Table 3 vs. Table 4), resulting in the anomalous conclusion that "quantization does not decrease but increases accuracy in this case," with the mechanism unexplained. Fourth, data augmentation: adding random noise to spectrograms during training to resist overfitting and improve robustness (Section 2.3), but without a control group with augmentation turned off (not reported by the paper), the independent contribution of augmentation cannot be separated. Fifth, scenario ablation—the two dimensions of three time slots and six people are essentially a systematic ablation of two external factors: "environmental noise" and "timbre difference." The conclusion is: the difference between time slots is not significant (the paper's original words: 3 PM is highest but not significantly different from other time slots, the system can correctly respond to keywords at any time); the difference between people is also not large (the results for each person in Table 6 fluctuate very little, with the standard deviation of On for six people being only 0.5164 times). A doubt not discussed by the paper is: the accuracy of the personnel experiment (92.67%/91.33%) is significantly higher than that of the time experiment (87.33%/89.5%). If the two experiments used the same model and same distance, this difference implies that test conditions (speaker cooperation, distance, volume) were not controlled, and the paper does not report records of these conditions.

## Main Contributions

- **Complete End-to-End Implementation Recipe**: Provides the full process implementation from Google Speech Commands V1 four-class re-annotation, Edge Impulse cloud training (MFCC plus three-layer 1D CNN), EON compiler 8-bit quantization, to Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense board-side deployment. The final board-side accuracy is 84.51% and latency is 26 ms (Table 4). All links use free tools and cheap hardware, with extremely high reproducibility.
- **Statistical Validation Protocol for Real-World Scenarios**: For the first time (in such development board implementation reports), uses a dual-dimension board-side measurement of "200 times at each of three time points plus 50 times for each of six speakers," and reports results with 95% confidence level t-test intervals (Equation 1), proving the system is time-invariant (means 87.33%/89.5%) and speaker-invariant (means 92.67%/91.33%).
- **Engineering Confirmation of Lightweight Models**: Confirms that this extremely simple architecture of three 8/16/32 channel 1D convolutions plus 0.25 dropout can achieve command word class accuracy at the 90% level (Table 3) on a four-class small vocabulary task, and identifies the real短板 of the system as the rejection capability of the Unknown class (55.4%), pointing out the direction for subsequent improvements.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

The Unknown class is the Achilles' heel of the system: the testing accuracy is only 55.4% (Table 3), with 12.4% of unknown words flowing into Off and 21.8% into On (Section 3.0), while the rejection capability of the Unknown class is exactly the core ability that distinguishes KWS from toy demos—a smart switch that hears One as On will be unplugged by users. The vocabulary scale has only two command words, far from the common ten-word task. Key information on the model side is largely missing: number of parameters, FLOPS, model size (KB) after quantization, peak RAM usage, and energy consumption are all not reported by the paper. Only one resource metric, latency 26 ms (Table 4), is available, and the claim of "minimum memory footprint" (abstract) lacks quantitative support. MFCC parameters (number of filter banks, dimension of coefficients, frame length and shift) are not reported by the paper, and there are ambiguities in network details (the original text "3 kernel sizes" should be kernel size 3 based on context). Furthermore, the paper itself cites the literature conclusion that MFCC has excessive energy consumption and is criticized (Introduction, Reference [12]), yet still selects the MFCC frontend and does not measure energy consumption to respond to this criticism.

### Deficiencies in Experimental Design

There is no same-setting comparison with external baselines and SOTA; all cited numbers only serve as a background board (no comparison experiments are reported by the paper). There is no repetition of random seeds for multiple trainings; the single-run results of training 88% and testing 82.4% (Table 2, Table 3) have no variance information. The 2.11 percentage point increase in accuracy before and after quantization (Table 3 vs. Table 4) is unexplained, and the 84.24% in the conclusion contradicts the 84.51% in Table 4, showing rough number management. There are flaws in statistical口径: the paper claims a one-tailed test (Section 3.1), but the t critical values used, 4.303 (degrees of freedom 2) and 2.571 (degrees of freedom 5), are actually two-tailed 95% values; one-tailed should be 2.920 and 2.015—the interval calculation itself (mean plus/minus t times σ divided by square root of n) is self-consistent when substituted, but the expression of the test type is wrong. The sample size is also on the small side: n=3 for the time dimension and n=6 for the personnel dimension. The anomaly that the personnel experiment accuracy is higher than the time experiment (92.67% vs. 87.33%) is not discussed by the paper, and conditions such as test distance, sound pressure level, and microphone orientation are not reported by the paper. The "sampling rate 16 Hz" in Section 3.0 is a typo for 16 kHz.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the短板 exposed by the paper, there are six natural paths for improvement. First, rebuild the Unknown class: significantly expand the unknown word training set from 1,123 entries and add hard negative samples with pronunciations close to command words (One, Off-rhyme words, etc.), or introduce open-set recognition ideas to directly target the most painful number of 55.4%. Second, complete KWS standard metrics: replace single accuracy with the operating point curve of FRR vs. FA/hour; engineering contracts for wake-word scenarios are signed based on this pair of metrics. Third, reproduce architectures like DS-CNN and BC-ResNet on the same dataset and hardware for true comparison, answering "how much unknown class accuracy can be exchanged for a little more model complexity." Fourth, complete the bill for resources and energy: post-quantization volume, peak RAM, and energy consumption per inference (using a power meter or onboard current sampling), responding to the MFCC energy consumption controversy. Fifth, expand the validation protocol: more speakers, wider age range, far-field distance, reverberation conditions under playback noise, and record test conditions (distance, sound pressure) in the case. Sixth, extend towards productization: add a training process for custom wake words, multi-language commands (Malaysian language scenario), and cascade KWS with VAD to reduce always-on listening power consumption—this is also the complete form of the cascade architecture envisioned by the paper. Among these directions, the first two are foundations that any subsequent work cannot bypass.
