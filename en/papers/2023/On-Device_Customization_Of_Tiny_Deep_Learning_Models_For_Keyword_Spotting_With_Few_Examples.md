## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Voice command sensors are being embedded into everyday objects in large numbers, ranging from smart speakers and wearable devices to various home controllers. Their core is a locally resident keyword spotting (KWS) pipeline that continuously processes audio captured by microphones. These devices are generally battery-powered, with the data processing engine being a microcontroller (MCU): the power budget is limited to tens of milliwatts, and both computational capacity and on-chip memory are extremely constrained, typically below a few MB. In recent years, the TinyML field has efficiently run DNN KWS models with only hundreds of thousands of parameters on such chips, making inference itself no longer an insurmountable obstacle.

The real pain point lies in the "customization" phase. The current design workflow for TinyML KWS involves: collecting a keyword dataset for a specific application, manual annotation, training a dedicated DNN on a high-performance server, and then deploying it back to the device. This workflow creates a bottleneck for rapid prototyping: changing a deployment environment or a set of target words requires repeating the entire chain of data collection, annotation, and retraining. Why is this process costly? First, collecting hundreds or thousands of speech samples from different speakers and under different acoustic conditions for a new word class is itself an expensive engineering activity; second, annotation costs grow linearly with data volume; third, training must occur on a remote server, creating a round-trip from device to server and back; fourth, performing backpropagation-based transfer learning directly on the device is unrealistic on an MCU—backpropagation requires storing intermediate activations and an equivalent amount of gradients (as well as optimizer states), while activation memory is already the primary pressure on MCU memory (the maximum activation map for ResNet15 in Table 1 of this paper reaches 537.3k values). Adding gradients and optimizer states would directly exceed the RAM budget, and the numerical stability, energy consumption, and time during training are also outside the constraints. Therefore, "enabling end-users to teach a tiny device to recognize a new command with just a few recording samples" is an algorithmic design problem forced by memory and energy constraints, not merely a simple engineering issue.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper categorizes existing approaches into four types and points out their defects under the goal of "on-device few-shot customization":

- **End-to-end multi-class softmax models** (e.g., Zhang et al.'s DS-CNN series, implemented on MCUs with over 94% accuracy on the 12 classes of GSC): Classification boundaries are fixed by the output layer, and the class set is "hardcoded" into the network architecture. Adding new keywords implies full retraining. Moreover, it is essentially a closed-set classifier with no principled rejection mechanism for unknown words and silence, relying only on post-hoc threshold tuning. Additionally, training data must come from the target domain.
- **Few-shot metric learning for KWS** (e.g., ProtoNet lineage, Vygon et al.'s triplet ResNet15 with kNN classifier, Huh et al.'s centroid classifier or SVM): These methods train and test data from the same domain (usually GSC), and the reported numbers (e.g., Vygon's over 98%) implicitly benefit from same-domain advantages. Most works only report accuracy on the target word test set and do not analyze the false acceptance rate (FAR) on negative samples, whereas false triggers are the primary risk for always-on voice sensors. At the classifier level, kNN must store all reference samples, and SVM must store support vectors, causing memory to grow with the number of samples, conflicting with MCU budgets. Furthermore, none of these works have been deployed and implemented on actual TinyML models.
- **Query-by-Example RNN approaches** (e.g., Chen et al.'s two-layer LSTM with similarity comparison): RNNs are memory-bandwidth-bound and are up to 28 times less efficient than convolutional models on target hardware levels. This design was prototyped on a Hi3516EV200 development board (ARM Cortex-A7, 900 MHz, 32 MB memory, 100 mW power envelope)—this is an application processor level of resources, far exceeding MCU capabilities.
- **Few-shot approaches based on transfer learning** (e.g., Mazumder et al.): Adding a linear classifier to an EfficientNet backbone with 11 million parameters and retraining the classification head with few-shot data. The backbone itself far exceeds MCU memory limits; testing still requires 8 epochs of backpropagation training on target data. Moreover, as proven in Table 4 of this paper, such classifiers are significantly more prone to false acceptances in open-set settings—when the threshold is adjusted to a FAR of 5%, their accuracy drops from 0.87 to 0.56, whereas the method in this paper only drops from 0.88 to 0.80.

### Key Challenges Addressed by This Paper

How to achieve on-device customization of keyword sets on an MCU with a power envelope of approximately 25 mW and memory in the MB range, while satisfying five mutually constraining conditions: (1) Zero backpropagation on the device; customization actions can only consist of forward inference; (2) Only a very small number (one to ten) of samples are allowed to be recorded for each new keyword; (3) Quantifiable rejection capability for unknown speech and silence (open-set) is required, with controllable FAR; (4) The feature extractor is trained on a source corpus disjoint from the target, and must generalize across domains to unseen target words; (5) After quantization to 8-bit, it must fully fit within latency, memory, and energy budgets. The combination of these five conditions tightens the problem from "few-shot learning" to "cross-domain, open-set, training-free, quantization-friendly few-shot learning," which is the true difficulty of this paper.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall solution is a two-stage pipeline (Fig. 1). **Stage 1 (Offline, one-time)**: Train a DNN feature extractor $f(\cdot)$ using triplet loss on the source dataset MSWC, freeze it, perform 8-bit post-training quantization (PTQ), and burn it into the MCU's Flash in read-only mode. **Stage 2 (On-device, once per keyword set)**: The user records $K$ 1-second speech clips for each target command, performs forward inference on each to obtain embedding vectors, and averages them by class to form prototype vectors $c_j$ (Eq. 2). Additionally, pre-computed silence and unknown prototypes are added, storing a total of $N+2$ vectors. **During inference**: A 1-second speech clip passes through the MFCC frontend to obtain a time-frequency map, which goes through the frozen feature extractor to get an embedding. The nearest prototype is found under L2 distance; only if the distance is below threshold $\gamma$ is that class output, otherwise it is judged as unknown (Eq. 3).

Why this split? The only computation on the device is forward inference and vector averaging: no gradients, no optimizer states, and no need to retain any training data. The computational cost of customizing a new word is $K$ forward passes (approximately 9.7 ms per clip for ResNet15) plus one trivial averaging operation, all reusing the inference data path; while all true "learning" is front-loaded into a metric space trained only once, independent of the target scenario. This is a design that completely decouples "learning" from "deployment."

Frontend configuration: 16 kHz sampling, 1-second speech segments, first 10 MFCC coefficients, frame length 40 ms, frame shift 20 ms, Hamming window, resulting in a $49 \times 10$ time-frequency map. Choosing such a small feature map is to minimize activation memory—the keyword discrimination mainly relies on coarse-grained spectral envelopes and does not require finer spectral resolution.

Five backbones (Table 1): TCResNet8 (61.2k parameters, 3.0 MMAC, max activation 23.1k, embedding dim 48), DS-CNN-S/M/L (21.8k/132.9k/407.3k parameters, 2.6/9.4/28.0 MMAC, embedding dim 64/172/276), ResNet15 (482.3k parameters, 235.1 MMAC, max activation 537.3k, embedding dim 64; the max activation for DS-CNN-M in the original text is incomplete, estimated around 52k). Selection logic: DS-CNN replaces standard 2D convolutions with depthwise separable convolutions to reduce parameters and operators; TCResNet8 rearranges the time-frequency map and uses 1D temporal convolutions (with MFCCs as input channels), having the fewest operators; ResNet15 is a standard 2D convolution residual network with the highest MMAC count—it is retained because it has the highest accuracy, and (this is a key hardware insight) GAP9's convolution accelerator can achieve a peak execution efficiency of 100 MACs per cycle for 3x3 dense convolutions, whereas the pointwise layers relied upon by DS-CNN only reach 40, so the disadvantage in operator count is partially mitigated on specific hardware (Table 3).

Embedding head design: After the last convolution layer, a normalization layer is added—ResNet15 and TCResNet8 use BatchNorm as in the original triplet work, while the DS-CNN backbone uses LayerNorm (citing Zhai and Wu's observation that "classification is a strong baseline for deep metric learning," where LayerNorm yields better representation quality in this setting); followed by spatial average pooling and L2 normalization of the output. Why L2 normalization is mandatory: it compresses all embeddings onto the unit hypersphere, bounding the Euclidean distance and making it comparable across samples and classes—this is the mathematical premise for "using a single fixed threshold $\gamma$ to decide acceptance." Without this step, the differences in embedding norms between different speakers would drown out the class geometric structure.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

**Triplet Loss (Eq. 1)**: For each triplet (anchor sample, same-class hard negative, different-class negative sample), the loss is:

$$L_{TR} = \sum_{N_{tr}} \max\left(d(z^{s}_{i,c},\, z^{s}_{j\neq i,c}) - d(z^{s}_{i,c},\, z^{s}_{neg}) + m,\ 0\right)$$

where $d(\cdot)$ is the squared Euclidean distance, and $m$ is the margin. It only penalizes when "intra-class distance plus margin exceeds inter-class distance," directly optimizing the geometric structure of the metric space. Training adopts an episode-based approach: each episode randomly samples $M$ classes from 500 classes, and $Q$ samples per class. The configuration in this paper is 40 epochs, 400 episodes per epoch, with each episode batch loading 20 classes × 80 samples = 1600 samples. Negative samples use random negative mining, margin $m$ is set to 0.5, Adam optimizer with learning rate 0.001, divided by 10 after 20 epochs, and each configuration is repeated with three random seeds and averaged.

Why use triplet instead of softmax cross-entropy? Cross-entropy optimizes separability on a fixed label set, learning "these 500 classes are separated from each other," providing no distance semantics for unseen classes nor guaranteeing generalization; triplet loss optimizes the relative geometry of "intra-class clustering and inter-class separation by at least $m$," a geometric property that can be transferred to new categories never seen during training—exactly what few-shot prototypical classification consumes. And episode-based sampling itself simulates few-shot testing conditions (discriminating between random class subsets), making the training distribution consistent with the test distribution.

**Prototypes and Discrimination (Eq. 2, Eq. 3)**: The prototype for each target class $j$ is the arithmetic mean of its $K$ registered embeddings:

$$c_j = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K} z^T_{i,j}, \quad j = 1..N$$

During inference:

$$y_{pred} = \begin{cases} \arg\min_j d(f(x),\, c_j), & \text{if } d(f(x),\, c_j) < \gamma \\ y_{unk}, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Why use mean prototypes instead of kNN or SVM? Storing one prototype is just one $D$-dimensional vector (ten keywords plus unknown plus silence; when $D$ is 64, this is about 768 values, occupying less than 1 KB in 8-bit storage). kNN must store all reference samples, and SVM must store support vectors, both of which grow with data volume (Table 4 notes that these two methods require training feature-level components on the target domain). Computationally, the nearest prototype decision is just $N$ $D$-dimensional distances, negligible compared to DNN forward passes. Statistically, averaging $K$ embeddings suppresses noise from individual recordings. The $\gamma$ threshold turns a closed-set nearest prototype classifier into an open-set detector: unknown speech and silence ideally fall outside all $\gamma$ spheres.

**Why set $\gamma$ to the training margin $m$**: This is the most ingenious calibration-free design in this paper. To adjust FAR to a specified target value, the conventional approach requires annotated negative samples from the target scenario for posterior calibration—precisely the thing unavailable during on-device customization. The paper's observation is that the training process of Eq. 1 itself drives "intra-class distance plus $m$ does not exceed inter-class distance," meaning $m$ is the intra-class distance scale guaranteed by training. Using $m$ as the acceptance radius is a proxy value endorsed by the training process, requiring no calibration data. The cost is that it is conservative: large models with loose class clusters will push many positive samples outside the $\gamma$ sphere under this fixed threshold (in Fig. 3, ResNet15 achieves only 54% accuracy, but this换来s a low FAR of 0.1%–0.2%).

The paper also examined softmax normalization of the distance vector (a common practice in few-shot methods like ProtoNet), concluding that not adding softmax is systematically better. Why: softmax re-normalizes the distance vector into "relative competition among several candidates," destroying the absolute magnitude information of distances—while "the distance from the current sample to all prototypes is very far" is the absolute information that is the sole evidence for open-set rejection. After relativization, unknown samples are forced to pick the nearest among several prototypes, increasing false acceptances.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Calibration-Free On-Device Prototype Registration Pipeline

The customization action is compressed to "record $K$ clips $\rightarrow$ $K$ forward passes $\rightarrow$ average $\rightarrow$ store one vector." The unknown and silence prototypes are pre-computed before deployment using $K$ background noise clips and $K$ random word clips, then directly stored in the device (assuming these random words do not contain target keywords). The entire process involves no backpropagation, no optimizer states, and retains no training data. The quantified significance of this lies in memory: one backpropagation requires storing activations (ResNet15 max activation 537.3k, Table 1) plus an equivalent amount of gradients plus optimizer momentum, which would be several times the inference working set, directly exceeding the MCU's RAM. In contrast, prototype registration completely reuses the inference data path, and the classifier's own storage is only $(N+2) \times D$ values. This is the watershed between "can customize on-device" and "cannot."

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Cross-Domain Training Protocol and Open-Set Evaluation Protocol

The training source (MSWC English training partition) and the test/registration target (GSC) are completely disjoint: the specific speech samples of the ten target words never appeared during feature extractor training, making the evaluation a true cross-domain, word-level generalization. Meanwhile, the protocol explicitly sets up unknown and silence class prototypes, and uses the remaining 20 word classes of GSC as negative samples to specifically measure FAR. Evaluation is divided into two regimes: (a) Posterior threshold tuning to FAR equal to 5% or 1% (Table 2, giving the achievable performance upper bound); (b) Deployment-faithful fixed $\gamma$ equal to $m$ (Fig. 3, giving the realistic performance available to users out-of-the-box). Why (b) is necessary: previous few-shot KWS works either train and test on the same domain, or only report accuracy without FAR, systematically overestimating method usability. An honest customization scenario is "target words unseen, negative samples unseen, no annotated calibration set."

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Hardware-Aware Backbone Selection and Lossless 8-Bit Deployment

Target hardware GAP9: 9-core RISC-V compute cluster, supporting FP16 and 8-bit integer vector MAC instructions, with a hardware convolution accelerator. Measurements were taken at 240 MHz, 0.65 V, with a power envelope of 25.5 mW. Quantization uses the vendor GAPflow toolchain, 8-bit asymmetric per-channel post-training quantization, with calibration using only 4 random training samples. Three hardware layer findings (Table 3): First, ResNet15's MAC count is 8.4 times that of DS-CNN-L, but the DNN forward time is only about 2.25 times that of the latter (9.21 vs 4.08 ms; the paper describes this as "2.25 times faster," but from the data, it essentially means higher execution efficiency per unit MAC—the accelerator's peak for 3x3 convolutions is 100 MAC/cyc, while DS-CNN's pointwise layers max out at 40 MAC/cyc). Second, DS-CNN's LayerNorm embedding head must run on the cluster cores, including feature map mean and variance calculations, accounting for 44% of DS-CNN-L's total load (3.23 ms out of 7.31 ms total). ResNet15 uses BatchNorm plus pooling, with embedding computation taking only 0.48 ms, and TCResNet8 even 0. Third, quantization is lossless for this few-shot problem (e.g., ResNet15 full precision and quantized both have ACC 0.73, FAR 0.0013; TCResNet8 goes from 0.62 to 0.63). ResNet15's BatchNorm, unable to be folded into subsequent convolutions by the tool (unlike other models where the layer order is reversed) was quantized separately, initially causing accuracy drops; changing BatchNorm quantization to FP16 fully recovered it. Methodological lesson: **"Fewer MACs" does not equal "faster on this hardware."** The backbone must be co-designed with the accelerator's instruction structure, and the hidden cost of non-convolution operators (normalization layers) running on cluster cores must also be included in the budget.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to end-to-end DS-CNN (Zhang et al.): No target domain training is needed; open-set rejection is built into the discrimination rule. In Table 4, end-to-end with full data reaches 0.95, while this paper's ten-sample training-free method reaches 0.80, a gap of about 15 percentage points, which is the cost of "on-device instant customization + cross-domain." Compared to Vygon, Huh et al.'s triplet with kNN/SVM lineage: Although both are in the triplet family, (1) the source and target domains are disjoint, (2) prototypes replace kNN/SVM whose memory grows with data, and (3) latency and energy implementations on MCUs are provided; their 0.98/0.93 are numbers under same-domain and full-data conditions. Compared to ProtoNet: The framework of episode training plus prototype discrimination is similar, but the optimization criterion uses margin-based triplet loss instead of cross-entropy, explicitly evaluates open-set FAR, deploys to TinyML hardware, and uses $\gamma = m$ instead of softmax. Compared to Query-by-Example RNN: Convolutional backbones are up to 28 times more efficient under memory-bandwidth constraints at the MCU level, and the benchmark system is Cortex-A7 level, not MCU. Compared to transfer learning (Mazumder et al.): Zero backpropagation at test time; Table 4's control experiment (same DS-CNN-L backbone, training linear classifier head for 8 epochs at test time) shows that both methods have similar accuracy when $\gamma$ is 0 (0.87 vs 0.88 for this paper), but the linear head of transfer learning lacks rejection capability based on distance magnitude, collapsing accuracy to 0.56 when FAR is pressed to 5%, while this paper's method maintains 0.80—open-set robustness is the true watershed between the two paradigms.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scales

**Training Source Dataset**: The English training partition of the Multilingual Spoken Words Corpus (MSWC), containing up to 39k unique utterances (the original text writes "unique utterances," inferred from the context of "appearing 180 times per class on average" to be the number of word classes); the class distribution is highly imbalanced. The experiment only takes the top 500 most frequent classes, totaling 2.7 million speech clips, averaging about 5470 clips per class. **Test and Registration Dataset**: Google Speech Commands (GSC), 1-second speech clips of 35 classes. The positive sample partition GSC+ takes ten target words (on, off, left, right, up, down, go, stop, yes, no). Samples from five classes (backward, forward, visual, follow, learn) are taken to compute the unknown class prototype. The negative sample partition GSC- consists of the remaining 20 classes. The original GSC training/test split is retained, few-shot registration samples are drawn from the training split, and silence prototypes are generated using GSC background noise waveforms. To eliminate the randomness of few-shot sampling, each test is repeated 10 times and averaged. **Deployment Baseline** (Table 3): Ten-sample, four keywords (yes, stop, go, no) plus unknown and silence prototypes, a "4+2" setting, reducing the word list according to real use cases and an acceptable accuracy standard (greater than 70%). Training budget: 40 epochs, 400 episodes per epoch, 1600 samples per batch, Adam initial learning rate 0.001 (divided by 10 after 20 epochs), margin $m$ is 0.5, three random seeds. Total training time and energy consumption are not reported by the paper.

### Definition and Rationale for Evaluation Metrics

**ACC** is defined as the proportion of speech clips correctly classified on the GSC+ test set; **FAR** is defined as the proportion of GSC- negative samples misclassified as a target word. The rationale for choosing this pair of metrics: Closed-set accuracy masks open-set false triggers, and the cost of false acceptance for always-on voice sensors is real power consumption and user trust (the device executing actions out of nowhere). An ideal classifier has ACC equal to 100% and FAR equal to 0%. In reality, lowering the threshold simultaneously lowers FAR and ACC. Therefore, the paper summarizes ROC (full curve in Fig. 2 for ten samples) using "ACC under FAR constraint" (posterior threshold tuning to FAR equal to 5% and 1%, Table 2), and then reports the actual available performance under no-calibration conditions using the fixed $\gamma = m$ regime (Fig. 3). Deployment-side metrics: On-chip measured DNN forward time and embedding computation time, execution efficiency (MAC/cyc), energy consumption per inference ($\mu$J, converted based on 25.5 mW power envelope) at 240 MHz, 0.65 V, plus parameter counts from Table 1 (proxy for Flash demand) and maximum activation maps (proxy for RAM demand). Why use proxies instead of itemized bytes: The paper aims to comparatively analyze the relative overhead of backbones; the specific firmware memory list is not reported.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

**Few-Shot Accuracy** (Table 2, posterior threshold tuning, ten keywords): The backbone ranking is stable as ResNet15 > DS-CNN-L > DS-CNN-M > DS-CNN-S and TCResNet8. ResNet15 with ten samples reaches 0.80 and 0.70 at FAR equal to 5% and 1%, respectively, 3 percentage points higher than DS-CNN-L and 11 percentage points higher than DS-CNN-S; with three samples it reaches 0.74; with a single sample it is only 0.55—the embedding noise from a single recording is too large, causing significant prototype degradation. The softmax column is systematically lower (ResNet15 ten samples 0.78 vs 0.80).

**Deployment Overhead** (Table 3, GAP9, 4+2 keywords, ten samples): ResNet15 full precision ACC 0.63 class-level result is 0.73, FAR 0.0013, unchanged after quantization; DNN forward 9.21 ms plus embedding computation 0.48 ms totals 9.69 ms, i.e., the 9.7 ms mentioned in the abstract to process 1 second of speech; execution efficiency 66.8 MAC/cyc (highest among the five backbones); energy consumption per inference 246.9 $\mu$J. TCResNet8 is the cheapest: 1.11 ms, 28.3 $\mu$J, but ACC is only 0.62, FAR 0.0079. DS-CNN-L total latency 7.31 ms (of which 3.23 ms is LayerNorm embedding computation on cluster cores), 186.2 $\mu$J, ACC 0.69. The paper concludes: The largest model consumes 8.7 times the energy of TCResNet8, but exchanges this for about 10 percentage points of accuracy and 0.66 percentage points lower FAR. Calculating per frame per second (author's calculation, not in the original paper), 246.9 $\mu$J per second translates to an average computational power of about 0.25 mW, far below the 25 mW envelope, making always-on operation feasible in terms of energy.

**Comparison with Literature** (Table 4, FAR equal to 5%, ten classes): This paper's ResNet15 three, five, ten samples are 0.74, 0.78, 0.80; DS-CNN-L versions are 0.70, 0.75, 0.77. The transfer learning baseline (same backbone, training 8 epochs at test time) is 0.30, 0.44, 0.56, and its FAR when $\gamma$ is 0 is as high as 0.79, 0.70, 0.60, corresponding to this paper's 0.35, 0.39, 0.55. End-to-end methods with full data reach 0.95, same-domain triplet with kNN 0.98, triplet with SVM 0.93 (FAR 0.35), but these numbers are built on same-domain and full-data conditions, qualitatively different from this paper's cross-domain ten-sample setting, which the paper explicitly distinguishes for comparability.

### Findings from Ablation Studies

**Sample Size Ablation** (Table 2): Monotonic improvement from one to ten samples (ResNet15 at FAR 5%: 0.55, 0.74, 0.78, 0.80), with the largest gain between one and three samples (about 19 percentage points), and diminishing marginal returns of 2 percentage points between five and ten samples—the sweet spot between registration cost and accuracy is between three and five samples.

**Softmax Ablation** (Table 2): Adding softmax is systematically worse than pure L2 distance; the smaller the sample size and the tighter the FAR, the larger the gap (e.g., TCResNet8 single sample 0.33 vs 0.38). The reason, as stated above: softmax destroys the absolute magnitude semantics of distances, while open-set rejection relies precisely on absolute magnitude.

**Threshold Regime Ablation** (Fig. 3, fixed $\gamma = m = 0.5$): Under fixed threshold, the capacity trend reverses—smaller capacity models actually have higher accuracy (DS-CNN-L reaches 58%, but FAR is 0.6%), while ResNet15 is only 54% but FAR is as low as 0.1%–0.2%. Converted to hourly terms, DS-CNN-L's false triggers are 3 times those of ResNet15. The paper's explanation: Large models exchange "relaxed intra-class compactness" for "stronger inter-class distance," causing embeddings to spread out more, making positive samples more likely to fall out of the $\gamma$ sphere and be judged as unknown under an absolute threshold. This reveals an important fact: the optimal backbone under posterior threshold tuning (ResNet15) and the optimal backbone under calibration-free deployment are not necessarily the same; threshold strategy and backbone selection are coupled decisions.

**Class-Level Structure Ablation** (Fig. 4, ResNet15 t-SNE and confusion matrix, $\gamma = m$): stop (79.3%), yes (78.5%), no (74.8%) have the highest accuracy due to compact class clusters not overlapping with neighbors; up (44.5%), off (47.0%), on (33.8%), left (49.3%) are below 50% due to dispersed representations. The confusion matrix shows that errors are predominantly "judged as unknown" (e.g., on row: 261 judged unknown vs 134 correct; left row: 209 vs 203; up row: 224 vs 189), with very few severe cross-word errors (max 14 for down to no, 5 for down to go, 12 for up to off). That is, the system's failure mode is conservative miss-detection rather than misclassification—this is a failure in the safe direction for wake-word applications. Author's interpretation: Short functional words like on, off, up are single-syllable words with weak acoustic energy and high speaker variability, making it naturally difficult for embeddings to cluster, whereas stop, yes, no contain significant fricative and plosive structures and longer temporal support.

**Quantization and Normalization Ablation** (Table 3 and text): 8-bit post-training quantization is lossless for all backbones (calibrated with four random samples). ResNet15's BatchNorm quantized separately caused initial accuracy drops, fully recovered after changing to FP16—this exposes toolchain folding limitations and provides a universal fix recipe. DS-CNN's LayerNorm embedding head accounts for 44% of its total load, explicitly marked by the paper as further optimization space (mean and variance calculations could be implemented more efficiently).

**Classifier Paradigm Ablation** (Table 4): Under the same backbone and data, prototypical classification compared to retraining linear classifier head at test time has similar accuracy when $\gamma$ is 0, but the advantage widens to 24 percentage points under FAR constraint (0.80 vs 0.56), proving that the source of the gap is not representation quality but the open-set rejection mechanism.

## Main Contributions

1. **The first (as claimed by the paper) calibration-free on-device KWS customization solution in the TinyML domain**: Cross-domain ten samples reach 80% accuracy (FAR 5%), with 9.7 ms inference time and 246.9 $\mu$J energy consumption per inference on a 25 mW class MCU, fully fitting "users recording a few sentences to customize commands" into the memory and energy budgets of microcontrollers.
2. **A complete design methodology**: Frozen triplet feature extractor plus on-device prototype registration plus calibration-free open-set threshold $\gamma = m$, and providing a complete account of accuracy, latency, memory, and energy across five backbones.
3. **Multiple transferable engineering insights**: Softmax harms open-set performance; backbone capacity trend reverses under fixed threshold, coupling threshold strategy and backbone selection; layer type execution efficiency (3x3 convolution 100 MAC/cyc vs pointwise 40 MAC/cyc) and hidden overhead of normalization operators on cluster cores (44% of DS-CNN-L) determine the real budget, requiring hardware-aware backbone selection; deployment recipe for fixing accuracy loss by changing BatchNorm quantization to FP16.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

- **Absolute accuracy gap**: 0.80 vs 0.95 for end-to-end, 0.98 for same-domain kNN (Table 4), a gap of 15 to 18 percentage points; under calibration-free fixed threshold, it drops further to only 54% (Fig. 3). The triple cost of cross-domain, few-shot, and training-free is ultimately reflected in this gap.
- **Threshold dilemma**: $\gamma = m$ is calibration-free but conservative; posterior threshold tuning performs better but requires annotated negative samples from the target domain—the two cannot be had simultaneously; the paper does not provide an intermediate path.
- **Prototype fragility**: Single sample is only 0.55; registration recording quality directly determines prototype quality (the paper reports statistics by averaging over ten repetitions); there is no adaptation to the actual user's voice during runtime.
- **Systematic degradation of short functional words**: on 33.8%, up 44.5% (Fig. 4); a single prototype cannot cover dispersed class clusters, yet these short words are precisely high-frequency words in voice commands.
- **Bill for the largest model**: 537.3k max activation (RAM pressure), 235.1 MMAC, 246.9 $\mu$J (8.7 times that of TCResNet8); the optimal solution for accuracy and the optimal solution for resources are separated.
- **Idealized assumptions**: The unknown prototype assumes $K$ random words do not contain target keywords; silence and unknown prototypes each have only one vector, unable to express the diversity of the negative space.
- **Language generalization unverified**: Although MSWC itself is multilingual, experiments were only completed on English; multilingual and cross-lingual transfer are not reported.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

- GSC as the target domain is clean, near-field, balanced 1-second isolated words, lacking far-field, reverberation, additive noise, continuous speech flow, and word overlap scenarios; FAR is only measured on 20 word classes, with limited coverage.
- The word list size is fixed at ten (or 4+2 during deployment); the scaling laws of ACC and FAR as the number of classes $N$ increases are not reported.
- The transfer learning baseline is only reproduced on DS-CNN-L with 8 epochs, and no control for ProtoNet-style cross-entropy episode training under the same setting is run; the cited end-to-end and kNN/SVM numbers come from their respective same-domain full-data protocols, which are qualitatively different strictly speaking.
- Energy consumption is only converted based on a 25.5 mW envelope at a single operating point of 240 MHz, 0.65 V, without DVFS or system-level battery life data; firmware memory (Flash/RAM bytes) is not reported itemized, only two proxies are given: parameter count and max activation.
- Total training cost (time, server energy) is not reported; sensitivity of source domain selection (why take top-500 classes) is not ablated.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

- **Post-registration adaptation** (the next step mentioned by the paper): Bridging the accuracy gap with end-to-end methods. Feasible paths include on-device prototype iterative refinement, embedding space calibration using only forward passes, and self-labeling using unlabeled streaming audio; the key is that any method must remain within the constraint of free or near-free backpropagation.
- **Better calibration-free thresholds**: Estimating the acceptance radius for each class from the intra-class distance distribution of registration samples (instead of globally sharing $m$), or building an extreme value model for negative sample distances, could preserve "no need for annotated negative samples" while tightening conservatism.
- **Co-design of backbone and hardware**: Replacing the cluster-core LayerNorm head that consumes 44% of the load (using foldable normalization or accelerator support); tilting towards structures friendly to 3x3 convolutions to fully exploit the peak efficiency of 100 MAC/cyc.
- **Representation-level enhancements**: Multi-prototypes per class (clustering registered embeddings) to cover dispersed class clusters like on, up, off; attention-weighted prototypes; speaker-conditioned embedding normalization; data augmentation for far-field.
- **Negative space modeling**: Replacing the single unknown prototype with learned outlier classes or density models to improve rejection discrimination.
- **Moving towards streaming detection**: Advancing from isolated 1-second windows to sliding-window streaming wake-word detection, handling window handover and decision smoothing—this is a necessary path for productization, which this paper does not touch.
