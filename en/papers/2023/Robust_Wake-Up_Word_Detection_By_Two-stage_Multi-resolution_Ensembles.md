## Problem Statement

### Problem Background and Domain Pain Points

Speech has become the mainstream hands-free interaction entry point for a large class of devices such as smart speakers, mobile phones, TVs, and in-car systems. The first gate for this interaction is the wake-up word mechanism: the device listens to the audio stream around the clock, and only after recognizing a specific trigger word (in this paper's scenario, the Spanish trigger word "Ok Aura," corresponding to Telefónica's own voice assistant product line) does it pass subsequent speech to the upper-level understanding module. This "always-on" attribute imposes three rigid constraints on wake-up word detectors in production environments, which are explicitly identified as key factors in the paper's introduction: robustness, energy-efficient operation, and minimizing communication delays. These three are particularly difficult to satisfy simultaneously on resource-constrained devices.

Behind these three constraints lie three completely different types of engineering pressures. The robustness pressure comes from the acoustic environment: detectors typically face far-field pickup, background noise, and reverberation. Under degraded conditions, if either recall or precision collapses, the user-side performance is either "having to shout three times to wake it up" or "lighting up by itself in the middle of the night." The energy efficiency pressure comes from persistent listening: the detector performs continuous sliding-window inference on the device, and every multiply-accumulate operation directly translates to standby power consumption. If the model is slightly larger, battery life and heat dissipation cannot withstand it. The latency pressure comes from interaction design: from the user finishing the wake-up word to the device providing a perceptible response, local detection, uplink transmission, and cloud verification each consume part of the dialogue response budget. If it drags on, users will assume the device didn't hear them and repeat the wake-up, directly ruining the interaction experience.

These three pressures squeeze each other in the classic "single-device, single-model" deployment: a larger model is accurate but power-hungry, while a smaller model saves power but collapses under noise; tightening the operating point reduces false wake-ups but increases missed wake-ups, while loosening it has the opposite effect. Turning any knob hurts the other end. The starting point of this paper is precisely the acknowledgment that this squeezing cannot be resolved simultaneously on a single model. Therefore, it splits the system into two stages, allowing different components at different positions to bear different constraints respectively—this is the root of all technical choices in the paper.

### Specific Shortcomings of Existing Methods

The paper's review of existing routes can be summarized into four points, each with a specific gap identified.

First, acoustic-level robustness methods: speech enhancement and denoising [1,2], context adaptation [3], and multi-channel methods utilizing spatial redundancy [4]. These methods improve input quality at the signal or feature level, but the cost is elsewhere or local—the enhancement module itself consumes on-device computing budget, multi-channel methods depend on microphone array hardware, and they only solve "listening clearer," completely failing to address the "single operating point" system structure problem.

Second, two-pass detection: deploying a verification network on the server side [5,6], or even directly using an ASR system for keyword spotting [7]. This route already acknowledges the upper limit of single-model capabilities, handing over refinement to a larger, more complex network in the cloud. However, it has a structural defect pointed out directly by the paper: raw audio must be uplinked to the cloud, bringing privacy concerns [8]—everything in the user's room must pass through the cloud first. In the context of large-scale deployment of smart speakers and increasingly strict privacy legislation, this is a product-level risk, not just an engineering flaw.

Third, architectural optimization of the detection network itself: fully connected networks, CNNs, RNNs, CRNNs, TDNNs, seq2seq, attention mechanisms [9]. This route continuously raises the upper limit of single-model accuracy, but it still operates on the same operating point: the on-device model is forced to simultaneously bear multiple conflicting goals of "not missing real users" and "few false triggers and low power consumption." No matter how good the architecture is, it only pushes the Pareto frontier outward slightly, without changing the constraint structure.

Fourth, training-side methods: data augmentation [10] and new loss functions [11], used to handle unseen background conditions. The paper acknowledges their value, but there is a pitfall in the dataset level that has not been fully discussed: training labels are usually only at the utterance level. In a positive sample audio, the trigger word occupies only a small segment, while the rest of the window consists of pre-silence, breathing, and non-keyword speech. The discriminative boundaries learned by the model on these "dirty boundaries" are systematically biased. This shortcoming is not merely pointed out by the paper; the authors conducted alignment ablation experiments to supplement the evidence (see Fig 2(a)).

Additionally, there is a shortcoming not explicitly criticized in the paper but implicit in the methodology: feature extraction parameters (number of MFCC coefficients, window length, frame shift) usually share a single configuration on both the device and the cloud. This forces the device to digest fine time resolutions prepared for cloud accuracy, causing inference time to rise linearly with information volume—Table 2 calculates this cost clearly: with the same 13 coefficients, on-device inference is 25.08ms with a 50ms frame shift, but rises to 51.62–82.75ms with 20ms/10ms shifts. The F1 improvement gained from three times the latency is negligible. On-device latency and energy efficiency are sacrificed in vain by a one-size-fits-all feature configuration.

### Key Challenges This Paper Aims to Solve

In summary, this paper aims to solve four problems simultaneously. The first is the system structure problem: within a "lightweight persistent on-device detection + strong cloud verification" two-stage framework, let the two operating points relax respectively—the on-device side focuses on recall and power saving, while the cloud side is responsible for suppressing false positives, avoiding the on-device side being forced to use overly tight thresholds that ignore genuine user requests. The second is the privacy problem: completing cloud verification without transmitting raw audio, and ensuring the uplinked data itself resists eavesdropping and man-in-the-middle attacks. The third is the data quality problem: replacing expensive per-sample manual annotation with automatic time alignment, tightening the time boundaries of positive samples, and quantifying the bias introduced by automatic alignment. The fourth is the selection problem: matching a set of feature parameter configurations for the device and cloud respectively, and selecting on-device candidates and cloud ensemble members among thirteen heterogeneous architectures based on the metric of "performance multiplied by inference time," ultimately ensuring the ensemble outperforms the strongest single model at every noise level. These four problems together form a complete engineering closed loop, which is what distinguishes it from pure architecture papers.

## Methodology

### Overall Architecture Design and Design Motivation

The overall solution (Fig 1) is a two-stage pipeline. The first stage runs persistently on constrained devices: a lightweight classifier (referred to as device-sgru in the following text) processes the audio stream in real-time using a sliding window, outputting local log-odds scores; the audio event manager sends "local log-odds + audio features" via a secure REST API channel to the cloud once the score exceeds the on-device operating point. The second stage runs in parallel with user utterance processing on the cloud: the verification model is a heterogeneous classifier ensemble, performing refinement decisions while the user utterance is being processed normally; if it determines the on-device trigger is a false positive, the entire user interaction can be discarded.

Every layer of this architecture deserves to be asked "why."

First, decoupling the two operating points is the most important system argument in the paper. In single-model deployment, the detection threshold is a global knob; tightening or loosening it hurts one side or the other. After two-stage decoupling, the on-device operating point can be set relatively wide (tolerating a higher proportion of false positive triggers), because each trigger only causes one feature uplink, rather than waking up the entire downstream chain; the true refinement decision is handed over to cloud computing power. The paper's original words are that this approach "avoids overly tight on-device configurations that might ignore valid user requests"—in other words, the on-device's responsibility is downgraded from "making the correct decision" to "not missing real users," significantly reducing the difficulty of decision-making. This directly explains why an sgru with only 145.6k parameters can胜任 the on-device role.

Second, parallel cloud verification and user utterance processing is the key design that allows the solution to兼顾 latency. The cloud's approximately 280ms inference is not serially blocking the interaction path, but occurs simultaneously with subsequent command processing; it only interrupts the interaction in reverse if it determines a false positive. Thus, the cost of "cloud slowness" is limited to false positive scenarios, rather than being spread across every normal interaction, and the perceived dialogue latency is determined by the on-device's approximately 25ms.

Third, the uplink channel transmits features, not audio, and the features are calculated using a different parameter configuration than the on-device detection. The paper further proposes that feature calculation details can be viewed as a secret key, and feature data can be further obfuscated, making the system more robust against man-in-the-middle attacks and eavesdropping. Note that the privacy logic here is layered: not transmitting raw audio is the first layer, directly shrinking the exposure surface; keeping feature parameter configurations secret is the second layer, making it difficult for interceptors to reconstruct audible speech even if they obtain the features—because without knowing the window length, frame shift, and coefficient configuration, the inverse mapping from features to audio is not unique.

### Mathematical Principles of Core Algorithms

The basic unit of training and inference is a 1.5-second fixed-length audio window, chosen based on the duration distribution covering the majority of audio in the library; each window outputs the probability of "containing the trigger word." During training, all audio is mixed with background noise, with SNR uniformly sampled in the [-10, 50] dB range. This design ensures the model has gradient signals from extremely low SNR to high-fidelity full spectrum, and directly defines the organization of all subsequent evaluations—all comparisons are reported by SNR bin, aligning the training distribution with the evaluation granularity.

Ensemble fusion adopts stacking rather than voting or averaging. Each member classifier outputs two paths: positive class output (WuW) and negative class output (any other sound), transformed into probabilities to calculate log-odds:

$$s_i = \log \frac{p_{i,\text{pos}}}{p_{i,\text{neg}}}$$

Choosing log-odds instead of raw probabilities for fusion has an engineering meaning: it pulls each member's confidence to a symmetric unbounded space. When probabilities are close to 0 or 1, the dynamic range collapses, making it difficult to linearly weight scores between multiple models. Log-odds turn multiplicative differences into additive differences, making it easier for the subsequent meta-learner to learn boundaries. N paths of log-odds are then fed into a deliberately simplified MLP meta-learner: one fully connected layer, one ReLU, and another fully connected layer with two outputs. The fusioner is not trained on the full dataset, but specifically on 153 reserved samples of "trigger words followed by genuine user utterances" (e.g., "Okey Aura, quiero una película estilo Star Wars" such connected speech samples). This data split has a clear motivation: the fusioner needs to learn how to weigh member scores in real interaction contexts (wake-up word plus command spoken continuously, acoustically dirtier), rather than overfitting to artificially high consistency on isolated, clean trigger word segments.

The training protocol itself is朴素: trained from scratch, uniformly initialized weights, up to 700 epochs, cross-entropy loss, batch size 128, Adam initial learning rate 0.001, one-order-of-magnitude reduction in learning rate upon plateau, and early stopping if learning rate is reduced four times consecutively with no improvement. The intention behind no fancy tricks is clear—to make the thirteen architectures comparable under equal training budgets, excluding the interference of tuning luck.

At the feature level, MFCC is chosen over Mel-spectrograms, with the reason stated plainly by the paper: to suppress energy information in the signal as much as possible. The energy dimension is sensitive to volume, playback level, and channel gain. Retaining it introduces non-robust factors (huge feature differences for the same sentence spoken loudly vs. softly) and adds another dimension of audible information available for speech reconstruction in the uplinked features, which contradicts privacy goals—suppressing energy simultaneously serves both robustness and privacy goals, a rare "kill two birds with one stone" design in the paper. Audio normalization is performed accordingly, and the 0-th order coefficient is replaced with log-energy. Feature extraction uses Sonopy, a lightweight implementation replicated by the authors in PyTorch (sonopytorch), open-sourced with the paper, ensuring on-device feature calculation itself does not become a bottleneck.

### Key Technical Innovation 1: Automatically Enhancing Training Data using CTC Pseudo-Forced Alignment

Database quality enhancement relies on time annotation, using a method from the authors' previous work: iterative pseudo-forced alignment by acoustic CTC loss, originally for semi-supervised domain adaptation in self-supervised ASR scenarios [15], here migrated to the wake-up word library: all positive samples in the full library are automatically aligned to obtain precise time boundaries for the trigger word, with tool code open-sourced with the paper.

Why is time alignment worth listing as a separate contribution? Because in positive samples with utterance-level labels, the trigger word often occupies only a small segment of the 1.5-second window, with the rest being silence and non-keyword speech. The discriminative surfaces learned by the model under the two types of labels are completely different: the former requires energy and phonetic content to hit the trigger word interval, while the latter only requires "the trigger word appeared in the window," absorbing a large amount of non-discriminative audio into the positive class. Aligning tightens the time boundaries of positive samples, effectively increasing label SNR for free.

Particularly noteworthy is how they control the quality of automatic alignment: the test set was manually time-annotated, used to measure the time bias introduced by the alignment tool, and then used to correct the automatic annotations of the training and development sets. This forms a quality closed loop of "small-scale manual investment anchoring large-scale automatic annotation," much more rigorous than the common practice of simply trusting automatic annotations. The ablation part (Fig 2(a)) shows the return: compared to using only VAD time references, aligned data improves the F1 of sgru at every SNR bin, and all subsequent experiments use only aligned data.

### Key Technical Innovation 2: Multi-resolution Feature Scheme with Device-Cloud Dual Configuration

The paper systematically scanned the feature parameter space: window lengths of 100ms, 30ms, 20ms; frame shifts of 50ms, 20ms, 10ms; and two levels of MFCC coefficients, 13 and 40. These were trained on sgru and evaluated by SNR bin (Fig 2(b)), while also providing the feature size and on-device inference time on Pixel XL for each configuration (Table 2).

The on-device selection is 13 coefficients, 100ms window, 50ms frame shift: a 1.5-second window produces only a (29, 13) feature matrix, with on-device inference taking approximately 25.08ms. The logic for choosing it is not "best performance" but "best cost-performance ratio"—Fig 2(b) shows that reducing time resolution only brings a slight F1 drop, but Table 2 shows inference time dropping from the 73–84ms range to the 25ms range. The saved computing power is exactly the energy efficiency budget for persistent listening. The cloud selection is the reverse: 40 coefficients, 30ms window, 10ms frame shift, i.e., maximizing both information volume and time resolution ((148, 40) feature matrix), because cloud verification is not on the critical interaction path, and 83.97ms inference is fully acceptable.

The essence of the name "multi-resolution" lies here: multi-resolution observations of the same word. The on-device side uses coarse resolution for fast screening, while the cloud side uses fine resolution for refinement confirmation. Both sets of features are calculated simultaneously by the device—feature extraction itself takes only about 13ms, followed by uplink. There is also a hidden correctness argument here: different feature parameter configurations for device and cloud mean that the error sources of the two stages are partially decoupled. The input seen by the cloud ensemble is not completely correlated with the on-device trigger basis, making the ensemble decision more independent and having more complementary value.

### Key Technical Innovation 3: Horizontal Evaluation of Thirteen Heterogeneous Architectures Plus Stacking Ensemble

The model pool covers the main architecture families of on-device speech classification at the time (Table 1 gives parameter count, operations, and size): CNN series (cnn-fat2019: 5.2M parameters, 1196.8M operations, 43.6MB; cnn-trad-pool2: 69.5k, 10.1M, 0.9MB); GRU series divided into three types based on time-dimension post-processing (sgru taking the final state memory, gru-max taking the max value along time, gru-att using Performer to process hidden states); ResNet series with dilated convolutions (resnet15: 237.4k, 456.6M, 10.6MB; resnet15-narrow: 42.4k, 81.6M, 4.2MB; resnet8: 109.8k, 16.8M, 1.5MB); Lambda layer lambda-resnet18 (89k, 3.3M, 1.1MB); broadcast residual learning bc-resnet-1 (9.4k, 4.0M, 5.7MB); Conformer (10.4M, 107.4M, 71.5MB, depth 12, 8 heads with 64 dimensions each, kernel size 31); and Audiomer series (audiomer-l processing raw audio: 674.2k, 7.7M, 18.4MB, where the authors improved performance by replacing the attention kernel with ReLU; and audiomer-2d newly proposed to process MFCC: 1.9M, 3.3M, 10.9MB, consisting of a coefficient-reducing convolution block plus four Performer layers with convolutional residual connections, where query and context are generated by convolutional layers).

The reason sgru won the on-device model selection is directly supported by Table 1: its operation count (144.4k multiply-accumulates) and size (0.81MB) are the lowest in the field. An interesting comparison is that gru-max and sgru have exactly the same parameter count (145.6k), but the operation count differs by two orders of magnitude (21.5M vs. 144.4k)—the difference lies in post-processing: gru-max needs to pool all time-step hidden states and feed them into the classifier, while sgru only consumes the final state. This precisely illustrates that "same parameter count, vastly different deployment costs," meaning on-device selection must look at operation count, not parameter count. Additionally, bc-resnet-1 has only 9.4k parameters but occupies 5.7MB, and audiomer-l has 674.2k parameters occupying 18.4MB. The paper does not report the reasons for the disproportionate size-to-parameter ratio in Table 1 (presumably including non-parameter buffers or storage format overhead).

Regarding ensemble methods, the paper chose stacking among four common combination methods: voting, Bayesian Model Averaging, bagging, and stacking. The motivation is that heterogeneous architectures have different error patterns—CNNs lean towards local textures, GRUs towards temporal dynamics, and ResNets towards multi-scale receptive fields. Non-linear fusion at the log-odds level can theoretically learn conditional weights for "which member to trust for what input," whereas voting and averaging cannot do this.

### Key Technical Innovation 4: Feature-Level Privacy Uplink Channel

Synthesizing the previous three innovations, the privacy mechanism can be fully described: after triggering, the device does not send raw audio, but only features calculated according to cloud-specific parameters (40 coefficients, 30/10ms) plus on-device log-odds; feature calculation details act as a secret key, and features can be further obfuscated. Compared to existing schemes where "two-stage verification must transmit raw audio" [5,6,7], this channel changes privacy from "trusting the cloud vendor" to "attackers lacking the configuration required for inverse mapping," shifting the threat model from honest-but-curious servers to external eavesdropping and man-in-the-middle attacks. It needs to be emphasized that this is a design claim; the paper does not provide any attack experiments to quantify its resistance to inverse transformation.

### Technical Differences with Existing Methods

Compared to the two-stage verification route [5,6], the uplinked data in this paper changes from raw audio to parameter-secret features, resulting in a different privacy structure; compared to the ASR scheme [7], this paper does not require a complete recognition decoder, keeping cloud costs controllable and the latency path shorter. Compared to the single-model optimization route [9], this paper acknowledges the upper limit of on-device model capabilities, trading robustness through system structure rather than architectural innovation. Compared to general ensemble works (voting [4], Bayesian Model Averaging [21], bagging [22], stacking [23]), the ensemble members in this paper are deliberately heterogeneous representatives of thirteen architecture families, the fusion features are log-odds, and the fusioner is trained on a dedicated "trigger word plus command" reserved set, closely matching the real interaction distribution. Compared to the authors' previous wake-up word work [1,2] (TASE and other speech enhancement routes), this paper shifts from "cleaning the input" to "splitting the decision chain," with consistent test splits for vertical comparability. Finally, the multi-resolution dual operating point is unique to this paper: on-device coarse resolution preserves latency, cloud fine resolution preserves accuracy, with decoupled feature configurations on both sides, which is a clear structural difference from the conventional practice of one-size-fits-all feature parameters.

## Experimental Results

### Datasets Used and Their Scale

The core is the private Spanish "Ok Aura" library, retaining the original test split from previous works [1,2] to ensure vertical comparability. The training and validation splits are thickened with external data: M-AILABS Spanish speech library [12], SLR28 real room impulse responses [13], Valentini-Botinhao noise library [14]. Additionally, 413 new Spanish speech samples were recorded, containing the target trigger word, spontaneous speech, and read speech; among them, 153 samples of "trigger word followed by user utterance" were separately cut out, specifically used to train and validate the score fusion ensemble.

The total library volume is 70 hours, approximately 55k samples, composed of: target keywords approximately 4k, other speech events approximately 26k, noise approximately 24k, room impulse responses approximately 1k. All positive samples are automatically aligned, with the test set additionally manually annotated to quantify alignment bias. The method to simulate real conditions is mixing with background noise, with SNR uniformly sampled in [-10, 50] dB.

### Definition and Rationale of Evaluation Metrics

The main metric is the WuW F1 score by SNR bin (Fig 2, Fig 3), with SNR bins cut from [-10, -5) all the way to [40, 45]. The reason for choosing F1 instead of a single precision or recall is the symmetry of retrieval tasks: false wake-ups (hurting precision) and missed wake-ups (hurting recall) are both hard flaws in products; F1 compresses both into one number; presenting it by SNR bin makes robustness (rather than accuracy at a single average point) visible. There are three levels of efficiency metrics: parameter count, operation count (multiply-accumulate times), and model size in Table 1, used for architecture-level screening; Real Time Factor in Fig 3(a), measured on a real Pixel XL device using PyTorch's official mobile performance recipe, used for deployment feasibility judgment; feature size and on-device inference milliseconds in Table 2, used for feature configuration selection. This combination of metrics forces "accuracy" and "speed" to be weighed in the same table, consistent with the paper's production orientation. The paper does not report false alarm rate per hour, AUC, or DET curves, nor does it report end-to-end latency distribution.

### Detailed Comparison with Baseline Methods and SOTA

The thirteen classifiers were horizontally evaluated under unified features (40 coefficients, 30ms window, 10ms frame shift), with the exception of audiomer-l processing raw audio and device-sgru using on-device coarse resolution (Fig 3(a), Fig 3(b)).

At the single-model level, the strongest is cnn-fat2019: F1 reaches 0.978 across all SNR bins [-10, 50] dB (Table 3), but the cost is 5.2M parameters, 1196.8M operations, and 43.6MB size (Table 1), nearly four orders of magnitude higher in operations than sgru, making persistent on-device deployment completely unfeasible. The second largest in parameter count, conformer (10.4M parameters, 71.5MB), does not dominate in the bin-by-bin comparison in Fig 3(b), indicating that large parameter counts do not necessarily translate to robustness in wake-up word tasks with short words and small vocabularies. From the on-device perspective, device-sgru is confirmed as the best compromise: it sacrifices some peak F1 in exchange for approximately 25ms inference and 0.81MB size. Fig 3(b) also presents a phenomenon very instructive for deployment: the gap between models is concentrated in the noisiest SNR bins, with smaller gaps as noise decreases—meaning the practical significance of architecture selection is mainly in the low SNR region; in quiet environments, everyone is accurate.

At the ensemble level, ensemble-3 (on-device device-sgru plus cloud cnn-fat2019, resnet15-narrow, bc-resnet-1) achieves the highest F1 of 0.981 in the field, exceeding the strongest single model's 0.978; more importantly, Fig 3(c) shows it outperforms the baseline single model at every SNR bin—"better at every bin" is much harder to achieve than a higher overall mean, indicating the gain comes from systematic complementarity rather than luck at individual bins.

The complete account of latency: local detection takes approximately 25ms, imperceptible to user interaction; on-device feature extraction before cloud feature uplink takes approximately 13ms; cloud ensemble inference takes approximately 280ms (excluding data transmission time), and occurs in parallel with user utterance processing, not blocking dialogue. It needs to be noted that all comparisons in the paper are conducted within the private Ok Aura library, with no benchmarking against external SOTA results on public benchmarks (such as Google Speech Commands).

### Findings from Ablation Experiments

The paper's three groups of ablations each answer a "why."

First group: The value of time alignment (Fig 2(a)). sgru was trained separately with VAD time references and CTC aligned annotations; the aligned version had higher F1 at all SNR bins. This verifies the hypothesis that "dirty label time boundaries are a real bottleneck," and also shows that automatic alignment is a zero-inference-cost data-side gain—once offline investment in exchange for robustness throughout the model's lifecycle.

Second group: The impact of feature parameters (Fig 2(b) plus Table 2). As time resolution goes from coarse to fine, F1 only drops slightly—in other words, the information lost by coarse resolution contributes very little to discrimination; but inference time rises linearly with the number of frames ((29, 13) configuration 25.08ms, (71, 13) configuration 51.62ms, (148, 13) configuration 73.43ms, (149, 13) configuration 82.75ms, (148, 40) configuration 83.97ms, all measured on Pixel XL). This data is the direct basis for the device-cloud dual configuration decision: the on-device side eats performance loss for three times the speedup, while the cloud side eats computing cost for maximum information volume.

Third group: The combination effect of ensemble members (Table 3). The curves in this group are not monotonic, and the information content is the highest. ensemble-1 (cnn-fat2019 plus device-sgru) has an F1 of only 0.972, lower than cnn-fat2019 single model's 0.978—fusion of the two members actually dragged down the strongest one, indicating that when there are too few members and insufficient complementarity, the fusioner introduces noise. ensemble-2 rises to 0.977 after adding resnet15-narrow, still slightly inferior to the single model. ensemble-3 jumps to 0.981 after adding bc-resnet-1, stably exceeding the baseline for the first time, and winning every bin (Fig 3(c)). ensemble-4 collapses to 0.958 after further adding lambda-resnet18, the worst ensemble in the field. The paper does not report an analysis of the cause of this degradation, but a reasonable speculation is that the error correlation between members is unbalanced or the fusioner overfits on the limited reserved samples—regardless, the translatable lesson from this ablation is: stacking ensembles have a sweet spot for the number of members; blindly stacking members may harm the overall performance, and architectural heterogeneity between members (CNN, ResNet, broadcast residual, on-device GRU each occupying a corner) is more important than the number of members.

## Main Contributions

The paper lists four contributions, with supporting evidence corresponding to each as follows. First, an automatic mechanism for database alignment: CTC pseudo-forced alignment plus test set manual anchoring, open-source tool, Fig 2(a) proves bin-by-bin full improvement. Second, parameter optimization of feature extraction: dual configuration selection of on-device 13 coefficients 100/50ms and cloud 40 coefficients 30/10ms, Table 2 and Fig 2(b) provide a complete cost-performance argument. Third, a systematic horizontal evaluation of thirteen heterogeneous architectures on two dimensions: performance and RTF: Table 1 plus Fig 3(a)(b), itself a selection reference table with reuse value. Fourth, a two-stage multi-resolution detection scheme: on-device approximately 25ms lightweight detection plus cloud stacking ensemble verification, feature-level privacy uplink, ensemble-3 exceeds the strongest single model at every SNR bin (Table 3, Fig 3(c)). At a more abstract level, the true contribution of this paper is reframing the wake-up word problem from a "single-model accuracy race" to a "operating point allocation problem": recall goes to the on-device side, precision goes to the cloud, energy efficiency goes to coarse resolution, robustness goes to heterogeneous ensembles, and privacy goes to feature uplink. Each constraint has a clear bearer.

## Limitations and Future Work

### Technical Limitations of the Method

First, the privacy claim lacks support from attack experiments. The design logic of feature uplink plus parameter secrecy is theoretically sound, but the paper does not report any quantitative evaluation of inverse transformation or reconstruction attacks. MFCC-like features are not generally recognized as irreversible in the literature; although energy information is deliberately suppressed, whether residual structures such as formant trajectories can reconstruct intelligible speech remains an open question; "features can be further obfuscated" also remains at the proposal level, with no obfuscation scheme provided. Second, cloud ensemble inference takes approximately 280ms and does not include transmission time; verification latency will lengthen in weak network environments, and false positive interactions occupy device and downstream module resources before verification returns. The paper does not report end-to-end worst-case latency. Third, the fusioner has only one MLP layer and is trained and validated on only 153 reserved samples. The sample size is small for estimating weights for multiple members; the performance collapse of ensemble-4 (0.958) is likely related to this, but the paper does not attribute it. Fourth, the scheme relies on a complete device-cloud collaborative chain (REST API, dual feature calculation, event management), lacking discussion on graceful degradation paths when offline or the cloud is unavailable.

### Shortcomings in Experimental Design

First, all conclusions are built on the private Spanish "Ok Aura" library. With a single language and a single wake-up word, and no benchmarking against external SOTA on any public benchmark, the external validity of the results is questionable. Second, evaluation only uses bin-by-bin F1 by SNR, lacking the product-side most critical metric of false alarms per unit time (e.g., number of false triggers per hour). The core selling point of the two-stage scheme is precisely shifting the false alarm pressure to the cloud—without this metric, the actual benefit of loosening the operating point cannot be quantified. Third, RTF and inference time are only measured on one device, the Pixel XL; conclusions for low-end devices rely on extrapolation. Fourth, the degradation of ensemble-4 is not analyzed, and the basis for the selection process of ensemble members (why lambda-resnet18 was selected as a cloud candidate, why the audiomer series and conformer were excluded from the ensemble) is not reported. Fifth, after mixing M-AILABS and other external corpora into the training set, the paper does not report the overlap between speakers and the test set. Sixth, the computing cost corresponding to 280ms cloud inference and the resource conflict with parallel utterance processing are not quantified.

### Possible Directions for Future Improvement

Following the paper's own framework, there are at least five directions for extension. First, turn privacy from a design claim into provably secure: perform anti-reconstruction evaluation on uplinked features, introduce revocable obfuscation or differential privacy mechanisms, and quantify the privacy-F1 trade-off curve. Second, use knowledge distillation to feed back the robustness of the cloud ensemble to the on-device model, allowing the on-device single model to approach ensemble levels even in offline or degraded modes, while maintaining the 25ms inference budget. Third, online fusioner: continuously adaptively adjust member weights using deployment traffic, replacing the static MLP trained once on 153 samples, alleviating small-sample overfitting. Fourth, extend to multi-language, multi-wake-up word, and custom wake-up word scenarios to test the universality of the feature dual configuration and heterogeneous ensemble conclusions. Fifth, supplement product-level evaluation metrics: false alarms per hour, end-to-end latency distribution, RTF on multiple tiers of real devices, and the complete interception rate of false positives under different network conditions, making the core benefits of the two-stage scheme truly measurable.
